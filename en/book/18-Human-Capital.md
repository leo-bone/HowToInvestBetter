# XVIII. Human Capital

Human capital is a classic concept proposed by Schultz and Becker: the knowledge, skills, health, and experience inside you that can create future income is itself an asset. For ordinary people, it accounts for nearly 100% of the household balance sheet in youth—far more valuable than that bit of savings and fund holdings. Regrettably, most people narrow "investing" down to buying stocks and funds, and neglect the management of this, their largest asset. The Central University of Finance and Economics' "China Human Capital Report 2025" estimates that China's total human capital reached 4,375.63 trillion yuan in 2023, or 4.0222 million yuan per capita; the World Bank's Human Capital Index (HCI) for China was 0.7 in 2020. These numbers remind us: how much "you are worth" is the starting point of financial planning.

The main logic of this chapter has only one thread: your primary-job income is the only source of all your investment principal. Every yuan invested in human capital (education, skills, health, salary negotiation, track choice) often has a higher return than the funds you repeatedly study; while career interruption, obsolete skills, and health collapse are "asset impairments" of very high certainty. Run "yourself" like a small company—first grow the human-capital asset, then talk about allocating financial capital; the order cannot be reversed—otherwise you're catching water with a leaking bucket.

The 12 entries below cover the cognitive framework, primary-job principal, deep cultivation and job-hopping, career interruption, continuous learning, the health foundation, and pitfall avoidance, all based on verifiable empirical research and Chinese local data. When reading, keep the "personal balance sheet" perspective: your salary is revenue, skills and health are assets, loans are liabilities, and social security is implicit shareholder capital.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses): cognitive framework—treat "yourself" as a core asset to be managed (Entry 1, Entry 5); primary-job income as the first pot of gold—salary negotiation and implicit compensation (Entry 2, Entry 12); primary-job deepening, industry choice, and job-hopping rhythm (Entry 3, Entry 6, Entry 7, Entry 11); the real cost of career interruption (Entry 4); skill half-life and continuous learning (Entry 8); health: depreciation and insurance of human capital (Entry 9); pitfall avoidance: don't pay an IQ tax for "investing in yourself" (Entry 10).

### 1. Your salary is the largest lump of principal in your life
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The opportunity perspective of narrowing "investing" to buying stocks and funds while neglecting your own income ability.
- In plain terms: In your twenties your biggest asset isn't that bit of savings—it's "the future decades of yourself that can earn money." Treat improving your income ability as your first investment; the return far exceeds studying funds.
- Payoff: From the household balance-sheet view, human capital is the highest-share asset for ordinary people in their youth. Guiso & Sodini, in Household Finance, give the life-cycle curve: at the start of work human capital is close to 1 (100%) of total wealth, then gradually declines as savings accumulate. The Central University of Finance and Economics' "China Human Capital Report 2025" estimates China's total human capital at 4,375.63 trillion yuan in 2023, or 4.0222 million yuan per capita; the World Bank's HCI for China was 0.7 in 2020 (i.e., a newborn grows into a "full-strength" worker at about 70% productivity). A concrete example: an engineer aged 25, earning 100,000 yuan a year with 35 more years to work, has a present value of future income of about 1.8 million yuan, while savings at that point may be only 50,000 yuan—human capital is over 97% of total wealth. So the compounding of improving income ability (education return ~10%/year) far exceeds the 7%–10% annualized you'd chase by studying funds for the same time, and is more certain. Grow this asset first, then talk about allocating financial capital.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Central University of Finance and Economics Center for Human Capital and Labor Economics Research (2025). China Human Capital Report 2025. <https://humancapital.cufe.edu.cn/info/1146/2472.htm>; World Bank (2020). Human Capital Index (HD.HCI.OVRL), China = 0.7. <https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/HD.HCI.OVRL?locations=CN>; Guiso, L. & Sodini, P. (2013). Household Finance: An Emerging Field. <https://www.eief.it/files/2014/01/guiso_sodini-household-finance-an-emerging-field.pdf>
- Note: Human capital cannot be traded or mortgaged, and is highly dependent on health; it needs insurance and continuous learning to hedge depreciation; the China total/per-capita figures are J-F lifetime-income-method estimates and contain methodological assumptions.

### 2. One salary negotiation can offset a year of investing
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The effort of preparation and rehearsal before salary negotiation (usually a few hours, and you must overcome "shame about discussing money").
- In plain terms: Negotiating 10% more at onboarding / a raise is a zero-risk gain that compounds every year. Many are too shy to negotiate, equal to voluntarily giving up money every year.
- Payoff: Glassdoor's 2023 survey of nearly 6,700 professionals shows 54% did not negotiate their last offer and only 46% did—meaning over half gave up a highly certain sum of money. The Atlanta Fed's Wage Growth Tracker shows job-changers' wage growth is persistently higher than stayers' (5.0% vs 3.6% in August 2026). A concrete tally: negotiating 10% more on starting pay (e.g., 60,000 → 66,000 yuan), at a 3.5% annual raise over a 40-year career, cumulatively adds about 494,000 U.S. dollars (an overseas conservative estimate, excluding bonuses and retirement matching); even within China only, the extra 10% becomes the base repeatedly amplified by each year's raise. This is a zero-risk, near-zero-cost pure gain, with a return far above spending the same time studying funds.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Glassdoor / Fishbowl (2023). 54% of Workers Didn't Negotiate Most Recent Job Salary. <https://fortune.com/2023/09/26/half-of-workers-didnt-negotiate-salary>; Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta (2026). Wage Growth Tracker. <https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/data/wage-growth-tracker>
- Note: Negotiation success rate and raise size are backed by large-scale surveys and central-bank tracking data; negotiation should be based on market data (prospectuses / recruitment platforms / peers), and implicit compensation (see Entry 12) must be counted together.

### 3. Main-job skills compound; don't fixate on side hustles
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The fixation on "side hustles are the real money" and the attention loss of spreading energy across ten shallow projects.
- In plain terms: Rather than spread your energy across ten shallow side hustles, get your main job into the top 20%—the compounding of promotion and raises is fiercer. With a solid base, the side hustle is stable.
- Payoff: The education/skill return (Mincer rate of return) in China rose from 4.0% in 1988 to 10.2% in 2001 (Zhang et al. 2005, summarized and adopting 10% as the baseline in PKU Guanghua's Research Brief No. 150), i.e., each extra year of education and each step up in skill raises income by roughly 10%. Forbes' 2026 JobLeads study is more direct: from junior to mid-level positions the average raise is 14%, and from mid to senior an average of 51%—the return of a level jump far exceeds any lateral side hustle. Pour time into main-job depth (top 20%), and push for promotion to the next level; its compounding far beats doing many shallow side hustles. The main job is the base; the side hustle is icing, not a substitute.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Peking University Guanghua School of Management (2019). Research Brief No. 150: Re-estimating China's Long-run Total Factor Productivity. <https://www.gsm.pku.edu.cn/thought_leadership/info/1110/2612.htm>; Forbes (2026). Should You Job-Hop Or Job-Hug? A New Study Offers Surprising Answers. <https://forbes-forbescom-live.non-prod.zephrcf.com/sites/bryanrobinson/2026/09/21/should-you-job-hop-or-job-hug-a-new-study-offers-surprising-answers>
- Note: Skill premium varies by industry and level; over-specialization also carries the risk of being replaced by technology (see Entry 8).

### 4. The cost of career interruption is seriously underestimated
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Assessing the true cost of resigning / a long gap (including the break in seniority, social security, and industry network, not just current wages).
- In plain terms: Quitting to lie flat or a long leave of absence loses more than a few months' wages—it's a break in seniority, social security, and your circle. Coming back often means a step down; many don't count this.
- Payoff: The cost of career interruption is long-term and underestimated. A San Francisco Fed 2023-25 Economic Letter study: if a mother returns to work one year earlier after childbirth, her wage 10–20 years later is 5%–6% higher; shortening the non-employment period by one year can raise future wages by 4.6%–6.8%. UK ONS / Greater London Authority data is more striking: a woman's income five years after her first child is 42% lower than a childless peer's of the same age, and her employment probability is 11 percentage points lower. This shows the gap period loses not just current income but interrupts seniority accumulation, social-security continuity, and industry connections, and coming back often means a downgrade. Before major decisions (resigning, leaving work to study for a master's, long-term travel), be sure to count this hidden impairment into the total.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (2023). How Much Do Work Interruptions Reduce Mothers' Wages? (Economic Letter 2023-25). <https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/el2023-25.pdf>; Greater London Authority / ONS (2023). The impact of motherhood on employment and earnings in London. <https://data.london.gov.uk/blog/the-impact-of-motherhood-on-employment-and-earnings-in-london>
- Note: Motherhood-penalty data is mostly female; male-interruption research is scarcer, but the direction "interruption → seniority break → downgrade on return" applies to everyone; skill updates can reduce interruption losses.

### 5. Run "yourself" like a small company
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Examining yourself from an operator's perspective, spending about 30 minutes a month on a personal balance-sheet review.
- In plain terms: Treat yourself as a company: salary is revenue, skills are assets, loans are liabilities. Regularly check "is my company's competitiveness rising or falling"—this decides how much you can earn more than staring at the market.
- Payoff: Viewing the individual as a "company" makes your true risk exposure explicit: income (salary) is revenue, skills and health are assets, mortgage and car loans are liabilities, and wage-growth rate is your "revenue growth rate." The World Bank's HCI for China was only 0.7 in 2020, meaning health and education depreciation directly lowers your "company's" asset return. The CFA framework "total household wealth = financial capital + human capital" points out that at age 25 human capital can be over 95% of total wealth, with financial capital only a tiny corner. Regularly reviewing "gross margin" (savings rate) and "competitiveness" (industry position, transferable skills, social-security continuity) decides your financial ceiling more than chasing rallies.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: World Bank (2020). Human Capital Index (HD.HCI.OVRL), China = 0.7. <https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/HD.HCI.OVRL?locations=CN>; CFA Institute / FA Magazine (2013). Human Capital And Asset Allocation (household balance-sheet framework). <https://www.fa-mag.com/news/human-capital-and-asset-allocation-25353.html>
- Note: Together with Entries 1, 9, 12 it forms the "personal balance sheet" perspective; this company framework is for self-diagnosis and does not replace professional financial planning.

### 6. Both job-hopping too much and never hopping lose
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Extreme thinking of either "job-hop for raises" or "stay loyal."
- In plain terms: Job-hopping every year looks flaky; ten years without moving gets you easily underpriced. There's a sweet spot in between—hop when you should, settle when you should. Don't go to extremes.
- Payoff: Job-hopping does bring a premium—the Atlanta Fed's Wage Growth Tracker shows job-changers' wage growth is persistently higher than stayers' (5.0% vs 3.6% in August 2026). But Forbes' 2026 JobLeads study gives the counterbalance: only 27% of respondents said "changing employer" brought the biggest career raise, while 31% said "internal promotion" did; 69% of job-hoppers' most recent raise was within 15%. Frequent hopping (<10 months) signals instability and breaks accumulation; never moving gets you easily underpriced internally and misses market prices. Research widely agrees there is a "moderate range," varying greatly by industry [disputed]—the criterion should be "did you get promoted to the next level," not "did you change company."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta (2026). Wage Growth Tracker. <https://www.atlantafed.org/research-and-data/data/wage-growth-tracker>; Forbes (2026). Should You Job-Hop Or Job-Hug? A New Study Offers Surprising Answers. <https://forbes-forbescom-live.non-prod.zephrcf.com/sites/bryanrobinson/2026/09/21/should-you-job-hop-or-job-hug-a-new-study-offers-surprising-answers>
- Note: [Disputed] The optimal job-hopping frequency varies by industry and level; triggering on "level jump + market price gap ≥ 10%" is steadier (see Entries 2, 11).

### 7. Industry Beta matters more than which company you're at
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The choice inertia of watching only the company and ignoring the track.
- In plain terms: In a hot industry, ordinary people also get raises; in a declining industry, even the skilled struggle. Pick the right track and your effort isn't buried.
- Payoff: In income growth, the explanatory power of the industry's overall prosperity (Beta) often exceeds the company's individual (Alpha). China's urban/rural human-capital gap directly confirms the "track effect": the Central University of Finance and Economics' "China Human Capital Report 2025" shows that in 2023 urban per-capita human capital was 5.0544 million yuan and rural only 1.2433 million yuan, an urban/rural per-capita ratio of 4.07; the top per-capita were Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, Jiangsu, Shandong, and the bottom Hainan, Yunnan, Xinjiang, Gansu, Qinghai. Picking a rising track (e.g., digital economy, healthcare) amplifies the return on personal effort; picking a declining track cancels out the effort. Therefore, before "effort," first pick the right industry—it is one of the human-capital decisions with the highest leverage.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Central University of Finance and Economics Center for Human Capital and Labor Economics Research (2025). China Human Capital Report 2025. <https://humancapital.cufe.edu.cn/info/1146/2472.htm>; World Bank (2020). Human Capital Index (HD.HCI.OVRL). <https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/HD.HCI.OVRL?locations=CN>
- Note: Beta and Alpha are hard to decompose precisely, but the importance of industry choice is confirmed by much labor-economics literature; combining with personal transferable skills lowers the cost of changing tracks.

### 8. Skill-obsolescence risk must be hedged with continuous learning
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Time invested in continuous updating (recommend a few hours a week) and a small learning expense.
- In plain terms: The skills you have now may be worthless in ten years. Regularly learn something new, especially transferable abilities—that's the way to keep "your company" from depreciating.
- Payoff: Technology substitution and job restructuring are accelerating, and the half-life of a single skill keeps shrinking. Education returns, while still substantial (China's Mincer return ~10%), are changing in structure: an East China Normal University 2011 study, after controlling for family background and ability, found the true education return fell to 4.6%–5.5% (14.3% overall uncontrolled, 13.1% male, 14.2% female). This means under "degree inflation," the marginal return of a stock diploma is falling, and an old diploma alone can no longer preserve value. Continuously learning new tools + honing transferable skills (communication, analysis, cross-domain integration) is the only way to keep human capital from depreciating; AI/digitalization is reshaping jobs, and the proactive updaters preserve value while the passive lose it.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: East China Normal University (2011). Study on the influence of diploma signaling, occupational factors, and family background on education returns. <https://xbzs.ecnu.edu.cn/CN/html/201106011.htm>; Peking University Guanghua School of Management (2019). Research Brief No. 150. <https://www.gsm.pku.edu.cn/thought_leadership/info/1110/2612.htm>
- Note: Linked with Entry 3 (main-job deepening) and Entry 10 (pitfall avoidance); learning should focus on "skills that verifiably increase income," not diffuse anxiety consumption.

### 9. Health is the foundation of human capital; falling ill = asset impairment
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Daily investment in sleep, exercise, and checkups (time + a little money).
- In plain terms: A serious illness isn't just medical bills—it's income cutoff. Maintain your body like an asset; the extra years you can work earn far more than any fund return.
- Payoff: Health is the foundation of human capital, and its economic value has hard data behind it. A Wuhan University doctoral dissertation based on CHNS estimates the contribution of health human capital to personal income at as high as 19.3%, slightly above that of education human capital. CGSS2015 (3,251 residents) empirical study shows: those with good mental health earn 12.3% more annual occupational income, and those with better physical health 7.1% more (propensity-score-matching result). Pocket-book research further points out that chronic disease significantly negatively affects labor participation and returns, and the true cost of a health shock is systematically underestimated because it's masked by "adaptive job adjustment." A major illness directly interrupts the income cash flow and devours savings—the most expensive "depreciation" of household finances. Keeping your working years is a more certain high-return investment than any financial management.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Wuhan University (doctoral dissertation on health human capital and economic growth). <https://openir.whu.edu.cn/AchievementDetail/degree_58de0fc2-cf89-443c-a85c-7cb4ff4cd31d>; Jiangsu Health Service Management (CGSS2015 empirical study). <http://www.jswssygl.org/jswssygl/ch/reader/view_reference.aspx?aid=2BF99E2DE8AA5BC68A422FA76FF96C7B>
- Note: Health returns contain a "masking effect" (the ill have already self-adaptively adjusted roles), so the true value may be underestimated; critical-illness / medical insurance can partly transfer the "cash-flow-cutoff risk."

### 10. Don't buy useless courses under the name of "investing in yourself"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Sobriety about "knowledge-payment anxiety" and five minutes of questioning before ordering.
- In plain terms: "Investing in yourself" is used by many courses as a payment QR code. Only what truly raises income counts as investment; otherwise it's consumption. Before ordering ask: can this course raise my income by a few points? If you can't say, it's a trap.
- Payoff: Many "wealth-management / cognition courses" are consumption, not investment—they teach no verifiable skill and improve no income. The education return is about 10% (Mincer return), but that refers to systematic education and skill improvement; East China Normal University's 2011 study shows that after controlling for ability, family background, and occupational factors, the true education return is only 4.6%–5.5%. Most short training courses on the market neither reach that marginal return nor can be validated for causality through "instrumental variables." The criterion is simple: after finishing, does it bring quantifiable income or ability gain? If yes, it's investment; if no, it's the "anxiety tax" of emotional consumption. Save that money and put it into an index fund; the long-run return is actually more certain.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: East China Normal University (2011). Study on the influence of diploma signaling, occupational factors, and family background on education returns. <https://xbzs.ecnu.edu.cn/CN/html/201106011.htm>; Peking University Guanghua School of Management (2019). Research Brief No. 150. <https://www.gsm.pku.edu.cn/thought_leadership/info/1110/2612.htm>
- Note: This does not deny all learning, but distinguishes "skills that verifiably increase income" from the "anxiety tax" of emotional consumption; systematic education / certification can still bring about a 10% Mincer return.

### 11. The premise of a side hustle is not harming main-job compounding
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The trade-off of side hustle occupying main-job energy, and the self-deception of "using a side hustle to cover up main-job stagnation."
- In plain terms: A side hustle earns a little extra, but delays your main-job promotion—that's a loss. The side hustle can't hurt the base; that's the red line.
- Payoff: If a side hustle drags down main-job performance and delays promotion, the opportunity cost is very high. Forbes' 2026 JobLeads study: from mid to senior level the average raise is 51%, far above any side-hustle extra; internal promotion (31%) and external job-hopping (27%) are the source of most people's biggest career raises. In other words, the "level-jump compounding" from main-job depth is the main engine of ordinary people's income curve; the side hustle is just icing. Use "does it harm the base" as the upper-bound switch for the side hustle—when the side hustle starts squeezing the time you could use for promotion and deep skill accumulation, hit the brakes. With a solid base, the side hustle is stable (echoing Entry 3).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Forbes (2026). Should You Job-Hop Or Job-Hug? A New Study Offers Surprising Answers. <https://forbes-forbescom-live.non-prod.zephrcf.com/sites/bryanrobinson/2026/09/21/should-you-job-hop-or-job-hug-a-new-study-offers-surprising-answers>; Peking University Guanghua School of Management (2019). Research Brief No. 150. <https://www.gsm.pku.edu.cn/thought_leadership/info/1110/2612.htm>
- Note: Linked with Entries 3, 6, 7; a side hustle fits as "diversification" after the base is solid, not as an escape when the main job stagnates.

### 12. Social security and the housing fund are implicit pay; count them when you leave
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The perspective of counting benefits into the total package, and checking the contribution base and ratio of the five insurances and one fund when comparing offers.
- In plain terms: When negotiating pay, don't just look at "take-home"; the social security and housing fund your employer pays is often over 30% of your salary. Comparing offers or resigning without counting this chunk costs you big.
- Payoff: The employer-paid portion of the five insurances and one fund often equals 25%–40% of salary—a seriously underestimated "implicit compensation." Taking Lai'an County government's 2023 interpretation as an example: at the minimum contribution base of 3,832 yuan, the employer pays the employee monthly 16% pension + 7% medical + 0.5% unemployment ≈ 918 yuan, the individual pays 403 yuan, totaling about 1,320 yuan (excluding the housing fund); adding the housing fund (employer 5%–12%), the employer's total burden easily exceeds 30% of salary. When comparing offers or assessing resignation cost, looking only at take-home salary seriously underestimates the total package; a job-hop "raise" that sacrifices social-security continuity, housing-fund contribution ratio, or subsidies is likely a fake raise. Counting the employer-paid portion into the total package is how you truly see your "operating revenue."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Lai'an County People's Government (2023). Interpretation of urban employee social insurance policy. <https://www.laian.gov.cn/public/161054716/1110644935.html>; Suzhou Industrial Park Social Security and Housing Fund (2023). Contribution and crediting ratios for urban employees' social insurance and housing fund. <https://www.sipspf.org.cn/publish/main/210/2022/20220111112007000004791/20220111112007000004791_.html>
- Note: Contribution ratios and caps vary by locality and must follow the latest local policy; the housing fund is not legally mandatory but is widely provided—upon resignation / city change, pay attention to transfer and lapse effects.
