# VII. Cost-Effective Moves

The truly "cost-effective" moves in investing are usually not the exciting-sounding tricks, but the few things that are low-cost, high-certainty, and compound over the long run. This chapter lists, from highest to lowest cost-performance, the moves ordinary people should do first—most don't rely on predicting the market, but on "making fewer mistakes" and "letting a system execute for you."

These moves fall into three logics: one, cut cost and friction (low fees, long holding, less screen-watching); two, improve behavior (auto-debit, rebalancing thresholds, sell rules, investment log); three, use the institutional dividends (emergency fund, insurance, social security and tax-advantaged accounts, special deductions). Stacked together, their effect far exceeds any single "magic move."

Note: the first few (1–4) are the book's core engine; if you don't get them right, the later, more refined moves are just icing on the cake. Do the parts you can surely control first—cost, discipline, time—and the market will hand you the long-run return.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses):

- Core engine · low-cost long holding: broad-market index dollar-cost averaging (Entry 1), stock-bond mix and rebalancing (Entry 2), emergency fund (Entry 3), long holding low turnover (Entry 4);
- Human capital and cognition: invest in yourself (Entry 5), keep an investment log (Entry 6), insurance backstop (Entry 7);
- Institutional dividends · tax and accounts: compounding + tax-advantaged account (Entry 8), individual-income-tax special additional deductions (Entry 9), index + IPO enhancement (Entry 10);
- Behavior design · automation and discipline: auto DCA / conditional orders (Entry 11), rebalancing threshold (Entry 12), less screen-watching (Entry 13), sell rules (Entry 14).

### 1. Broad-market index fund dollar-cost averaging (flagship move)
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Annual expense ratio 0.03%–0.5% (e.g., VOO/VTI about 0.03%, A-share broad ETF about 0.15%–0.5%) + a few minutes a month to set up and check.
- In plain terms: The flagship of the whole guide. Don't pick stocks, don't time the market, don't watch the screen—just buy a low-cost index covering the whole market and invest a fixed amount monthly. It promises no riches, but parks you firmly on the long-term winner's side.
- Payoff: The source of certainty is data—S&P's SPIVA scorecard shows that over the 15 years to 2024, in 22 U.S. equity fund categories "zero" had a majority of active funds beat the benchmark; large-cap underperformed by 65% in one year, 84% in ten years, 92% in twenty. Meanwhile ICI data (2024) shows index equity funds' average fee is just 0.05% versus 0.64% for active funds—a 0.6-percentage-point annual fee gap compounds over 30 years enough to eat about a quarter of wealth. Buy and hold the whole market, and you get the market's real long-run return at extremely low cost (U.S. stocks about 10% nominal, about 6%–7% real over a century).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices (2025). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard Year-End 2024 (Exhibit, 20-year 92% active underperformance). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-us-year-end-2024.pdf>; Fama, E. F. & French, K. R. (1993). Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds. Journal of Financial Economics 33(1), 3–56. <https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/Data_Library/facts.html>; Investment Company Institute (2024). 2024 Investment Company Fact Book (index/active fund fees). <https://www.ici.org/research/stats/us-funds>
- Note: "A" here means "long-run, cross-market, repeatable"; because A-shares have a high retail share, active funds' short-run win rate occasionally beats U.S. stocks, but fee erosion holds just the same, so low-fee broad-market remains the first choice. For the Chinese SPIVA wording see the annual SPIVA China editions.

### 2. Stock-bond mix + periodic rebalancing
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: One check a year (or adjust when off target by a 5% threshold); restrain the impulse to "go all-in on stocks for high return."
- In plain terms: Stocks up, bonds down—sell stocks, buy bonds, pull the ratio back to target. This isn't timing, it's discipline—forcing you to sell high and buy low, needing no predictive ability.
- Payoff: Vanguard institutional research (2015) concludes clearly: monthly, quarterly, and annual rebalancing show "no significant difference" in risk-adjusted return, but trading count and cost rise sharply with frequency; so annual/semi-annual monitoring + a 5% threshold suffices. Rebalancing's value isn't "earning more" but "controlling volatility and max drawdown"—the classic 60/40 portfolio drew down about −20% to −30% in 2008, far less than all-stocks' −50%+, so you needn't liquidate in panic. It also naturally executes "sell high, buy low" and relies on no judgment at all.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Vanguard Institutional Research (2015). Best Practices for Portfolio Rebalancing (Zilbering, Jaconetti, Kinniry). <https://www.vanguard.ca/documents/best-practices-for-portfolio-rebalancing.pdf>; Vanguard (2019). Getting Back on Track: A Guide to Smart Rebalancing. <https://personal.vanguard.com/pdf/ISGGBOT.pdf>
- Note: Rebalancing is a "risk control" tool, not an "income boost" tool; frequent rebalancing in taxable accounts can create tax bills, so use the annual-threshold method for U.S. accounts, and the same logic for A-share accounts.

### 3. Build a 3–6 month emergency fund before investing
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: This money earns almost zero (in a money market fund / demand deposit / high-yield savings); the opportunity cost is low.
- In plain terms: The emergency fund is the "airbag" of your investment system. Without it, one job loss or medical emergency forces you to liquidate at the worst time. Buffer first, then risk—the order can't reverse.
- Payoff: The CFPB recommends an emergency fund covering 3–6 months of "necessary expenses" (not total income); the Federal Reserve's 2024 SHEP survey shows about 37% of Americans still can't cover a 400 USD unexpected expense. A buffer means at the bear-market bottom you "needn't sell"—and forced selling is the retail investor's worst loss scenario: you lock in the loss at the low and miss the rebound. The emergency fund turns "should I sell" into "no need to sell," directly protecting the long-run compound from interruption.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB). An Essential Guide to Building an Emergency Fund. <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund>; Federal Reserve (2024). Report on the Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households (37% can't cover a $400 unexpected expense). <https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2024-economic-well-being-of-us-households.htm>
- Note: Freelancers / single-income families should take the upper end (6 months or more); the emergency fund is a "defensive asset" and shouldn't be mixed with investment principal, lest you spend it on impulse.

### 4. Long holding + low turnover
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Give up short-term thrills; bear the psychological pressure of a 30%+ paper drawdown.
- In plain terms: Compounding is most powerful, but it takes time. Every sale interrupts compounding once and pays friction once more. After buying good assets, "doing nothing" is the hardest and most profitable discipline.
- Payoff: Trading frequency directly eats returns. Barber & Odean (2000), using 66,000 brokerage accounts, found the top-quintile-turnover accounts earned only 11.4% net annually, while the lowest-turnover group earned 18.5% and the market index 17.9%—the most active earned about 7 percentage points less per year than the least active. DALBAR's QAIB (2024) shows the average stock-fund investor earned only 16.54% for the year, lagging the S&P 500's 25.02% by 848 basis points, and has lagged for 15 years straight. Wells Fargo estimates: from 1995–2024, missing the 30 best days dropped the annualized return from 8.4% to 2.1%. Moving less is the cheapest weapon ordinary people have against the "behavior gap."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2000). Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth. Journal of Finance 55(2). <https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/behavior%20of%20individual%20investors.pdf>; DALBAR (2025). Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB) 2024 (average stock investor 16.54% vs S&P 25.02%). <https://www.dalbar.com/press-release/investors-missed-the-best-of-2024s-market-gains-latest-dalbar-investor-behavior-report-finds/>; Wells Fargo Investment Institute (2025). The Perils of Trying to Time Volatile Markets. <https://www.wellsfargoadvisors.com/research-analysis/reports/policy/volatile-markets.htm>
- Note: Low turnover isn't "never sell"; rebalancing, thesis falsification, and needing cash still require selling, but the principle is to minimize trade count.

### 5. Invest in yourself: human-capital return is often highest
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Time, tuition, energy; the payoff cycle is counted in years.
- In plain terms: Pushing the annualized from 7% to 8% is agonizing; raising monthly pay from 10,000 to 15,000 has the same "return rate" but is far easier. A bigger principal is the real engine of ordinary people's wealth—fix that first.
- Payoff: For most people, the "investable principal" growth from rising main-income beats any financial trick by far. Monthly pay 10,000 → 15,000 equals +50% investable cash flow, for life; while cutting a fund fee from 0.5% to 0.1% saves only 0.4 percentage points a year. The World Bank's *World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior*, Chapter 6 "Household Finance," notes a household's scarcest resource is "financial bandwidth," and raising skills and income improves both saving rate and risk resilience. Becker's human-capital theory also shows education and health investment are the steadiest "assets" with long-run return. Rather than fuss over a 0.5% fee difference, grow the main-income engine first.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: World Bank (2015). World Development Report 2015: Mind, Society, and Behavior, Chapter 6 "Household Finance". <https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/Worldbank/Publications/WDR/WDR%202015/Chapter-6.pdf>; Becker, G. S. (1964/1993). Human Capital: A Theoretical and Empirical Analysis. University of Chicago Press. <https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1992/becker/facts/>
- Note: "C" because "personal income growth" varies widely and is hard to test in a controlled study; but the direction (principal growth >> fee tweaks) is near-common-sense for wage earners.

### 6. Keep an investment log, review afterward
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Write one line per trade—"why buy/sell, what's the expectation, what was the mood" (zero money).
- In plain terms: People are good at forgetting misses and magnifying lucky hits. Write it in black and white; the market scores you. A near-zero-cost, lasting-payoff "meta-move."
- Payoff: A decision log makes "hindsight bias" visible. Behavioral finance finds people selectively remember after a loss and credit luck for skill; writing the buy logic and expectation, then coldly comparing to reality six months later, continuously exposes repeated errors (chasing highs, panic-selling, acting on tips). The SSA behavioral-economics review and World Bank WDR 2015 both note that light mechanisms like "pre-commitment / recording" significantly cut decision noise. One executable practice: record "trigger reason + expected holding period + stop-loss/take-profit condition" per trade, review quarterly—you'll find most losses come from the same behavioral hole, more useful than any paid course.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Social Security Administration (2010). The Role of Behavioral Economics and Behavioral Decision Making in Americans' Retirement Savings Decisions, Social Security Bulletin 70(4). <https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v70n4/v70n4p1.html>; World Bank (2015). World Development Report 2015, Chapter 6 "Household Finance". <https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/Worldbank/Publications/WDR/WDR%202015/Chapter-6.pdf>
- Note: The key to a log isn't "writing" but "reviewing regularly"; writing without reviewing is as good as not writing. Use a spreadsheet or app, with uniform fields.

### 7. Insurance backstop (medical / life / accident)
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Premiums (5%–10% of household annual income for base protection, prioritizing medical + life + accident, wary of wealth-type).
- In plain terms: Investing is offense, insurance is defense. Heavy investing with no backstop is charging forward with no troops behind. Get medical/life cover first, then talk returns.
- Payoff: One serious illness or accident can punch through investment principal and household cash flow—World Bank WDR 2015 stresses a household's first line of financial-risk management is "preventing a one-time shock from zeroing years of savings." An unprotected household is often forced to liquidate investments low in a crisis, even borrow at usury, cutting the long-run compound in one stroke. Cover large medical bills with a million-medical policy (annual premium hundreds to about 1,000 yuan), and cover the breadwinner's death risk with term life (coverage = outstanding mortgage + children's education + 5–10 years of family expenses)—very high leverage. Insurance buys "no broken capital," letting you hold long and not over-conserve out of accident fear.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: World Bank (2015). World Development Report 2015, Chapter 6 "Household Finance" (risk management and liquidity buffer). <https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/Worldbank/Publications/WDR/WDR%202015/Chapter-6.pdf>; Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB). An Essential Guide to Building an Emergency Fund (emergency / shock resistance). <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund>
- Note: Protection comes before return-type insurance; secure "consumption-type" protection first, then grow via investment accounts—don't turn premiums into low-return savings.

### 8. Compounding + tax-advantaged account stacked
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Account-planning time; private pension annual contribution cap 12,000 yuan (nationwide from 2024).
- In plain terms: A tax-advantaged account + long holding are the two accelerators of compounding. Use it legally if you can—especially the higher your income and marginal tax rate, the more you save.
- Payoff: The private pension follows the Ministry of Finance and STA Announcement 2022 No. 34 (extended nationwide by 2024 No. 21) for tax deferral: at contribution, up to 12,000 yuan a year is deducted from comprehensive income; investment gains are untaxed meanwhile; at withdrawal a separate 3% tax applies. For wage earners in the 10%–20% marginal bracket, just the contribution deduction saves 1,200–2,400 yuan a year; at 7% annualized over 30 years, 12,000 yuan/year under tax deferral + compounding accumulates significantly more than a taxable account. Stacked with low-fee indices, the tax advantage is one of the few "certain gains" for ordinary people.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration (2022). Announcement on Individual Pension Personal-Income-Tax Policy (Ministry of Finance & STA Announcement 2022 No. 34). <https://sichuan.chinatax.gov.cn/art/2022/11/4/art_7757_794159.html>; Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration (2024). Announcement on Implementing the Individual Pension Personal-Income-Tax Preference Nationwide (2024 No. 21). <http://beijing.chinatax.gov.cn/bjswj/c105411/202212/048860fe6bec43df84934d9f6dca4959.shtml>
- Note: The private pension is taxed at a separate 3% at withdrawal, suiting those whose current marginal rate exceeds 3%; products inside the account should still be low-fee, or fees eat the tax advantage.

### 9. Use the individual-income-tax special additional deductions
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: A few minutes a year to check and file in the tax app.
- In plain terms: This is "free money" from the state; not claiming it is a loss. Spend a few minutes each year checking deductions—the tax saved is net income.
- Payoff: Per the *Interim Measures for Special Additional Deductions for Individual Income Tax* (State Council [2018] No. 41), deductible items include children's education (from 1,000 yuan/month per child, raised to 2,000 yuan for multi-child/infant care from 2023), continuing education, serious-illness medical (self-paid over 15,000 yuan, capped at 80,000 yuan), first-home mortgage interest (1,000 yuan/month, up to 240 months), housing rent (800–1,500 yuan/month), and supporting elderly (2,000–3,000 yuan/month). For an only child + mortgage + 1 child, several thousand yuan a month is deductible, directly lowering taxable income; for the 10%–20% bracket that's thousands of yuan saved a year. This is a zero-risk, certain "raise."
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: State Council (2018). Notice on Issuing the Interim Measures for Special Additional Deductions for Individual Income Tax (State Council [2018] No. 41). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/2018-12/22/content_5351181.htm>; State Taxation Administration (2025). Guide to Confirming Special Additional Deduction Info (from 2025 children's education / infant care 2,000, supporting elderly 3,000). <https://news.cctv.cn/2025/12/01/ARTIl08Iz1MzQJNdo4Q8eF1v251201.shtml>
- Note: Standards have been raised several times since 2019; follow the tax app's current-year wording; rent and mortgage interest can't be claimed together—choose the higher.

### 10. Index + IPO enhancement (A-shares)
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The market-value base held to participate in IPOs (use a broad ETF; no need to overweight high-risk stocks).
- In plain terms: You were going to buy the base anyway; IPO subscription is a free ride on the side. But don't overweight high-risk stocks to raise win odds—putting the cart before the horse.
- Payoff: A-share IPO subscription has long been "low-threshold enhancement," but returns swing wildly with market-based issue pricing. Per China Securities Journal citing Wind: IPO break-par rate was 5.03% in 2020, 4.2% in 2021, 28.27% in 2022 (peak), 16.61% in 2023, about 3.2% in 2024; first-day average gain was 157% in 2021, 30.5% in 2022, 66.5% in 2023, about 105% in 2024. So 2022 was "winning a lot is catching a falling knife," while after 2023 break-par fell and the money effect returned. Subscription on a broad base still adds positive return, but it's no longer risk-free—pick, don't blind-subscribe.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: China Securities Journal (2024). IPO-ecosystem change: break-par rate falls to about 3%, first-day average gain doubles (Wind data). <https://finance.sina.com.cn/stock/zqgd/2024-04-07/doc-inaqyxpu8470694.shtml>; Sina Finance (2024). Dragon-Year new stocks average gain near 200%, none broke par (historical break-par rates). <https://finance.sina.cn/2024-06-16/detail-inaywspq1335358.d.html>
- Note: [Contested] After break-par, "enhancement" becomes "drag"; under registration-based pricing the IPO edge converges. Subscribe mainly on market value, don't lever or concentrate holdings for it. The near-0 break-par in 2025 is a phase, not a steady state.

### 11. Use conditional orders / auto DCA instead of manual
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The time to set a DCA / conditional order once (then near-zero maintenance).
- In plain terms: The biggest enemy of manual DCA is you (afraid to buy when down, want to chase when up). Set auto-debit / conditional orders and let the system execute—don't itch to touch.
- Payoff: Behavioral economics proves the power of "default options." Madrian & Shea (2001) found that after 401(k) auto-enrollment, participation rose from 49% to 86%; Thaler & Benartzi's "Save More Tomorrow" plan lifted employees' saving rate from 3.5% all the way to 13.6% (after four raises). For retail, auto DCA turns "should I buy" into "the system already bought," dodging timing emotion; the SSA behavioral review and Nobel laureate Thaler both stress automation as the strongest nudge turning "good intentions" into "actual savings." Set it and stop watching daily—discipline is backstopped by the system.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Madrian, B. C. & Shea, D. F. (2001). The Power of Suggestion: Inertia in 401(k) Participation and Savings Behavior. Quarterly Journal of Economics 116(4). <https://www.jstor.org/stable/2696507>; Thaler, R. H. & Benartzi, S. (2004). Save More Tomorrow™. Journal of Political Economy 112(S1), S164–S187. <https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2017/thaler/159673-richard-thaler-interview-transcript>; Social Security Administration (2010). Behavioral Economics and Retirement Savings. <https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v70n4/v70n4p1.html>
- Note: Auto doesn't mean "never adjust"; you can still raise the DCA amount yearly with income growth (mimic SMarT's auto-escalation).

### 12. Set a "rebalancing threshold," not timing
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Set a deviation threshold (e.g., ±5% or once a year), write it into the plan.
- In plain terms: Don't guess highs and lows—give the portfolio an "alarm": adjust back when it's off by 5%. It's the only "buy-low-sell-high" mechanism needing no prophecy ability.
- Payoff: Same logic as Entry 2 but more emphasis on "rules." Vanguard (2015) empirically: monthly/quarterly/annual rebalancing show "no significant difference" in risk-adjusted return, while frequent rebalancing only adds cost; so use a "threshold trigger" (off target by 5%) rather than "calendar timing"—fewer useless trades, and the stock-bond ratio doesn't drift. Its essence is removing the "is it expensive now" question no one can answer, handing the decision to a set rule—assets that fell more get bought back, those that rose get sold, naturally selling high and buying low long-run, costing no willpower.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Vanguard Institutional Research (2015). Best Practices for Portfolio Rebalancing (5% threshold, annual/semi-annual monitoring). <https://www.vanguard.ca/documents/best-practices-for-portfolio-rebalancing.pdf>; Vanguard (2019). Getting Back on Track: A Guide to Smart Rebalancing. <https://personal.vanguard.com/pdf/ISGGBOT.pdf>
- Note: The threshold shouldn't be too narrow (e.g., 1%–2%) or frequent trading wastes taxes; 5% or annual is the balance of cost and discipline for most.

### 13. Turn off real-time quotes / watch the screen less
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Turn off app push, delete the red/green self-selected alerts (zero money).
- In plain terms: Eight of ten people who check their account ten times a day are losing (see Chapter 4 and Chapter 12 data). Turn off notifications, look once a quarter—your return may even be better.
- Payoff: Screen-watching frequency correlates with trading frequency, and trading frequency eats returns (see Entry 4: the most active accounts earn about 7 points less a year). Barber & Odean (2000) and DALBAR QAIB (2024: investors lag the benchmark by 848 bps) point to the same conclusion—"the more you watch, the more you move, the more you lose." Multiple SSE yearbooks also show individual-investor returns fall as trading frequency rises. Turning off app notifications and setting a "quarterly review day" replaces willpower with environment design; information overload is the fuel of loss, and watching less is erring less.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2000). Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth. Journal of Finance 55(2). <https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/behavior%20of%20individual%20investors.pdf>; DALBAR (2025). Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB) 2024. <https://www.dalbar.com/press-release/investors-missed-the-best-of-2024s-market-gains-latest-dalbar-investor-behavior-report-finds/>
- Note: Watching less isn't "never reviewing"; do one disciplined check per the Entry 12 threshold each quarter/half-year, and let the system run the rest of the time.

### 14. Write the "sell rule" before buying
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Before buying, write clearly "under what conditions to sell" (target price / thesis falsified / need cash).
- In plain terms: Most people only plan "buy," not "sell," so they hold through dips and sell randomly on rallies. Write "when do I sell" before buying—it insures your future self.
- Payoff: This is an application of "pre-commitment mechanism." World Bank WDR 2015 notes embedding decision rules "into the product/plan" (e.g., auto-execute, commitment accounts) significantly lifts saving and investing discipline; the SSA behavioral review also shows pre-written rules counter loss aversion and hindsight. Concrete practice: at purchase, record "buy price, logic, expected holding period, falsification condition, take-profit/stop-loss line" together. When price triggers, execute by rule—no panic on drops, no greed on rises—locking the "future emotional self" in the rule. It doesn't raise win rate, but steadily cuts the costliest error class: not selling when you should, selling what you should hold.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: World Bank (2015). World Development Report 2015, Chapter 6 "Household Finance" (commitment mechanisms / auto-execution). <https://www.worldbank.org/content/dam/Worldbank/Publications/WDR/WDR%202015/Chapter-6.pdf>; Social Security Administration (2010). Behavioral Economics and Retirement Savings Decisions. <https://www.ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v70n4/v70n4p1.html>
- Note: Rules must be "operable, verifiable" (e.g., "sell only if it breaks XX and fundamentals change"); vague rules equal no rules; pair with the Entry 6 investment log for review.
