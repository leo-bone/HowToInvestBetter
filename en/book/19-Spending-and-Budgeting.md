# XIX. Spending and Budgeting

Investing earns the "increment," while spending and budgeting manage the "stock leakage." For ordinary people whose principal is still thin, saving 100 yuan is more certain than risking volatility to earn 100 yuan—no principal needed, no risk taken, and tax-free. This chapter isn't about "how to buy funds," but about "how to first stop the money from leaking": first build the base of a savings rate, then use bookkeeping to see where the money goes, and finally use a few low-cost rules (cooling-off period, hourly-wage conversion, three-column categorization) to keep impulsive and irrational spending out the door. The underlying logic comes almost entirely from hard data in behavioral economics and household finance, not the slogan "saving is glorious."

First look at some national numbers: the People's Bank of China's four quarterly 2023 urban-depositor surveys show the share of residents inclined to "save more" was 58.0%, 58.0%, 62%, and 61% respectively, clearly above the 53% average of 2020–2022; National Bureau of Statistics data for the same period shows 2023 per-capita disposable income of 39,218 yuan (year-on-year +6.3%), per-capita consumption expenditure of 26,796 yuan (year-on-year +9.2%), consumption growth outpacing income growth, and the household savings rate (per-capita basis) had fallen to 31.7%. This shows there is a whole set of consumption-habit gaps between "wanting to save" and "actually saving"—and this chapter fills exactly that gap. More crucially, behavioral finance proves saving can be "designed": Thaler's Save More Tomorrow plan raised participants' savings rate from 3.5% to 13.6%, and the UK's auto-enrollment pension sign-up rate exceeded 90%. Saving isn't about gritting through with self-discipline, but about good rules.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses): the base of saving and frugality (why save, savings-rate context)—Entry 1, Entry 2; seeing where the money goes (bookkeeping and subscription leaks)—Entry 4, Entry 5; controlling large and impulsive spending (cooling-off period, hourly-wage conversion, three-column categorization)—Entry 3, Entry 9, Entry 12; spending where it's worth more (experience over stuff, buy right not cheap, avoid conspicuous consumption)—Entry 6, Entry 7, Entry 8; don't pay out of your own pocket for "peace of mind" and "face" (extended warranties, debt)—Entry 10, Entry 11.

### 1. What you save is after-tax return
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Rebalancing the overweight on "earning more is the only way," and admitting frugality also creates certain returns.
- In plain terms: Saving 100 yuan is more certain than investing to earn 100 yuan—no principal, no risk, tax-free. When principal is small, saving beats earning; every yuan you don't spend is one more yuan of investable principal.
- Payoff: Saving 100 yuan equals net 100 yuan of after-tax income; to earn 100 yuan by investing, you first need principal, bear volatility, and get nibbled by taxes and fees. China's comprehensive individual income tax is a seven-tier progressive rate of 3%–45%; the higher the marginal rate, the more prominent the cost-performance of "saving" versus "earning." At 2023 figures, per-capita disposable income was 39,218 yuan and per-capita consumption 26,796 yuan; at the margin, every extra yuan saved is one more yuan of investable principal; at an 8% long-run annualized return, saving an extra 500 yuan a month and investing for 30 years grows to about 740,000 yuan. For those with limited principal, frugality is a zero-risk, zero-tax "rate of return"—the seed capital for investment compounding. It doesn't change your risk, but directly enlarges the chips you bet with.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: National Bureau of Statistics (2024). 2023 Residents' Income and Consumption Expenditure (per-capita disposable income 39,218 yuan, consumption 26,796 yuan). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202401/t20240116_1946622.html>; Thaler, R. (1985). Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice, Marketing Science 4(3):199–214 (mental accounting: saved money and earned money are equal in the wealth account). <https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.4.3.199>
- Note: Limitation: "saving" has a floor (basic living and developmental spending cannot be cut), and being overly stingy hurts quality of life and relationships; this entry emphasizes eliminating waste at equal satisfaction, not compressing necessary consumption. Cross-reference: Entry 2, Entry 4.

### 2. Lifestyle creep quietly eats your principal
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The inertia of raising spending one notch each time income rises one notch, which needs sustained restraint to break.
- In plain terms: Salary goes up, spending follows, and in the end you still can't save. For financial freedom, a share of the income increase must go into investing, not all into upgrading your life.
- Payoff: After income rises, consumption often upgrades in sync (bigger house, pricier car, more frequent dining out and subscriptions), the savings rate stays put, and financial freedom is always one step away. National Bureau of Statistics data shows 2023 per-capita consumption expenditure rose +9.2% year-on-year, faster than the +6.3% growth of disposable income; the National Institution for Finance and Development (NIFD) "2023 China Leverage Rate Report" points out the household savings rate had fallen to 31.7%, with consumption growth clearly outrunning income. Behind this is "lifestyle creep": the new income is eaten by instant consumption upgrades. Locking a fixed share (e.g., 50%) of each raise into an investment account, rather than upgrading life across the board, is how you preserve the savings rate. The PBOC's 2023 survey also shows residents' saving willingness rose to 58%–62%, indicating a strong defensive mindset, but willingness only works when it lands in the action of "automatic transfer."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: National Bureau of Statistics (2024). 2023 Residents' Income and Consumption Expenditure (consumption +9.2% faster than income +6.3%). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202401/t20240116_1946622.html>; National Institution for Finance and Development NIFD (2024). Fighting for the "Nominal": 2023 China Leverage Rate Report (household savings rate fell to 31.7%). <http://www.nifd.cn/Uploads/SeriesReport/7f9287b2-1405-4f24-a2d6-d7690c47fcf3.pdf>
- Note: Limitation: in the low-income stage, "improve life first" is itself reasonable; this entry targets the group "income already rose but savings rate didn't." Suggest using a "savings-rate target" (e.g., a fixed 20%) to constrain consumption in reverse. Cross-reference: Entry 1, Entry 10.

### 3. Before a large purchase, enforce a cooling-off period
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The restraint of waiting 24–72 hours (extend to 30 days for big items).
- In plain terms: Want to buy something expensive? Wait a day first. If you still want it the next day, it's probably a real need; if you don't want it after waiting, that's money saved.
- Payoff: Impulse buying happens at the emotional peak, and emotions cool. Behavioral research shows the "pain" of payment is greatly numbed by credit cards and one-click checkout—Prelec & Simester (2001) found that for the same item, people were willing to pay nearly double (up to ~2x) when paying by credit card versus cash, because the card dulls the "pain of spending." Conversely, artificially inserting a cooling-off period is waking the numbed pain back up. Industry compilation Baymard Institute, across 49 studies, puts cart-abandonment rate at about 70%, showing that "pausing before paying" alone blocks most purchases. Set a hard rule—"anything over X yuan must wait a day / big items wait 30 days"—and it intercepts lots of irrational spending, at zero cost and zero risk. It doesn't rely on willpower; it drags the decision from a "hot state" to a "cold state."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Prelec, D. & Simester, D. (2001). Always Leave Home Without It: The Effect of Credit Cards on Willingness to Pay, Marketing Letters 12(1):5–13. <https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1010950306613>; Finlume (2024). 7 Psychology-Backed Strategies to Stop Impulse Buying (24-hour / 30-day cooling-off, ~70% cart-abandonment from Baymard). <https://finlume.net/en/blog/reduce-impulse-buying-psychology>
- Note: Limitation: a cooling-off period is meaningless for "true needs" (e.g., fixing a fridge); the rule should cover only non-essential consumption; addictive shopping disorder needs professional help. Cross-reference: Entry 9, Entry 12.

### 4. Bookkeeping isn't to save money, but to see the leaks
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Ten-odd minutes to half an hour a month on bookkeeping (can be lowered by auto-syncing bank flows with an app).
- In plain terms: Bookkeeping isn't about stinginess; it's about seeing where the money leaks. Those dozens-of-yuan deliveries, rides, and subscriptions may add up to your biggest hole.
- Payoff: The value of bookkeeping isn't "saving" but exposing the structure of "where the money went." A behavioral-review compilation of studies shows regular bookkeepers have a savings rate about 40% higher than non-bookkeepers, and spend 15%–20% less—note, this happens even without "deliberately dieting consumption," purely by the behavior change from "being seen" (observer effect). A Cambridge University study of 12,000 people likewise found that those who regularly monitor finances save more, owe less, and feel less financial stress. In the National Bureau of Statistics' 2023 data, per-capita consumption was 26,796 yuan, of which transport and communication +14.3%, education/culture/recreation +17.6%, and other goods and services +17.1%—these high-frequency small items are the easiest to ignore; without bookkeeping, you'll only ever remember "I didn't buy anything big this month" yet miss dozens of 30-yuan leaks. Seeing is the only way to plug.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Freenance (2025). Why Track Expenses — Scientific Arguments, Research, and Habits (compiling Journal of Consumer Research 2019, University of Cambridge 2021: bookkeepers' savings rate ~40% higher, spending 15–20% lower). <https://freenance.io/guides/why-track-expenses-scientific-arguments>; National Bureau of Statistics (2024). 2023 Residents' Income and Consumption Expenditure. <https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/zxfb/202401/t20240116_1946622.html>
- Note: Limitation: bookkeeping itself generates no return and must be linked with "adjust after seeing" to work; obsessing over the second decimal place adds burden—recommend checking category shares monthly. Cross-reference: Entry 1, Entry 5.

### 5. Subscriptions are chronic leakage
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: A few minutes each quarter to export and clean the auto-renewal list from bank cards / Alipay / WeChat.
- In plain terms: Those tens-of-yuan monthly memberships keep charging if you forget to cancel. Doing a subscription audit each quarter saves more than people seriously underestimate.
- Payoff: Subscriptions are a typical leak point of "small amount, high frequency, default renewal," and people systematically underestimate them. C+R Research and West Monroe 2023 surveys both show Americans actually spend about 219 U.S. dollars a month on subscriptions but self-estimate only 86 dollars, an understatement of 154%, with 84% underestimating their subscription spending; SubScrub's 2024 compilation estimates the average person wastefully spends about 348 U.S. dollars a year on "forgotten" subscriptions. The same holds in yuan terms: video, music, cloud storage, tools, auto-renewal memberships—low unit price but considerable in aggregate, and "default renewal" makes people forget to cancel. Doing a "subscription audit" each quarter often saves a few percentage points of monthly cash flow—for a fund being dollar-cost-averaged, this saved money directly becomes future principal, with considerable long-run compounding.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: CBS News (2023). Experts suggest taking stock of how much your monthly subscriptions really cost (citing C+R Research, West Monroe: monthly 219 dollars, understated 133 dollars, 84% underestimate). <https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/newyork/news/experts-suggest-taking-stock-of-how-much-your-monthly-subscriptions-really-cost-before-the-end-of-the-year>; SubScrub (2024). The Average Person Spends $348/Year on Forgotten Subscriptions (compiling C+R Research 2023, West Monroe 2023). <https://subscruber.com/blog/average-person-subscription-spending>
- Note: Limitation: subscriptions also have real value (legitimate software, learning platforms); the audit aims not to cut all but to clear the "forgotten" part; use the credit-card bill's "subscription" category for a one-click check. Cross-reference: Entry 4, Entry 11.

### 6. Buying experiences is worth more than buying things
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Moving some money from "stockpiling stuff" to "experiences" (travel, courses, gatherings, shows).
- In plain terms: Spend the same money, and the happiness from a trip often lasts longer than from buying a bag. Things you get used to right after buying; experiences stay with you longer.
- Payoff: Whether the same amount goes to "experiences" or "stuff" makes a significant happiness difference. Van Boven & Gilovich (2003), in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, through a national large-sample survey and experiments found that people recall experiential purchases (travel, courses, gatherings) with higher happiness than material purchases, and the following 15 years of much research (Carter & Gilovich 2010, Kumar & Gilovich 2016, etc.) repeatedly confirmed: experiences resist "hedonic adaptation" more, are more easily positively reconstructed, and strengthen social ties. A 2019 review in the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Acta Psychologica Sinica also confirmed experiential purchases more effectively raise happiness than material ones. The lesson for investors: on non-essential consumption, prioritize spending on "experiences" rather than "ornaments"—it both raises quality of life and reduces the pile-up of stuff that depreciates the moment you buy it. Same money, higher happiness output, equal to "more cost-effective consumption."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Van Boven, L. & Gilovich, T. (2003). To Do or to Have? That Is the Question, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 85(6):1193–1202. <https://db.cngb.org/data_resources/literature/14674824>; Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (2019). How to spend money happier? The effect of purchase type on happiness (review of Van Boven & Gilovich 2003, etc.). <https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlkxjz/article/2019/1671-3710/1671-3710-27-12-2133.shtml>
- Note: Limitation: experiential consumption also needs to fit your means, and some "stuff" (instruments, sports gear) approximates experiential because it generates experiences; this entry opposes pure-material buying for showing off / hoarding. Cross-reference: Entry 7, Entry 8.

### 7. A "pricey-when-warranted" buy beats a cheap compromise
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The judgment and time to "buy the right version once" for durables.
- In plain terms: Some things, bought cheap and replaced repeatedly, end up costlier; buy right once and use for years is the saving. Don't compromise on key items; that's where you should pinch pennies.
- Payoff: For key durables (shoes, appliances, tools, mattresses), buying cheap knock-offs often breaks fast and gets replaced repeatedly, and the total cost of ownership (TCO) is actually higher. Consumer Reports' long-run reliability tests repeatedly show low-price models often don't win on failure rate and repair cost; buying right once and using 5–10 years, the per-year cost is usually lower than the "cheap → broken → rebuy" loop. Conversely, for fast-iterating, low-frequency non-key items (fast fashion, disposable gadgets), there's no need to chase high specs. The core is to tier your money: dare to pay the right price on categories that are "high-frequency use, affect daily experience, expensive to repair," and dare to pinch on "low-frequency, replaceable" categories. This neither lowers quality of life nor gets repeatedly harvested by the "low-price trap," and long-run total spending is lower with less hassle.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Consumer Reports (ongoing). Product reliability and total cost of ownership (TCO) tests (cheap durable knock-offs often higher failure rate and replacement cost than buying right once). <https://www.consumerreports.org/>; National Institution for Finance and Development NIFD (2024). 2023 China Leverage Rate Report (residents prefer safe assets and cautious debt, consistent with the steady "buy right not cheap" orientation). <http://www.nifd.cn/Uploads/SeriesReport/7f9287b2-1405-4f24-a2d6-d7690c47fcf3.pdf>
- Note: Limitation: TCO judgment needs experience; "buying expensive" ≠ "buying right"—combine with reviews and your own usage frequency; this entry opposes compromising on key items purely for cheapness. Cross-reference: Entry 6, Entry 10.

### 8. Conspicuous consumption is a negative-return investment
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The impulse to pay for "face," which needs identifying and restraining.
- In plain terms: Things bought to show off earn nothing and burn money; those who truly get it don't look at your stuff. Money spent for face is a negative return in financial terms.
- Payoff: Things bought to display status (name bags, luxury cars, extravagance beyond need) generate no cash flow, accelerate principal consumption, and have very low "signaling" value outside your acquaintance circle. Thorstein Veblen's 1899 The Theory of the Leisure Class coined "conspicuous consumption," still repeatedly validated by status-consumption research: such spending serves "being seen" more than use utility. The cost to investors is directly eroding principal that could compound—at 8% annualized, 100,000 yuan spent would have grown to about 1,000,000 yuan in 30 years; and conspicuous goods depreciate shockingly on resale, almost certainly a net loss. Turning the "for others' eyes" budget into "for your own cash flow" investment is the most cost-effective status upgrade.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Veblen, T. (1899). The Theory of the Leisure Class (coined conspicuous consumption). <https://www.gutenberg.org/files/833/833-h/833-h.htm>; Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (2019). How to spend money happier? The effect of purchase type on happiness (status / material consumption lower utility than experiential). <https://journal.psych.ac.cn/xlkxjz/article/2019/1671-3710/1671-3710-27-12-2133.shtml>
- Note: Limitation: moderate "presentable consumption" has real function in social settings; this entry targets the part that is "excessive for showing off and sacrifices saving"; signaling value varies by person. Cross-reference: Entry 6, Entry 11.

### 9. For big-ticket decisions, convert to "wage per hour"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The habit of converting price into work hours (set once, reuse long term).
- In plain terms: A 3,000-yuan item doesn't sound expensive—but at a 100-yuan hourly wage that's 30 hours of work. Ask yourself: is it worth 30 hours of work for it? Many pseudo-needs instantly reveal themselves.
- Payoff: Dividing a 3,000-yuan consumption by your hourly wage (e.g., 100 yuan/hour) = 30 hours of life. Measuring with "would I willingly work 30 hours for it" filters out lots of pseudo-needs. This is backed by two hard studies: first, Whillans et al. (2017) in PNAS, "Buying Time Promotes Happiness," proved people generally "undervalue time, overvalue money," and proactively using money to buy time (rather than stockpile stuff) is actually happier; second, Frederick et al. (2009) proposed "opportunity cost neglect"—when spending, people rarely automatically think "this money could have done something else," and once you explicitly convert price into work hours or "the abandoned alternative goal," the willingness to buy drops noticeably. Hourly-wage conversion is the cheap tool against both biases: it turns the abstract price into the real life-time you pay, making pseudo-needs show themselves on the spot.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Whillans, A. V., et al. (2017). Buying Time Promotes Happiness, PNAS 114(32):8523–8527. <https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1706541114>; Frederick, S., et al. (2009). Opportunity Cost Neglect, Journal of Consumer Research 36(4):553–561. <https://doi.org/10.1086/599764>
- Note: Limitation: the hourly-wage method may inversely overstate cost for those "time-rich but cash-tight," needing marginal-utility adjustment; freelancers can use a "target hourly wage" rather than the book wage. Cross-reference: Entry 3, Entry 12.

### 10. Extended warranties and membership insurance are mostly negative-expected-value
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Vigilance against "paying extra for peace of mind," and doing an expected-value tally before ordering.
- In plain terms: Merchants dare sell extended warranties because they've calculated you most likely won't use it and the money is free. Extended warranties on small appliances are basically giving money away; what you should really insure is the unbearable stuff like illness and death.
- Payoff: Merchants dare sell extended warranties because actuarially they win for sure. The University of Nebraska–Lincoln College of Business (2023), citing Consumer Reports and WarrantyWeek data, notes U.S. consumers spend about 23 billion U.S. dollars a year on extended warranties / protection plans for appliances, electronics, computers, and phones, and retailers keep over 50% of the premium directly as profit; most small appliances and electronics are quite reliable within the two-year warranty coverage, and the contracts mostly go unused. More crucially, extended warranties cover "low-probability, low-loss" repairs, while what you should really insure is "low-probability, high-loss" risk (critical illness, death, accident)—the former is negative-expected-value consumption, the latter is positive-expected-value protection. Conclusion: except for easily-dropped phones and high-priced, expensive-to-repair devices, most extended warranties are worse than putting that money into a "self-insurance fund."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: University of Nebraska–Lincoln (2023). Nebraska Business Tip: Are Extended Warranties Needed? (citing Consumer Reports, WarrantyWeek: ~23 billion dollars/year, retailers keep ≥50%). <https://business.unl.edu/news/nebraska-business-tip-are-extended-warranties-needed/>; KSAT / Consumer Reports (2023). Read that warranty fine print before you spend big bucks (CR: most warranties not worth it, better keep emergency cash). <https://www.ksat.com/news/local/2023/10/13/read-that-warranty-fine-print-before-you-spend-big-bucks/>
- Note: Limitation: for high-priced, fragile, very-expensive-to-repair devices, an extended warranty may turn positive-expected-value; credit cards often bundle extended warranty, check benefits before deciding. Cross-reference: Entry 7, Chapter 14 (Insurance).

### 11. Consuming on debt is taxing your future self
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The sustained restraint of overdrafting the future for present pleasure.
- In plain terms: Swiping a credit card for things you don't urgently need equals borrowing high-interest money above 10% and spending future money. This is worse than not investing—you're paying interest out of pocket.
- Payoff: Using credit cards and consumer loans to buy non-essentials equals spending future income early at high interest, with interest compounding. Regulation caps credit-card overdraft rates at a daily rate of 0.05% (five ten-thousandths), equivalent to about 18.25% annualized—far above the long-run expected return of stocks and bonds, meaning "borrowing to consume" almost certainly loses to the math of "borrowing to invest." The National Institution for Finance and Development (NIFD) "2023 China Leverage Rate Report" shows that at end-2023 resident deposits reached 137.9 trillion yuan (up 16.7 trillion from the prior year), and the household balance sheet shows "safe-asset share rising, risky-asset share falling"—essentially deleveraging and cautious borrowing; the PBOC's 2023 survey also shows residents' investment preference is steady (wealth-management 43.9%, funds 21.5%, stocks 15.3%). For ordinary people, pay off high-interest consumer debt before investing—the order can't be reversed. Debt is a certain loss; investment is an uncertain gain.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: National Institution for Finance and Development NIFD (2024). 2023 China Leverage Rate Report (resident deposits 137.9 trillion yuan, safe-asset share up, risky-asset share down). <http://www.nifd.cn/Uploads/SeriesReport/7f9287b2-1405-4f24-a2d6-d7690c47fcf3.pdf>; People's Bank of China (2023). Q1 2023 Urban Depositor Survey Report (residents' investment preference: wealth-management 43.9%, funds 21.5%, stocks 15.3%). <https://www.cnr.cn/newscenter/native/gd/20230404/t20230404_526206104.shtml>
- Note: Limitation: low-interest debt that forms assets, such as a mortgage, is excluded; this entry targets high-interest consumer debt. The overdraft-rate cap of "0.05% daily" comes from the PBOC's "Notice on Matters Concerning Credit Card Business" (Yin Fa [2016] No. 111). Cross-reference: Entry 1, Entry 10.

### 12. Separate spending into three columns: "need / want / follow-the-crowd"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Three seconds of categorization before ordering (can be made into a phone memo three-column template).
- In plain terms: Before buying, pause three seconds: is it a must, a want, or being led by the crowd? The higher the "follow-the-crowd" column's share, the faster the money leaks. Review this column regularly.
- Payoff: Sorting each expense into three columns—"need" for survival essentials, "want" for quality improvement, "follow-the-crowd" for pure atmosphere-driven—quickly exposes wealth leaks. This has solid behavioral grounding: Thaler's (1985) "mental accounting" theory says people book money into different accounts by category, and without explicit categorization, "follow-the-crowd consumption" blends into "normal spending" and gets ignored; Frederick et al.'s (2009) "opportunity cost neglect" proves that once each sum is explicitly tagged with "what it sacrificed," irrational spending drops. In practice, treat the follow-the-crowd column's share as a "leakage indicator" and review it monthly; the higher the ratio, the more easily you're led by marketing and environment. It belongs to the same toolkit as "cooling-off period" and "hourly-wage conversion": categorize first, then cool, then validate with time cost—three lines of defense stacked, and impulsive and follow-the-crowd spending have nowhere to hide.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Thaler, R. (1985). Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice, Marketing Science 4(3):199–214. <https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.4.3.199>; Frederick, S., et al. (2009). Opportunity Cost Neglect, Journal of Consumer Research 36(4):553–561. <https://doi.org/10.1086/599764>
- Note: Limitation: the three columns are an "awareness tool," not an abstinence order; the want column can reasonably be kept; the key is to make the follow-the-crowd column visible and controllable. Cross-reference: Entry 3, Entry 9.
