# III. Behavioral Biases

Behavioral finance, using decades of account-level data, tells us: those who lag the market long term are rarely beaten by "a brutal market"—more often it's "themselves trading too much." This chapter lists human reactions, backed by papers, that lose money steadily—from overtrading, timing, chasing rallies and selling panic, to loss aversion, confirmation bias, mental accounting. They aren't moral flaws; they're factory settings wired into the brain: in prospect theory, the pain of a loss is about twice the pleasure of a gain; in the disposition effect, people naturally want to sell winners and hold losers. The good news: these biases can be hedged by "rules"—regular investing, rebalancing, indexing, written-in-stone plans are essentially replacing emotion with system. This chapter breaks down twenty of the deadliest biases one by one, each with real-study numbers, sources, and actionable countermeasures.

The right way to read this chapter: first claim how many you've fallen for (almost everyone has), then pick the 2–3 most expensive to fix. The A-grade evidence (frequent trading, timing, disposition effect, loss aversion, overconfidence) are "certain loss sources" already proven by tens of thousands of real accounts, highest priority; the C-grade are mostly robust in the lab but hard to quantify alone in the market, also worth guarding against. All entries share one underlying conclusion: you think you're "making decisions," but mostly your instincts "decide" for you.

These 20 entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses):
- Frequent trading and overconfidence: Entry 1 (frequent trading), Entry 6 (overconfidence), Entry 14 (illusion of control)
- Timing and myopia: Entry 2 (timing), Entry 18 (hyperbolic discounting / present bias), Entry 12 (recency bias)
- Loss psychology: Entry 4 (loss aversion), Entry 8 (disposition effect), Entry 11 (regret aversion)
- Crowd and emotional contagion: Entry 3 (chasing rallies and selling panic), Entry 9 (herding), Entry 17 (social comparison / envy)
- Information and judgment biases: Entry 5 (confirmation bias), Entry 15 (gambler's fallacy), Entry 19 (ambiguity aversion), Entry 20 (narrative fallacy), Entry 16 (survivorship bias)
- Mental valuation biases: Entry 7 (anchoring), Entry 10 (endowment effect), Entry 13 (mental accounting)

### 1. Frequent trading: the number-one internal cause of retail losses
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Trading commissions, stamp duty, bid-ask spreads, plus the impulse cost of "itchy hands wanting to act."
- In plain terms: Barber and Odean tracked 66,465 real accounts at a large U.S. discount broker from 1991–1996. The conclusion is cold: the more you trade, the worse you do. You think you're "seizing opportunities"; the data says you're "bleeding steadily." The most active group's annualized net return was only 11.4%, while the market returned 17.9% over the same period—trading alone ate 6.5 percentage points.
- Payoff: Barber & Odean (2000), using 66,465 U.S. household accounts (1991–1996), show the average account's annualized net return was 16.4% with a 75% annual turnover; the top quintile by trading frequency, after costs, returned only 11.4% annualized, 6.5 points below the market's 17.9%, and its gross return of about 18.7% was almost entirely eaten by trading costs and stock-picking mistakes. In other words, the more you act, the more you lose, and the loss comes entirely from the "action" itself, not the market. Compounding the 11.4% vs 17.9% gap over 10 years, the most active end up with about 40% less wealth than those who sat still.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2000). "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth: The Common Stock Investment Performance of Individual Investors." Journal of Finance, 55(2), 773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00226>; Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2001). "Boys Will Be Boys: Gender, Overconfidence, and Common Stock Investment." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 116(1), 261–292. <https://doi.org/10.1162/003355301556400>
- Note: The sample is U.S. discount-broker clients, concentrated in the 1990s, but the conclusion has been replicated in Finland, Taiwan, China, and other markets. Graded A for the huge sample, account-level real trades, and extensive replication. Countermeasure: see Entry 18 (hyperbolic discounting) and "regular investing to beat timing." Cross-reference: Entry 6 (overconfidence is the root driver of frequent trading).

### 2. Timing: beating the market consistently is nearly impossible
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The time and anxiety of calling tops and bottoms, plus the rallies you miss by "waiting empty."
- In plain terms: The most expensive miss is being absent on the market's best days. The cost of timing is that while "dodging the drop" you almost certainly "miss the rise." DALBAR has tracked real U.S. mutual-fund-investor behavior for 30 consecutive years with one consistent conclusion: ordinary people lag the index every year through timing.
- Payoff: DALBAR's 2025 Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB) shows that in 2024 the ordinary equity-fund investor returned only 16.54% annualized, while the S&P 500 rose 25.02%, a gap of 848 basis points (8.48 percentage points), the second worst in over a decade; and ordinary equity investors have lagged the S&P 500 for 15 consecutive years. The longer-run "behavior gap" is also stark: SPIVA data shows that as of year-end 2024, over the 10-year period 84.3% of large-cap actively managed funds lagged the S&P 500, and over 15 years "not one of 22 equity categories had a majority of actively managed funds win." The double failure of timing and stock-picking is the main cause of the behavior gap.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: DALBAR (2025). "2025 Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB)." <https://www.dalbar.com/press-release/investors-missed-the-best-of-2024s-market-gains-latest-dalbar-investor-behavior-report-finds>; S&P Dow Jones Indices (2025). "SPIVA U.S. Scorecard Year-End 2024." <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-us-year-end-2024.pdf>
- Note: QAIB infers investors' actual realized returns from "fund flows in and out," naturally capturing timing errors; SPIVA measures actively managed funds and includes survivorship-bias correction. Graded A for data spanning over 30 years and covering all market mutual funds. Countermeasure: abandon timing, mechanize "buying" with regular fixed-amount investing. Cross-reference: Entry 1 (frequent trading), Entry 18 (hyperbolic discounting).

### 3. Chasing rallies and selling panic: trading at the weakest point of human nature
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The high price of chasing highs + the low price of panic-selling, wearing on both sides.
- In plain terms: You fear missing the rise, fear worse on the drop, so you rush in at highs and cut at lows. Barber & Odean (2008) used broker data to prove retail net-buys most the stocks that just surged and are hottest in the media—translating "attention" directly into losses. Doing the reverse earns positive returns, but that's against human nature, so rely on a plan, not feelings.
- Payoff: Barber & Odean (2008) found both individuals and institutions significantly "chase the rise": the stocks investors net-buy are exactly those with the highest gain the prior week/month and the most news exposure; this attention-driven buying subsequently significantly lags the stocks they sold. In China, SSE statistics on tens of millions of accounts also show retail pouring in en masse at the bull-market peak and panic-selling at bear-market lows, with net losses far above institutions across the cycle. Returning "buy high, sell low" to the market and switching to disciplined "buy low, sell high" is one of the few actions that steadily contributes positive returns.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2008). "All That Glitters: The Effect of Attention and News on the Buying Behavior of Individual and Institutional Investors." Review of Financial Studies, 21(2), 785–818. <https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm079>; Shanghai Stock Exchange (2020). Shanghai Stock Exchange Statistical Yearbook. <https://www.sse.com.cn/>
- Note: SSE data is cited in detail elsewhere in this book (the 53.4-million-account retail study), cross-referenced here. Graded B: the "attention hypothesis" of chasing rises has strong account evidence, but "panic-selling" in the A-share sample is hard to fully separate from timing errors. Countermeasure: regular investing + rebalancing, decoupling buying/selling from emotion. Cross-reference: Entry 9 (herding), Entry 17 (social comparison).

### 4. Loss aversion: the pain of losing ≈ twice the joy of gaining
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The discomfort of admitting "cutting losses," plus the opportunity cost paid to avoid that discomfort.
- In plain terms: The pain of losing 100 is about the joy of gaining 200. So you won't sell a loss, and rush to sell a gain—this instinct is a negative asset in investing and must be held down by rules. Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory quantified this: the value function is steeper in the loss domain.
- Payoff: Kahneman & Tversky (1979) prospect theory gives a loss-aversion coefficient λ ≈ 2.0–2.5: the negative utility of losing 100 yuan is about equal to the positive utility of gaining 200–250 yuan, a conclusion replicated across dozens of countries and experiments. In investing it directly creates two loss-making actions—holding losing positions and selling winners too early—and amplifies "myopic loss aversion": Benartzi & Thaler (1995) proved that the more frequently you check your account (daily vs yearly), the more you perceive stocks as risky and the less you can hold, demanding an excessive equity risk premium. Loss aversion plus frequent checking is a main reason for long-term underweighting of stocks and missing compounding.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk." Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185>; Benartzi, S. & Thaler, R. H. (1995). "Myopic Loss Aversion and the Equity Premium Puzzle." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 110(1), 73–92. <https://doi.org/10.2307/2118511>
- Note: λ ranges 1.5 to 3.0 across studies but is always significantly above 1. Graded A: prospect theory is among the most-cited papers in economics history and backed by multiple field experiments. Countermeasure: reduce checking frequency, replace discretionary stop-loss/stop-profit with regular investing and rebalancing. Cross-reference: Entry 8 (disposition effect), Entry 11 (regret aversion).

### 5. Confirmation bias and "acting on tips"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of effort to actively seek counterexamples and falsify your own views.
- In plain terms: When you like a stock, you automatically collect the good news and ignore the bad. The more "inside" and urgent a tip, the more you should suspect the reverse—real steady-winning info never reaches a free group chat. The SEC has repeatedly warned: "hot stock picks" on social media often accompany pump-and-dump scams.
- Payoff: Nickerson (1998) systematically reviewed confirmation bias: people actively seek, interpret, and recall information supporting existing beliefs while ignoring disconfirming evidence; in investing this shows as "only believe the bullish, avoid the bearish," thus adding to overvalued positions and clinging after the thesis is falsified. The SEC investor alert notes that "hot stock" recommendations on social media are often used by manipulators to create buying frenzy and dump after pumping; retail who take over are deep-trapped; so-called "inside info" is almost certainly a scam or old news already public for years. Escaping confirmation bias at least avoids the one-time harvest of "inside info" in groups and big-V calls.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Nickerson, R. S. (1998). "Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises." Review of General Psychology, 2(2), 175–220. <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175>; U.S. SEC (2021). "Investor Alert: Thinking About Investing in the Latest Hot Stock?" <https://www.sec.gov/resources-for-investors/investor-alerts-bulletins/thinking-about-investing-latest-hot-stock>
- Note: Confirmation bias itself is a robust psychological conclusion (graded B because the investing scenario is mostly inference rather than a single large-scale empirical study). Countermeasure: force-write a "bear-case list," use only verifiable public financial-report data. Cross-reference: Entry 20 (narrative fallacy).

### 6. Overconfidence: the more accounts you open and trade, the more you think you can win
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of self-doubt that "I might not be that divine."
- In plain terms: In a bull market everyone's a stock god; you can't tell until the tide recedes. Treating luck as skill is the starting point of the next loss. Barber & Odean exposed it by gender: men trade more and earn less, the gap exactly from higher overconfidence.
- Payoff: Barber & Odean (2001), using over 35,000 accounts (1991–1997), found men trade 45% more frequently than women; and trading's damage to men's annualized net return reaches 2.65 percentage points vs 1.72 for women—men lose about 0.94 points/year more due to greater overconfidence. The psychological basis: people generally overestimate their ability to predict and pick stocks (about 90% of drivers think they're above average). Shifting energy from "picking the next multi-bagger" to controllable things like "cost and position control" is the rational choice.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2001). "Boys Will Be Boys: Gender, Overconfidence, and Common Stock Investment." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 116(1), 261–292. <https://doi.org/10.1162/003355301556400>; Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2000). "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth..." Journal of Finance, 55(2), 773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00226>
- Note: Female samples also overtrade, just less; the conclusion is not "gender superiority" but "overconfidence gradient." Graded B: strong account evidence, but gender conclusions have cultural and generational discussions. Countermeasure: assume you can't beat the index, just buy broad-market. Cross-reference: Entry 1 (frequent trading).

### 7. Anchoring: pinned down by the "purchase price"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Letting go of the obsession "I'll only sell once I break even."
- In plain terms: "I bought at 10, selling at 7 now is a loss"—wrong. At 7 its value is 7, unrelated to 10. Decide only on the future, not sunk cost. Tversky and Kahneman's anchoring experiments show people get pinned by the first number they meet.
- Payoff: Tversky & Kahneman (1974) proposed the anchoring heuristic in Science: when estimating, people are systematically pulled by an irrelevant initial number. In investing, the purchase price, historical high, and "break-even line" all become anchors—investors refuse to sell stocks with no fundamentals left "because they haven't broken even," or cash out too early "because they've already profited." Behavioral finance further finds anchoring makes investors decide on paper gains/losses rather than the portfolio's future risk, causing lagged rebalancing and concentrated trapped holdings. The way to break anchoring: every decision ask "at today's price, would I still buy?"
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1974). "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124>; Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory..." Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185>
- Note: Anchoring is extremely robust in the lab, but in real accounts it often stacks with other biases (loss aversion, disposition effect) and is hard to quantify alone, hence graded C. Countermeasure: decide using valuation and fundamentals instead of cost basis. Cross-reference: Entry 8 (disposition effect), Entry 10 (endowment effect).

### 8. Disposition effect: sell winners, hold losers
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The discipline (against instinct) to stop-loss/stop-profit by logic.
- In plain terms: People love selling winners (lock in gains) and holding losers (wait to break even). The result: your portfolio is full of trapped holdings. The right move is often the reverse: let winners run, admit mistakes and cut losers. Odean quantified this with 10,000 accounts, crystal clear.
- Payoff: Odean (1998) analyzed 10,000 accounts at a large discount broker from 1987–1993, defining "proportion of gains realized (PGR)" and "proportion of losses realized (PLR)": full-year PGR = 14.8% while PLR = 9.8%—winners were sold about 50% more often than losers (except December tax-loss selling). More fatal: the sold winners on average rose 3.4% more than the held losers in the following year, showing "sell winners, hold losers" is not tax or rebalancing, purely cutting good tickets and keeping bad. The portfolio thus keeps deteriorating: cut the risers, keep the fallers, quality must decline long term.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Odean, T. (1998). "Are Investors Reluctant to Realize Their Losses? An Analysis of Daily Equity Transactions." Journal of Finance, 53(5), 1775–1798. <https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00072>; Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory..." Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185>
- Note: Originally graded B, but this study is based on 10,000 real accounts and replicated in Finland/Taiwan/China markets, upgraded to A. Loss aversion (prospect theory) is its main explanation. Countermeasure: use rebalancing rules to force-sell some winners and stop-loss some losers. Cross-reference: Entry 4 (loss aversion), Entry 11 (regret aversion).

### 9. Herding: others buy so I buy
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: A bit of courage for independent judgment.
- In plain terms: When the cleaning lady asks which stock to buy, it's often the most dangerous time. The crowd isn't wisdom, it's the prelude to a stampede. Information-cascade theory shows: others buy so I buy, pushing price further from fundamentals.
- Payoff: Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992)'s "informational cascade" model proves that when latercomers only observe predecessors' actions and don't share private info, they rationally follow the crowd, eventually driving price far from true value and forming bubbles and stampedes. In A-shares, SSE statistics on tens of millions of accounts show retail pour in en masse at index highs and flee in panic at lows; at the bull peak new accounts and volume surge, then they take over on the pullback. The cost of following: systematically buying when others want to sell, selling when others want to buy.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Bikhchandani, S., Hirshleifer, D. & Welch, I. (1992). "A Theory of Fads, Fashion, Custom, and Cultural Change as Informational Cascades." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 107(4), 992–1026. <https://doi.org/10.2307/2118375>; Shanghai Stock Exchange (2020). Shanghai Stock Exchange Statistical Yearbook. <https://www.sse.com.cn/>
- Note: SSE retail data see cross-reference in other chapters (53.4-million-account study). Graded C: cascade theory is model deduction, A-share evidence is statistical correlation, hard to isolate causality. Countermeasure: treat "others are buying" as a reverse thermometer, not a signal. Cross-reference: Entry 3 (chasing rallies), Entry 17 (social comparison).

### 10. Endowment effect: what's in hand is especially hard to sell
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The coldness to objectively assess holdings.
- In plain terms: The same stock, meh before buying, a treasure after. That's psychological premium, not real value. Ask regularly: "if I were flat now, would I still buy it?"
- Payoff: Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler (1990) experimentally proved the endowment effect: people's asking price for something they "already own" is significantly higher than the buying price for the same unowned item (in the classic mug experiment the gap can be 2x). In investing, once bought, the asset is tagged "mine" in the mental account, so you refuse to stop-loss at market price, refuse to rebalance, and are especially lenient toward losing positions. The result is excessive single-stock concentration and unnoticed portfolio risk drift.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Kahneman, D., Knetsch, J. L. & Thaler, R. H. (1990). "Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem." Journal of Political Economy, 98(6), 1325–1348. <https://doi.org/10.1086/261737>; Thaler, R. H. (1980). "Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice." Marketing Science, 4(3), 199–214. <https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.4.3.199>
- Note: Original entry cited Thaler (1980); here completed with the 1990 classic experiment and kept 1980 as the intellectual source. Graded C: extremely robust in the lab, but investing scenarios are mostly inference. Countermeasure: assume you're an "empty-position observer" and re-evaluate each holding. Cross-reference: Entry 13 (mental accounting).

### 11. Regret aversion: paralysis from fear of regret
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The psychological cost of admitting "I bought expensive before."
- In plain terms: Many won't sell because they fear "it'll rise right after I sell, and I'll regret more." But the cost of holding on is real; regret is just emotion. Let rules decide for you, take yourself out.
- Payoff: Loomes & Sugden (1982)'s "regret theory" shows people pre-compute "if the outcome is bad, how much will I regret" and thus avoid options that may cause regret—in investing this shows as: rather hold bad tickets than stop-loss (fear "it'll rise after I sell"), rather chase highs than miss out (fear "not on board"). This asymmetric fear of "action regret" vs "inaction regret" keeps investors long-term off the optimal portfolio. Handing selling/buying to set rules (e.g., rebalancing thresholds, regular-investing calendar) effectively removes "regret" from the decision loop.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Loomes, G. & Sugden, R. (1982). "Regret Theory: An Alternative Theory of Rational Choice Under Uncertainty." Economic Journal, 92(368), 805–824. <https://doi.org/10.2307/2232669>; Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory..." Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185>
- Note: Regret aversion heavily overlaps with loss aversion and disposition effect, often called the psychological basis of the "disposition effect." Graded C: theory mature but hard to quantify alone in investing. Countermeasure: write down trading rules, use automated execution instead of on-the-spot judgment. Cross-reference: Entry 4, Entry 8.

### 12. Recency bias: only seeing the latest moves
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of patience to stretch the time window.
- In plain terms: The brain overweights recent events. Yesterday's crash feels like doom, last week's rally feels forever. Stretch the window to a year or even a decade and the noise shrinks.
- Payoff: Tversky & Kahneman (1974) point out that when judging trends people are dominated by the "most recently available" sample (a form of representativeness heuristic), over-weighting recent big rises/falls and treating noise as signal. Benartzi & Thaler (1995) further prove that the more frequently investors evaluate their portfolio (daily vs yearly), the more easily scared off by short-term swings and the higher the risk compensation they demand—this is "myopic loss aversion." Recency bias makes you panic-sell after a crash and impulse-chase after a rally, exactly executing "sell low, buy high."
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1974). "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124>; Benartzi, S. & Thaler, R. H. (1995). "Myopic Loss Aversion and the Equity Premium Puzzle." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 110(1), 73–92. <https://doi.org/10.2307/2118511>
- Note: Same source as Entries 4, 18; graded C: robust in lab, hard to quantify alone in market. Countermeasure: lower checking frequency, use long-term perspective and regular investing to smooth short-term noise. Cross-reference: Entry 18.

### 13. Mental accounting: splitting money into different "jars"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The intuition of separating "investment losses" from "food money" in accounting.
- In plain terms: You dare eat a big meal after 10k stock gains, but cling to a 10k fund loss without selling—it's the same money. Break the illusion of "this is travel money, that is pension money" and decisions turn clear.
- Payoff: Thaler (1985, 1999) mental accounting theory points out people split money into different "jars" by source and use, applying different risk attitudes to different jars—thus gambling with "won money," over-conservative on the "principal account," clinging to the "loss account." In investing this causes: (1) no consolidation across accounts, imbalanced overall allocation; (2) treating the floating-loss account as "not yet lost" and refusing rebalancing; (3) using after-tax gains to subsidize consumption, eroding compounding. Money has no labels; the correct framework is a unified view of total assets and risk budget.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Thaler, R. H. (1985). "Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice." Marketing Science, 4(3), 199–214. <https://doi.org/10.1287/mksc.4.3.199>; Thaler, R. H. (1999). "Mental Accounting Matters." Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 12(3), 183–206. <https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0771(199909)12:3<183::AID-BDM318>3.0.CO;2-F>
- Note: Thaler won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Economics for behavioral economics, with mental accounting a core contribution. Graded C: solid theory, but market losses from "account mismatch" are hard to isolate. Countermeasure: build a unified total-asset view and global rebalancing discipline. Cross-reference: Entry 10 (endowment effect).

### 14. Illusion of control: thinking you can influence random outcomes
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Believing the illusion "my timing is sharper / my picks are better."
- In plain terms: Checking daily and trading often makes you "feel in control," but the market doesn't honor your diligence. The more you think you control, the more easily you're harvested by fees and emotion. Langer's experiment long proved: people feel control over "random outcomes unrelated to them."
- Payoff: Langer (1975)'s "illusion of control" experiment shows that even when outcomes are purely random, people overestimate their control because "they participated in the choice/operation" (e.g., those who pick their own lottery numbers are more reluctant to sell). In investing this shows as: timing daily, frequent rebalancing, addicted to "the feel of operating," mistaking random fluctuation for a signal they can master. Massive account studies (see Entry 1, Barber & Odean 2000) show investors' "sense of control" is unrelated to real returns, but by raising trade frequency it pushes up costs and amplifies emotional loss, significantly dragging long-term returns.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Langer, E. J. (1975). "The Illusion of Control." Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 32(2), 311–328. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0076498>; Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2000). "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth..." Journal of Finance, 55(2), 773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00226>
- Note: Illusion of control is the psychological fuel of frequent trading (Entry 1). Graded C: robust experiment, market-level resonates with trading-frequency research. Countermeasure: admit the market is uncontrollable short term, use indexing and regular investing to minimize "need for control." Cross-reference: Entry 1.

### 15. Gambler's fallacy: "after consecutive falls it must rise"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Believing "it's fallen so much, a rebound is due."
- In plain terms: "Five down days, the sixth must rise"—that's gambler logic, not investing logic. A thoroughly fallen stock can keep falling; don't pep yourself up with "should rebound."
- Payoff: Tversky & Kahneman (1971)'s "law of small numbers" and representativeness heuristic reveal: people wrongly believe small samples should "look like" large samples, thus believe after consecutive falls "should rise," after consecutive rises "should fall." But stock prices are approximately random walk short term, past moves have no predictive power. Experiments and field data consistently show investors holding the gambler's fallacy tend to "bottom-fish" junk stocks in declines and fear-sell after consecutive rises, losing on both sides. The rational approach: treat each decision as independent, judge by valuation and fundamentals not "number of consecutive falls."
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1971). "Belief in the Law of Small Numbers." Psychological Bulletin, 76(2), 105–110. <https://doi.org/10.1037/h0031322>; Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D. (1974). "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases." Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131. <https://doi.org/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124>
- Note: Same source as Entry 12 (recency bias); graded C. Countermeasure: abandon "bottom-fishing counting," use batch regular investing and valuation thresholds instead of intuition. Cross-reference: Entry 12.

### 16. Survivorship bias: only seeing those who survived / made money
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Being led by stories of "who got financially free by trading."
- In plain terms: Many brag about trading profits at dinner; those who jumped or closed accounts stay silent. Deciding by "I know someone who made it" equals looking only at the winner list.
- Payoff: Malkiel (1995) used 1971–1991 "all" U.S. equity mutual fund data to prove survivorship bias severely inflates fund performance: counting only surviving funds, annualized return was about 17.1%, but including the liquidated/merged "losers," all funds annualized only 15.7%, and average alpha not significantly different from zero—much of the "funds can beat" is manufactured by "dead funds not speaking." Same in investing: the "trading-rich" samples you see are systematically contaminated by silent losers; success rates estimated from them far exceed the true value, and deciding on them inevitably overestimates your own win rate.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Malkiel, B. G. (1995). "Returns from Investing in Equity Mutual Funds 1971 to 1991." Journal of Finance, 50(2), 549–572. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1995.tb04795.x>; Thaler, R. H. (1999). "Mental Accounting Matters." Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 12(3), 183–206. <https://doi.org/10.1002/(SICI)1099-0771(199909)12:3<183::AID-BDM318>3.0.CO;2-F>
- Note: Classic case is WWII Wald's statistical correction of returning-plane bullet distributions (see sampling-bias literature). Graded C: strong financial empirics, but as a "cognitive bias" entry its investment loss is hard to measure alone. Countermeasure: look only at statistics including all samples, don't trust isolated cases. Cross-reference: Entry 5.

### 17. Social comparison / envy: I feel bad when others earn
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Being stimulated by friends' / group's returns, randomly adding positions.
- In plain terms: Others show gains and you're anxious, so you rush in at the high—exactly taking over their position. Others' accounts are none of your business; watch your own plan.
- Payoff: Behavioral finance treats "social preference and comparison" as the emotional fuel of chasing rallies: when peers earn more than you, investors feel relative deprivation, then raise risk-taking and add at highs to "catch up" (Bikhchandani et al.'s information cascade and attention effects also support this). Empirically, Barber & Odean (2008) show retail net-buys concentrate on "everyone's-talking-about" hot stocks, and this attention-driven buying lags long term. Social platforms amplify this comparison, making people most anxious and impulsive at the market top.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Barber, B. M. & Odean, T. (2008). "All That Glitters..." Review of Financial Studies, 21(2), 785–818. <https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhm079>; Bikhchandani, S., Hirshleifer, D. & Welch, I. (1992). "A Theory of Fads... as Informational Cascades." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 107(4), 992–1026. <https://doi.org/10.2307/2118375>
- Note: Same source as Entries 3, 9. Graded C: emotional mechanism has experimental and account evidence, but the "envy → loss" causal chain is inferential. Countermeasure: don't look at return leaderboards, mute showing-off groups, only keep your own rebalancing plan. Cross-reference: Entry 9.

### 18. Hyperbolic discounting / present bias: a small reward now beats a big reward later
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Preferring "money I can spend now / trades that feel good now," undervaluing long-term compounding.
- In plain terms: People naturally overvalue the present and undervalue the future. That's why most can't hold—compounding is a "future big prize," but the small present swing grabs more. Use rules to lock in the long term for yourself.
- Payoff: Laibson (1997)'s "hyperbolic discounting" model shows people assign disproportionate weight to near rewards and rapidly devalue far rewards, explaining why investors struggle to persist with regular investing and long holding. Frederick, Loewenstein & O'Donoghue (2002) review shows this "present bias" makes people check accounts frequently and get hijacked by short-term swings (see Entry 4 myopic loss aversion), thus chasing in at highs from "fear of missing" and cutting at lows from "fear of worse." Making long holding a "not-uncomfortable" default—automatic regular investing, less checking—is the cheapest weapon against human shortsightedness.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Laibson, D. (1997). "Golden Eggs and Hyperbolic Discounting." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 112(2), 443–477. <https://doi.org/10.1162/j.1997.112.2.443>; Frederick, S., Loewenstein, G. & O'Donoghue, T. (2002). "Time Discounting and Time Preference: A Critical Review." Journal of Economic Literature, 40(2), 351–401. <https://doi.org/10.1257/002205102320161311>
- Note: This entry directly corresponds to "regular investing to beat timing"—regular investing mechanizes buying, dropping the instant-decision cost to zero. Graded C: model and experiment robust, long-term return improvement is inferential but direction clear. Cross-reference: Entries 1, 2, 4.

### 19. Ambiguity aversion: avoiding what you don't understand, chasing what's familiar
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Only buying "heard names / trending concepts."
- In plain terms: You dare buy familiar baijiu stocks, not unfamiliar medical ETFs—but "familiar" is often the reverse of information edge. Using broad-market index instead of "I feel familiar" is actually steadier.
- Payoff: Ellsberg (1961) with the "Ellsberg paradox" proved people systematically prefer "risk with known probability" over "uncertainty with unknown probability" (ambiguity aversion), even when expectations are equal. In investing this shows as "home bias" and "familiar-stock bias": investors overweight stocks and industries they've heard of and that people around them talk about, avoiding unfamiliar but more diversified and cheaper index products. The result is excessive single-stock and single-industry concentration, unknowingly taking non-systematic risk, while covering the whole market with a broad-market index trades "giving up ambiguity judgment" for steadier exposure.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Ellsberg, D. (1961). "Risk, Ambiguity, and the Savage Axioms." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 75(4), 643–669. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1926465>; Kahneman, D. & Tversky, A. (1979). "Prospect Theory..." Econometrica, 47(2), 263–291. <https://doi.org/10.2307/1914185>
- Note: Highly related to "home bias" and "over-concentration." Graded C: extremely robust experiment, market loss mostly inferential. Countermeasure: use broad-market index instead of single-stock bets on "I feel familiar." Cross-reference: Entry 13.

### 20. Narrative fallacy: a good story beats bad data
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Being moved by good stories like "a track changing the world" or "ten-bagger-in-ten-years logic."
- In plain terms: Good stories make people ignore expensive valuations and high risk. The better the story, the more you should ask: what does it actually earn from? If it can't be explained, it's a gamble.
- Payoff: Shiller (2017, Nobel laureate) proposed "narrative economics": good stories that "go viral" drive asset-price bubbles (1929 and 2008 both accompanied popular narratives). Taleb (2007)'s "narrative fallacy" points out people habitually explain random results with simple causal stories, thus treating "track narrative" as a moat, ignoring valuation and cash flow. Empirically, assets pumped by stories often have overstretched valuations and brutal drawdowns; investors obsessed with narrative take over at highs, ignoring the hard questions of "where does the money come from, what's the valuation, what can I verify."
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Shiller, R. J. (2017). "Narrative Economics." American Economic Review, 107(4), 967–1004. <https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.107.4.967>; Taleb, N. N. (2007). "The Black Swan: The Impact of the Highly Improbable." Random House. <https://www.amazon.com/Black-Swan-Improbable-Robustness-Fragility/dp/1400063515>
- Note: Narrative often synergizes with Entry 5 (confirmation bias) and Entry 3 (chasing rallies) to amplify bubbles. Graded C: narrative's causal effect on price is hard to quantify precisely. Countermeasure: before any buy, write clear "profit model + valuation anchor + falsifiable condition." Cross-reference: Entry 5.
