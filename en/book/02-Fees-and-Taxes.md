# II. Fees and Taxes

Fees and taxes are the only deductions in investing you can calculate 100% before you even place an order. You can't predict market swings, but the stamp-duty rate, fund management fee rate, subscription/redemption fees, and marginal income-tax rate are fixed numbers written into law and contracts. This chapter tallies a "certainty account": every basis point you save stays in your pocket forever and compounds; every basis point you overpay is a sure sum handed first to the tax office, the broker, and the fund company. Keeping this line as low as possible beats predicting which fund will rise next year—because it doesn't depend on luck, only on whether you're willing to read the contract and the statute.

On the evidence front, the negative link between fees and net returns is repeatedly verified worldwide: in its 2026 testimony to the U.S. Congress, Morningstar stated plainly that cost is "the most reliable predictor of future fund performance," with the lowest-fee quintile funds beating the highest-fee quintile; Carhart (1997) and long-run SPIVA data likewise show that each notch of higher fees makes it harder for actively managed funds to beat their benchmark. The tax side is entirely policy-determined—China's A-share stamp duty was halved from 0.1% to 0.05% (one-sided) in August 2023, the differentiated dividend tax makes holdings over 1 year tax-free, and the private pension account defers tax and is taxed at only 3% on withdrawal. All figures in this chapter are labeled with year, country, and the original statute so you can verify them; estimates are flagged as such.

The entries below are grouped by theme (entry numbers in parentheses) for easy reference.

### 1. Fund fees are the most certain "negative return"
<!-- tags: money=little time=0 will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Annually deducted management fee + custodian fee. Actively managed equity funds commonly charge 1.0%–1.5%/year, index funds about 0.1%–0.5%/year, and broad-market ETFs can be as low as 0.03%–0.15%/year.
- In plain terms: A fee is a "negative return" you know with 100% certainty before buying. You can't predict market swings, but the fee is written into the contract. Cut it to the bone first—that's far more reliable than researching which fund is great.
- Payoff: Fees are significantly negatively correlated with future net performance, and it's one of the few variables you can know in advance. Morningstar's 2026 congressional testimony states verbatim that "cost is the most reliable predictor of future fund performance," with the lowest-fee quintile funds' success rate far above the highest-fee quintile. Compounding example: at a 7% annual gross return assumption, a 1% annual fee eats about 25% of the final value over 30 years, and a 2% fee eats about 43%; China's actively managed funds average about 1.2%–1.5% in fees vs index funds' 0.1%–0.5%, and just the fee gap alone opens a final-value gap of over 20% over 20 years. Every basis point saved stays in your account and compounds.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Morningstar (2026). Morningstar to Congress: Investor Success Hinges on Lower Costs, Greater Transparency (testimony: "cost is the most reliable predictor"). <https://www.morningstar.com/funds/morningstar-congress-investor-success-hinges-lower-costs-greater-transparency>; Carhart, M. (1997). On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance, Journal of Finance 52(1):57–82. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03808.x>; Morningstar (2026). 2026 US Fund Fee Study (asset-weighted average fee fell to 0.32% in 2025). <https://www.morningstar.com/funds/morningstar-congress-investor-success-hinges-lower-costs-greater-transparency>
- Note: Limitation: low fees don't guarantee high returns, they only raise the win rate under the same strategy; active management can create excess returns in some inefficient markets (e.g., fixed income, emerging markets). Cross-reference: Entries 5, 10, 13.

### 2. Commission, stamp duty, subscription/redemption fees: the compounding killer
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The explicit friction of every buy and sell. A-share stamp duty on the seller has been halved to 0.05% of transaction value (one-sided) since 2023-08-28; broker commissions are generally around 0.025% (2.5 bps) plus about 0.001% (0.1 bp) handling fee; ETFs are exempt from stamp duty and transfer fees.
- In plain terms: Trading a few times a month, friction eats 1%–3% a year, and over 20 years the cumulative gap is huge. The share of your money that goes to the broker and the tax office is proportional to your turnover.
- Payoff: Frequent trading is the "invisible pump" draining retail net returns. The classic Barber & Odean (2000) study found that the most frequently trading retail group significantly lagged the market and low-frequency traders in annualized net return. Take A-shares as an example: assuming a full-portfolio turnover once a month, one-way cost is about 0.05% stamp duty + 0.025% commission ≈ 0.075%, about 0.9% a year over 12 times, and with bid-ask spreads and impact costs it often actually reaches 1%–3%; if you cut friction from 1.5%/year to 0.1%, at 7% annualized over 30 years the final value gap can reach about 30%. ETFs, exempt from stamp duty and tradable on-exchange at low cost, are the direct tool to cut friction.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration (2023). Announcement on Halving the Securities Trading Stamp Duty (MFA-STA Announcement No. 39 of 2023, halved from August 28, 2023). <https://fgk.chinatax.gov.cn/zcfgk/c102416/c5211343/content.html>; Barber, B. & Odean, T. (2000). Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth, Journal of Finance 55(2):773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2000.tb03224.x>; State Taxation Administration (2023). Q&A on Recent Stamp Duty Hot Topics (one-sided 0.5‰ levied on the transferor). <https://shanghai.chinatax.gov.cn/gate/big5/shanghai.chinatax.gov.cn/zcfw/rdwd/202309/t468612.html>
- Note: Uncertainty: stamp duty is a policy variable and may be adjusted in the future; the ETF stamp-duty exemption follows current rules. Cross-reference: Entry 5 (ETFs exempt from stamp duty).

### 3. Put money into tax-advantaged / tax-exempt accounts first
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: A bit of account-opening and quota-planning effort; China's private pension has an annual limit of 12,000 yuan, and funds are generally locked until retirement (withdrawable only in a few special cases).
- In plain terms: Same annualized return, after-tax and pre-tax differ by a chunk. Tax you can legally defer is a free compounding accelerator; and the pension account being "troublesome to withdraw" conveniently keeps your hands off.
- Payoff: MFA-STA Announcement No. 34 of 2022 stipulates that the private pension allows a deduction of up to 12,000 yuan/year from comprehensive or business income when contributing, investment gains credited to the account are not taxed during the investment phase, and the withdrawal phase is taxed at a flat 3% rate (the original tax-deferred pension insurance effective burden dropped from 7.5% to 3%). For those in a marginal income-tax bracket of 10% or above, just the contribution deduction saves up to 1,200 yuan in income tax per year, while the "tax-free compounding" during the investment period amplifies with the years; the account's "long-money" nature also inversely suppresses frequent trading (see Entry 2). Announcement No. 21 of 2024 has extended this policy nationwide.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration (2022). Announcement on Individual Pension Personal Income Tax Policy (MFA-STA Announcement No. 34 of 2022, Article 1 tax deferral, 3% withdrawal rate). <https://sichuan.chinatax.gov.cn/art/2022/11/4/art_7757_794159.html>; Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration (2024). Announcement on Implementing the Individual Pension Personal Income Tax Preferential Policy Nationwide (No. 21 of 2024). <https://zhejiang.chinatax.gov.cn/art/2026/3/9/art_24107_650245.html>; IRS (2025). Topic No. 451, Individual Retirement Arrangements (IRAs) (U.S. traditional IRA/401(k) pre-tax contributions, tax-free investment phase, taxed as ordinary income on withdrawal; taxable accounts' long-term capital gains at 0%/15%/20%, see Topic No. 409). <https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc451>
- Note: Limitation: the quota is small (12,000 yuan/year) and withdrawals are locked, and the tax-saving effect scales with the marginal rate; the U.S. traditional IRA/401(k) works the same way as a reference, but taxable accounts' long-term capital gains are only 0%/15%/20% (IRS 2025, 20% above $533,400 single taxable income, plus 3.8% NIIT). Cross-reference: Entries 2, 4.

### 4. Reinvest dividends—don't take cash
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Tick one option (zero cost).
- In plain terms: A dividend is part of your assets; taking cash is auto-reducing your position and giving up future upside. Unless you live off the dividends, default to "dividend reinvestment."
- Payoff: Caishui [2015] No. 101 stipulates that for individuals receiving dividends from listed-company shares on the open market, dividends held over 1 year are temporarily exempt from individual income tax; holdings of more than 1 month to 1 year (inclusive) are counted at 50% of taxable income (i.e., effective 10% burden), and within 1 month (inclusive) are taxed in full at 20%. A-shares held long term are tax-free, so you should reinvest to keep compounding. On a 100,000 yuan position with a 3% annual dividend yield, if dividends are tax-free and fully reinvested into a portfolio returning 6% annualized, the tax-free dividends and their reinvestment can accumulate tens of thousands of yuan more over 10 years (estimate, depends on net-value growth). U.S. accounts' in-account DRIP likewise avoids the friction of frequent small trades (see Entry 2).
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Ministry of Finance, State Taxation Administration, CSRC (2015). Notice on Differentiated Personal Income Tax Policy for Listed-Company Dividends (Caishui [2015] No. 101, Article 1: over-1-year holding temporarily exempt). <https://www.chinatax.gov.cn/chinatax/n810341/n810765/n1465977/201510/c1967339/content.html>; State Taxation Administration 12366 (2024). Q&A on Dividend Personal Income Tax (differentiated rates apply). <https://12366.chinatax.gov.cn/nszx/onlinemessage/detail?id=30479c1fc89c4a419852b2f2da5e4f55>
- Note: Limitation: if you need cash flow for retirement, that's another matter; reinvestment returns are an estimate. Cross-reference: Entry 15 (dividends don't add value).

### 5. ETFs beat off-exchange subscription/redemption funds (under the same strategy)
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Need a securities account (one-time), then trade on-exchange.
- In plain terms: Buying the CSI 300 the same way, an on-exchange ETF's fee is often one-third to half of an off-exchange fund's, and it's exempt from stamp duty. What differs long term is your net return.
- Payoff: Broad-market ETF annual management + custodian fees are often 0.03%–0.5% (e.g., CSI 300 ETF about 0.15%/year), while actively managed off-exchange equity funds are about 1.0%–1.5%/year; and ETF on-exchange trading is exempt from stamp duty (see Entry 2), highly transparent, with small tracking error (mainstream broad-market ETFs' tracking error mostly 0.1%–0.5%/year, see Entry 14). On 1 million yuan over 20 years at 7% annualized, the final value gap between a 1.2% fee and a 0.15% fee is about 22% (the high-fee side gets only about 78% of the low-fee side's). Morningstar's 2026 data shows the U.S. asset-weighted average fee has fallen to 0.32%, and under the same strategy the ETF is the most direct tool to cut holding cost.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Morningstar (2026). 2026 US Fund Fee Study (asset-weighted average fee 0.32% in 2025, halved from 0.80% twenty years ago). <https://www.morningstar.com/funds/morningstar-congress-investor-success-hinges-lower-costs-greater-transparency>; China Securities Regulatory Commission (2014). Administrative Measures for the Operation and Management of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds (ETF operation and disclosure). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c106256/c1653978/content.shtml>; Shanghai Stock Exchange. ETF Business Implementation Rules (on-exchange trading, stamp-duty exemption mechanism). <https://www.sse.com.cn/>
- Note: Limitation: ETFs have premium/discount and tracking error (see Entry 14); off-exchange funds are more convenient for auto-deducted regular investing. Cross-reference: Entries 2, 14.

### 6. QDII / overseas assets: exchange-rate and quota costs
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Exchange-rate friction + long subscription/redemption time (T+several days or more) + premium when quota is tight.
- In plain terms: Buying a U.S.-stock fund isn't only for U.S.-stock gains, it also hedges RMB risk; but exchange rates move both ways—don't treat it as pure enhancement.
- Payoff: QDII fund subscription/redemption is mostly T+ business days (usually T+7 to T+10 confirmation), with higher capital occupation and opportunity cost than domestic ETFs; when quota is tight (e.g., 2020–2021 some QDII-ETFs had 5%–20% premium), on-exchange premiums appear, and buying at a premium means losing a notch up front. On exchange rates, RMB appreciation erodes overseas gains, depreciation amplifies them—not a free lunch, but two-way exposure. Moderate allocation diversifies single-currency/single-economy risk, but you should ignore short-term exchange-rate swings, hold long term, and avoid turning "exchange-rate timing" into another layer of friction.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: State Administration of Foreign Exchange. QDII quota approval and overseas investment rules (premium mechanism when quota tight). <https://www.safe.gov.cn/>; People's Bank of China. RMB exchange-rate risk research (two-way risk). <http://www.pbc.gov.cn/>; Morningstar. QDII fund premium/discount cases (2020–2021, some products 5%–20% premium when quota tight). <https://www.morningstar.com/>
- Note: Controversy: the allocation value is real, but timing exchange rates is pure gambling; buying at a premium is avoidable explicit loss. Cross-reference: Entry 14 (premium/discount).

### 7. Hidden fees are more expensive than explicit fees
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of effort reading the "Fees" section of the contract / prospectus.
- In plain terms: You can see the explicit management fee; it's the hidden "various service fees" that bite. Every fine-print line in the fee table is your future negative return.
- Payoff: The SEC investor bulletin points out that fund fees, beyond management fees, also include 12b-1 (distribution/service fee; FINRA caps distribution at 0.75%/year and service at 0.25%/year), redemption fees (SEC cap 2%), account fees, etc.; among these, 12b-1 and service fees are deducted straight from net value and are hard for investors to notice. Robo-advisors stack another layer of advisory fee (often 0.25%–0.50%/year). Once these hidden layers stack up, a fund labeled 0.5% can actually cost over 1% to hold. Reading the fee table line by line is the prerequisite to avoiding hidden skimming.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: SEC (investor bulletin). Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses (12b-1, redemption fees, account fee disclosure). <https://sec.gov/answers/mfcal.htm>; SEC (PDF). Mutual Fund Fees and Expenses – Investor Bulletin (FINRA caps 12b-1 distribution at 0.75%, service at 0.25%). <https://www.investor.gov/sites/default/files/ib_mutualfundfees.pdf>; China Securities Regulatory Commission. Fund Information Disclosure Compilation Rules (fee listing requirements). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 10, 11, 13 (true fee = sum of all items).

### 8. Hidden erosion from fund switching and redemption fees
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Redemption fee + new subscription fee charged when frequently "switching" between funds.
- In plain terms: Many platforms package "switching" as zero-cost, but it may actually charge a redemption fee + a new subscription fee. See the total cost clearly before you move.
- Payoff: Some funds' "switch" is actually "redeem first, then subscribe," triggering a redemption fee (SEC cap 2%, domestically mostly within 0.5% and higher for short holding) and a new-share subscription fee; and frequent switching is often driven by short-term rankings, a case of behavioral finance's "chasing performance"—SPIVA shows most actively managed funds lag their benchmark long term, and bouncing around pays fees and times wrongly. Lowering the cost of "voting with your feet" keeps you from being lured by rankings into repeated bouncing and preserves compounding continuity.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2014). Administrative Measures for the Operation and Management of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds (redemption/switch fee rules). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c106256/c1653978/content.shtml>; SEC (investor bulletin). Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses (redemption fee cap 2%). <https://sec.gov/answers/mfcal.htm>; S&P Dow Jones Indices. SPIVA Scorecard (actively managed funds lag benchmark long term). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research-insights/spiva/>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 2, 13 (redemption fee and sales service fee).

### 9. Beware the sales-driven traps in the "fund supermarket"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of independent judgment, don't blindly trust the platform's "hot sale / recommended" labels.
- In plain terms: A platform's "recommended for you" is first motivated by its own commission, not your return. The sorting power is in your own hands.
- Payoff: The SEC notes conflicts of interest in sales channels: recommended slots are often driven by trailing commissions/distribution fees (see Entry 13), not investor interest. Domestic fund distributors are bound by the suitability constraints of the Administrative Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Fund Sales Institutions, but "best-seller lists" and "blockbusters" are still mostly products of traffic and commission. Products pushed in front of you tend to have higher fees or style drift. Build the habit of "check the fee table first, then the performance," avoiding sales-driven traps—zero cost but potentially saving 0.5%–1% a year in hidden concessions.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: SEC (investor bulletin). Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses (12b-1 and sales-incentive conflicts). <https://sec.gov/answers/mfcal.htm>; China Securities Regulatory Commission. Administrative Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Fund Sales Institutions (suitability and sales norms). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/>; Morningstar. Fee and sales-incentive research (recommended slots and fee structure). <https://www.morningstar.com/>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 7, 11, 13 (hidden fees and trailing commissions).

### 10. The true fee rate is the sum of "management + custodian + sales service fee"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: A glance at the "Fees" section of the prospectus.
- In plain terms: The fee table has three fine-print lines, and only their sum is what you actually pay each year. Don't just look at the eye-catching "0.15% management fee"—there may be a hidden sales service fee behind it.
- Payoff: True holding cost = management fee + custodian fee + sales service fee (Class C commonly 0.2%–0.8%/year) + other fees, i.e., the "total expense ratio." Morningstar research: the lowest-fee quartile's success rate is far above the highest-fee quartile (e.g., global large-cap group 60% vs 23%). Compounding example: a 1% annual fee at 7% annualized over 30 years eats about 25% of final value, 2% eats about 43%; a 1% fee gap ≈ nearly a quarter of final value. Seeing the sum of the three fine-print lines clearly is the first step to cutting cost, and the concrete action behind Morningstar's so-called "most reliable predictor."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Morningstar (Australia) (2024). Future Focus: The three factors that will increase your returns (verbatim "a fund's annual fee is the most proven predictor of future fund returns," lowest quartile success rate 60% vs 23%). <https://www.morningstar.com.au/personal-finance/future-focus-three-factors-that-will-increase-your-returns>; Morningstar (2026). Congressional testimony (cost is the most reliable predictor). <https://www.morningstar.com/funds/morningstar-congress-investor-success-hinges-lower-costs-greater-transparency>; Carhart, M. (1997). On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03808.x>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 1, 7, 13 (fee breakdown and trailing commissions).

### 11. A "10%-off" subscription fee can still be a sales concession
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Compare subscription fees and trailing commissions across channels.
- In plain terms: A 10%-off subscription fee looks sweet, but the platform still skims a trailing commission from your assets every year. Long term, low management fee + no sales service fee is the real saving.
- Payoff: Trailing commissions (client maintenance fees) are annually deducted from net value by assets; even at a 10% subscription discount, the platform still skims continuously (commonly Class C sales service fee 0.2%–0.8%/year, see Entry 13). On a 1 million yuan position at 0.4%/year trailing, the cumulative skim over 10 years is about 47,000 yuan (excluding compounding), far exceeding the few thousand yuan saved by the one-time subscription discount. Choose Class A or ETF with "no sales service fee + low management fee" for significantly greater long-term savings. The SEC also warns that fee structures persistently erode net returns.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission. Administrative Measures for the Supervision of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Fund Sales Fees (client maintenance fee / trailing commission deduction). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/>; SEC (investor bulletin). Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses (12b-1/distribution fee continuously deducted). <https://sec.gov/answers/mfcal.htm>; Morningstar. Fee-structure research (trailing commissions erode net returns). <https://www.morningstar.com/>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 7, 9, 13 (hidden fees and sales service fees).

### 12. Funds that are too small carry liquidation risk
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Avoid mini-funds below the liquidation line.
- In plain terms: Check fund size before buying; below 200 million yuan be wary, near the 50 million red line avoid all the more. Liquidation isn't about how much you lose—it's being forced to exit at an unwanted time.
- Payoff: Article 41 of the Administrative Measures for the Operation and Management of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds stipulates: after an open-end fund's contract takes effect, if for 20 consecutive working days the number of holders is fewer than 200 or the net asset value is below 50 million yuan, it shall be disclosed in periodic reports; if the above persists for 60 consecutive working days, the fund manager shall report to the CSRC and propose a solution (change of operation method, merger with another fund, or termination of the fund contract, etc.) and convene a holders' meeting for a vote. Liquidation returns at the last-day net value, equivalent to a forced redemption, which may "lock you into a loss" or break compounding. Checking size and holder count before buying is basic to avoiding forced exit.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2014). Administrative Measures for the Operation and Management of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds, Article 41 (200 holders / 50 million yuan liquidation red line). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c106256/c1653978/content.shtml>; Chinese Government Website. Administrative Measures for the Operation and Management of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds (full text incl. Article 41). <https://big5.www.gov.cn/gate/big5/www.gov.cn/zhengce/2021-12/16/content_5724611.htm>; China Securities Journal / Securities Times (2024). Reports on normalization of mini-fund liquidation and auto-termination clause revisions. <https://www.cs.com.cn/tzjj/jjdt/202407/t20240711_6423774.html>
- Note: Limitation: liquidation is not inevitable (there's a 60-working-day buffer and a holders' meeting procedure); seed funds have a separate three-year survival rule. Cross-reference: Entry 14 (liquidity and premium/discount).

### 13. Trailing commissions (client maintenance fees) skim from your assets continuously
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Choose share classes that don't charge or charge little sales service fee.
- In plain terms: Class A vs C isn't "which is better," it's different fee structures. For short holding Class C (no subscription fee) may pay off; for long holding Class A (no sales service fee) is cheaper.
- Payoff: Class C shares' sales service fee is often 0.2%–0.8%/year, continuously deducted from net value paid to the sales channel; Class A charges a one-time subscription fee but no sales service fee. On 1 million yuan held 10 years at Class C 0.4%/year service fee, the cumulative skim is about 47,000 yuan (excluding compounding), while Class A's one-time subscription fee at 10% discount is only about 1,000–1,500 yuan. The break-even point is about 1–2 years: shorter than that choose C, longer choose A. FINRA caps 12b-1 distribution at 0.75%/year, but the sales service fee is still substantial; for long holding you must compare total expense ratios.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission. Administrative Measures for the Supervision of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Fund Sales Fees (client maintenance fee / trailing commission). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/>; SEC (investor bulletin). Mutual Fund and ETF Fees and Expenses (12b-1 fee cap). <https://sec.gov/answers/mfcal.htm>; FINRA. Rule 2341 (12b-1 distribution fee 0.75%, service fee rate 0.25% cap). <https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2341>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 7, 9, 10, 11 (hidden fees and true fee rate).

### 14. ETF premium/discount and tracking error are hidden costs
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A glance at the ETF's premium/discount and tracking error.
- In plain terms: An ETF's price is the market trading price, which may be above (premium) or below (discount) net value. Buying at a premium means losing a notch up front; long term, only low tracking error tracks the benchmark.
- Payoff: An ETF's trading price fluctuates around the IOPV (indicative net value); when liquidity is poor or sentiment-driven, the premium can reach 1%–5% or higher (QDII-ETFs had 5%–20% premium when quota was tight in 2020–2021, see Entry 6), and buying at a premium immediately floats a loss. On tracking error, mainstream broad-market ETFs' annual tracking error is mostly 0.1%–0.5%, but some sector/theme ETFs can reach over 1%, deviating from the benchmark long term. Choose high-liquidity, low-tracking-error ETFs, and avoid chasing buys at a premium—these are hidden cost control points; the existence of discounts also signals "market price ≠ net value" needs checking.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Shanghai Stock Exchange. ETF Business Implementation Rules (IOPV and subscription/redemption mechanism). <https://www.sse.com.cn/>; China Securities Regulatory Commission (2014). Administrative Measures for the Operation and Management of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds (tracking error disclosure). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c106256/c1653978/content.shtml>; Morningstar. ETF tracking-error data (broad-market 0.1%–0.5%/year). <https://www.morningstar.com/>
- Note: Cross-reference: Entries 5, 6 (ETF fees and QDII premium).

### 15. Fund dividends are "left pocket to right pocket"—no value added
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Zero.
- In plain terms: A fund dividend isn't money given to you; it's moving money from your left pocket to your right, dropping the net value a notch. Marketing loves to use "many dividend payouts" as a selling point—don't fall for it.
- Payoff: After a fund dividend the unit net value drops by the same amount, total market value unchanged (ex-dividend mechanism), adding no value and not inherently tax-free (unless the differentiated dividend holding period is met, see Entry 4). Some funds use "high dividends" to create a stable illusion, even forced to sell holdings to pay dividends, incurring trading costs and taxes. Defaulting to "dividend reinvestment" (see Entry 4) avoids auto-reducing your position on dividends. Regulations require dividends disclosed in periodic reports, but marketing often exaggerates; recognizing that dividends create no return can keep you from being lured by the "dividend gimmick" into buying high-fee products.
