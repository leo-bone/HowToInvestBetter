# XXIII. Global and ETF Allocation (Unbinding yourself from a single market)

Investing only in your home market means betting your fate on a single economy, a single currency, and a single regulatory cycle. This chapter won't teach you to "trade the whole world"; it teaches you how to use ETFs—low-cost, high-transparency, small-sum-DCA-friendly tools—to unbundle your assets from "single-market dependence": with one or two broad-based funds you can own the world's major listed companies, at the cost of only a few hundredths of a percent in annual management fees. The core of global allocation isn't "more excitement," it's "more resilience": when A-shares fall and U.S. stocks rise, or the dollar strengthens and the home currency weakens, they hedge part of each other's risk.

But "globalization" has its own pitfalls: the high premium on cross-border ETFs driven by time-zone gaps, the two-way drag of exchange rates, products like the VIX that "look like a hedge but are certain losers long term," and the Stock Connect's 500,000-yuan threshold and institutional differences. This chapter covers "why allocate, what to allocate, how to buy without stepping on a mine" in one pass; all conclusions come with verifiable institutional sources and concrete numbers wherever possible.

### 1. U.S. stocks have a long bull run, but they can also halve
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Accept the reality of "long-term up, mid-way halving"; buy with idle money you can stomach drawdowns, and prefer a broad-based ETF with an expense ratio ≤0.03% over individual stocks.
- In plain terms: U.S. stocks aren't a money-printer that only goes up. Their strength is "after falling, they still make new highs"—provided you don't cut your losses at the halving. In 2008 they halved from the peak by nearly half, but held to now they're many multiples up.
- Payoff: The S&P 500's long-term (1928–2026) annualized total return is about 10% (dividends reinvested). But drawdowns are brutal: 2000–2002 fell about 49% cumulatively, 2008's deepest intra-year drawdown was about -47.7%, and 2020's COVID year fell -33.9% intra-year. Per Oppenheimer, since 1988 the average intra-year drawdown is about 14% per year, and 10 of 27 years ended in the red. That means: the premise of capturing this 10% via an ETF is being able to hold floating losses of 30%–50% at a time—most people cut at the halving, turning "long-term return" into "someone else's return."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Oppenheimer Asset Management (2025). A Record of Resilience (chart: S&P 500 deepest intra-year drawdown, 2008 -47.71%, 2020 -33.92%). <https://www.oppenheimer.com/_assets/docs/pdf/2025/oam/record-of-resilience.pdf>; SlickCharts (data). S&P 500 Total Returns by Year (1928–2026, 2008 -37.00%, 2020 +18.40%). <https://www.slickcharts.com/sp500/returns>; S&P Dow Jones Indices. S&P 500 Index Fact Sheet (long-term annualized return about 10%). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/indices/equity/sp-500/#overview>
- Note: The 10% annualized figure is a historical mean including dividend reinvestment, not a promise for the future; individual stocks are more volatile than the index. Participate via a U.S.-stock account (Futu / Tiger, etc.) or QDII / cross-border ETFs, and note that from 2026 the domestic crackdown on illegal cross-border securities business tightens—prefer compliant channels like Stock Connect, QDII, and the Cross-boundary Wealth Management Connect.

### 2. Cross-market allocation lowers single-economy risk
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Allocate a small slice (e.g., 20%–40% of equity assets) to overseas / global indices, as a stabilizer of the portfolio rather than the main attack.
- In plain terms: When A-shares fall, U.S. stocks may rise, and vice versa. Put a bit in each, and no matter who crashes, it won't zero out the whole field. Global allocation isn't "more exciting," it helps you have fewer nightmares.
- Payoff: Different markets cycle out of sync, so portfolio volatility is lower than a single market's. MSCI research shows a global portfolio built on MSCI ACWI (including emerging markets, same-period volatility about 21%) has long-term historical volatility of about 15.1%, lower than MSCI USA's 15.6%; across the three crises of the 2000 tech bubble, the 2008 financial crisis, and 2020's pandemic, the global portfolio's max drawdown was smaller than a purely domestic one in every case. State Street data also shows that since the 2010s, the daily correlation between U.S. stocks and Japan and Europe has fallen from 0.82 and 0.93 to 0.47 and 0.63, reviving the value of diversification. The price is giving up the "full-position elasticity" of a single-market bull, in exchange for a smoother drawdown curve.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: MSCI (2020). Can diversification help weather the coronavirus storm? (MSCI ACWI historical volatility lower than most single countries; lower drawdowns in three crises). <https://www.msci.com/www/blog-posts/can-diversification-help/01778632038>; State Street Global Advisors (2026). Global diversification is regaining value (MSCI ACWI's U.S. weight rose from 53% to 63%, regional correlation falling). <https://www.ssga.com/us/en/individual/insights/mind-on-the-market-07-september-2026>
- Note: Diversification reduces volatility and tail risk, not a guarantee of higher return; in recent years the U.S. share has risen (about 63% in ACWI), so a pure "global" portfolio already carries heavy U.S. exposure—keep that in mind.

### 3. The exchange rate is a double-edged sword; don't treat it as pure alpha
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Accept two-way exchange-rate swings; don't treat them as a stable add-on; hold long term and ignore short-term currency noise.
- In plain terms: Buying a U.S.-stock fund isn't just betting on U.S. stocks—it also quietly bets on the yuan. The exchange rate can help you or hurt you: RMB appreciation eats your dollar gains, depreciation hands you an extra slice. Don't treat the exchange rate as "free alpha."
- Payoff: Overseas assets both hedge home-currency depreciation risk and can eat returns when the home currency appreciates. MSCI research (1969–2009) shows U.S. investors' "hedged" vs "unhedged" returns on non-U.S. stocks were close in long-term annualized terms (MSCI ACWI unhedged 9.5% vs hedged 9.0%), with hedging cost about -26bp to +10bp, indicating the exchange rate trends toward equilibrium long term and isn't a persistent drag. Short term, in 2022 the strong dollar created extra currency loss for RMB-denominated QDII U.S.-stock funds. The right posture: treat the exchange rate as a "random term," ignore it long term; if you hate volatility intensely you can hedge partly, but don't time it frequently on short-term swings. Also mind taxes: under the CRS, Chinese tax residents' offshore financial-account information is automatically exchanged back home every year (see Note sources).
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: MSCI (2009). Globalization of Equity Policy Portfolios (Exhibit 14–15: hedged/unhedged returns close, hedging cost -26bp~+10bp). <https://www.econ.puc-rio.br/mgarcia/Seminario/textos_preliminares/MSCI_Subramanian_Globalization_of_Equity_Policy_Portfolios_Oct_2009.pdf>; State Taxation Administration of Heilongjiang Province (2026). What is the Standard for Automatic Exchange of Financial Account Information? (OECD published CRS in 2014, 170 countries committed, 171 million accounts exchanged in 2024). <http://heilongjiang.chinatax.gov.cn/art/2026/9/10/art_7578_597977.html>
- Note: Exchange-rate impact hits the NAV of RMB-denominated QDII / cross-border ETFs; a direct U.S.-stock account also involves FX quotas and compliance. Under the CRS (Common Reporting Standard), a Chinese tax resident's deposit accounts, securities accounts, and cash-value insurance policies at offshore brokers/banks are automatically exchanged by the counterparty tax authority to the State Taxation Administration every year—normal investing is unaffected, but hiding offshore income is not.

### 4. Gold is insurance, not the main course
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Hold a small share (5%–10%) long term, via a gold ETF or physical gold, not leveraged mining stocks.
- In plain terms: Gold isn't for getting rich—it's insurance for "something still goes up when the market crashes." Hold a little, don't make it the main course; too much drags long-term returns.
- Payoff: World Gold Council 2026-edition research shows that adding 5% gold to a typical "50% stocks + 40% bonds + 10% alternatives" portfolio lifted the 20-year annualized return only from 6.3% to 6.4%, cut annualized volatility from 9.9% to 9.6%, raised risk-adjusted return from 0.635 to 0.668, and improved max drawdown from -35.3% to -33.0%—small but certain "lower volatility, hedge the tail." In crises, gold is mostly negatively correlated with stocks and bonds, an insurance against panic. But the cost is clear: gold's long-term real return lags stocks (no cash flow, no dividends); overholding (e.g., >10%–15%) drags compounding. Conclusion: 5%–10% is the "sweet spot" repeatedly found in the evidence; beyond that, marginal utility diminishes.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: World Gold Council (2026). Gold as a strategic asset: 2026 edition (5% gold lifts 20-year risk-adjusted return 0.635→0.668, max drawdown -35.3%→-33.0%). <https://www.gold.org/goldhub/research/gold-strategic-asset-2026-edition/20450>; World Gold Council (China) (2026). The impact of gold on investment portfolios—risk/return performance. <https://china.gold.org/goldhub/research/publication/2026/02/04/19500/19527>
- Note: Gold pays no interest and no dividend, lagging equities long term; its value is "insurance," not "return." Allocate via a low-fee gold ETF (e.g., domestic gold ETFs); avoid mining stocks (which carry equity beta) and leveraged products.

### 5. REITs: turn real estate into tradable cash flow
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand its dual nature of "high dividends, rate-sensitive," hold with idle money and an income-collecting mindset.
- In plain terms: REITs are like "buying rent-collecting property through a stock account"—high dividends (2024 dividend yield about 3.96%). But they're the enemy of rates—prices often fall when rates rise; don't use them as a bond fund.
- Payoff: REITs provide steady dividends and inflation hedging, but prices are highly rate-sensitive. Nareit data: the FTSE Nareit All Equity REITs index returned +4.9% total in 2024, but fell -24.9% in 2022 on rate hikes; 2024 dividend yield 3.96%, far above the S&P 500's 1.22%. The index is clearly negatively correlated with the 10-year Treasury yield—after October 2024, as Treasury yields rose, REITs fell about 8.9% while the Russell 1000 rose 5.1% over the same period. Long term (past 25 years), REITs annualized about 11.4%, beating the S&P 500's 7.6%, but with volatility greater than bonds and less than stocks, and the rule "rates up → price down" makes them unfit as a steady bond fund. Participate via southbound / cross-border REITs ETFs or domestic REITs.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Nareit (2024). REITs Gain 4.9% in 2024 as December Headwinds Trim Earlier Gains (2024 +4.9%, 2022 -24.9%; dividend yield 3.96% vs S&P 1.22%). <https://www.reit.com/news/blog/market-commentary/reits-gain-49-2024-december-headwinds-trim-earlier-gains>; Nareit (2026). REITWatch (REITs dividend yield negatively correlated with 10-year Treasury yield). <https://www.reit.com/sites/default/files/reitwatch/RW2602.pdf>
- Note: REITs aren't bonds; they're highly rate-sensitive. Prefer a diversified REITs ETF over a single-property REIT, to avoid sector risk like office/commercial real estate.

### 6. Emerging markets: high volatility, high potential return
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Bear higher volatility; allocate only a small share (e.g., 5%–15% of equity assets) long term; don't go heavy betting on a country's rise.
- In plain terms: Emerging markets can rise 50% in a year or fall 40%. Hold a little for elasticity; don't stake your life on a "country rises" story.
- Payoff: Emerging markets have higher growth potential than developed markets, but risk is higher in step. MSCI data shows the MSCI Emerging Markets index's same-period annualized volatility is about 21%, above the global portfolio's 15.1%, with higher policy, currency, and liquidity risk. Their long-term return does not steadily beat developed markets—between 2008–2020 it lagged badly multiple times, and a single country's drawdown often exceeds 40%. So its role should be "a satellite sleeve of the portfolio": use a small share (5%–15%) for higher potential return and geographic diversification, not as the core. For most investors, participate via an emerging-market ETF (e.g., MSCI EM) rather than individual stocks / single-country funds, and be mentally and financially prepared to "ride the roller coaster."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: MSCI (2020). Can diversification help weather the coronavirus storm? (MSCI EM same-period volatility 21%, above the global portfolio). <https://www.msci.com/www/blog-posts/can-diversification-help/01778632038>; MSCI (2009). Globalization of Equity Policy Portfolios (non-U.S. stocks' higher potential return comes with higher risk). <https://www.econ.puc-rio.br/mgarcia/Seminario/textos_preliminares/MSCI_Subramanian_Globalization_of_Equity_Policy_Portfolios_Oct_2009.pdf>
- Note: Emerging markets diverge wildly internally (China, India, Brazil each different); avoid betting on a single country, prefer a broad-based EM ETF. QDII has several emerging-market / single-country funds; mind subscription quotas (limits).

### 7. HK stocks are often cheap, but with institutional and liquidity differences
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: First understand the Stock Connect threshold and the rules, then decide how much to allocate; don't go heavy just because it's "cheap."
- In plain terms: Hong Kong stocks are cheap for a reason—liquidity, short-selling mechanism, foreign dominance, no price limits. To buy HK stocks, the most compliant channel is Stock Connect (securities + cash accounts combined ≥ 500,000 yuan).
- Payoff: HK stocks are often cheap due to a liquidity discount and low risk appetite, offering "valuation trough" opportunities; but the rule differences are significant: T+0, no price limits, possible long trading suspension or even direct delisting. The main channel is Stock Connect under the mutual market connect—per HKEX investor materials, the Shanghai–HK Stock Connect launched 2014-11-17 and the Shenzhen–HK Stock Connect 2016-12-05; mainland retail investors need securities and cash account balances combined of not less than 500,000 yuan (excluding margin-financed portions). As of mid-2026, cumulative net Stock Connect buys had exceeded 5 trillion HKD, becoming a major buyer in HK stocks. Conclusion: use Stock Connect for a small allocation to undervalued HK blue chips / high-dividend names, but first understand its liquidity divergence and institutional differences—don't treat "cheap" as a safety cushion.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing (HKEX). Shanghai-Hong Kong / Shenzhen-Hong Kong Stock Connect Investor Information Booklet (retail investor accounts combined not less than 500,000 yuan; Shanghai Connect 2014-11-17, Shenzhen Connect 2016-12-05). <https://www.hkex.com.hk/-/media/HKEX-Market/Mutual-Market/Stock-Connect/Getting-Started/Information-Booklet-and-FAQ/Information-Book-for-Investors/Investor_Book_Cn_(May).pdf>; Economic Information Daily (2016). 14 questions you must know about Shenzhen-HK Stock Connect in practice (Stock Connect 500,000-yuan threshold, T+0, no price limits). <https://www.jjckb.cn/2016-11/26/c_135859995.htm>
- Note: Besides Stock Connect, you can also participate via QDII HK-stock funds; direct HK/U.S. accounts are affected by the 2026 cross-border securities-business clean-up, so mainland residents should prefer compliant channels. Stock Connect settles in RMB and quotes in HKD, with FX costs included.

### 8. The VIX / fear index can't be held long term
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand the persistent drag of futures contango; treat it only as "very short-term insurance," never hold long term.
- In plain terms: A panic-index product is "short-term insurance"; holding it long almost certainly loses—roll-over decay is like a leaking bucket. Products like VXX can shed over 90% in a few years.
- Payoff: VIX-related products (e.g., VXX) track short-term VIX futures, and VIX futures are in contango most of the time—far contracts pricier. Whaley (2013) notes VIX futures were in contango about 80% of trading days, and roll-over decay makes buy-and-hold almost certain to lose long term; Eraker & Wu estimate investing in VIX futures lost over 97% from 2006-01 to 2013-03, about -30% annualized. The academic conclusion is consistent: VIX ETPs are "unsuitable for buy-and-hold," providing a hedge only in the very short window when the market panics and the curve turns to backwardation. For ordinary investors, the right use is "don't touch," or use only a tiny amount over a very short period to express a safe-haven view—never treat it as an asset to hold long term.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Whaley, R. (2013). Trading Volatility: At What Cost? (VIX futures in contango 80% of trading days, buy-and-hold almost certain to lose). <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256062271_Trading_Volatility_At_What_Cost>; Eraker, B. & Wu, Y. (2013). Explaining the Negative Returns to Volatility Claims: An Equilibrium Approach (2006–2013 loss >97%, about -30% annualized). <https://wsbfiles.bus.wisc.edu/digital/ywu98/intellcont_journal/VIXETNs_ErakerWu-3.pdf>
- Note: VIX products are a timing tool, not an allocation asset; leveraged inverse products are even riskier. The vast majority of long-term holders lose; list it under "no long-term holding."

### 9. Cross-border ETFs have the double trap of "time gap + premium"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Avoid the high premium when domestic and foreign trading sessions are misaligned; check the premium/discount rate (market price vs IOPV) before buying.
- In plain terms: When U.S. markets are closed you can still trade U.S.-stock ETFs in A-shares; then the price is an "emotional price." Buying at a high premium, you get carved up when U.S. markets open / arbitrage kicks in.
- Payoff: Because of the time gap, a cross-border ETF's underlying (U.S. / Japanese stocks) NAV is "frozen" during A-share trading, so premiums can widen on emotion. In May 2023 the Nikkei 225 ETF was frenzied: the E Fund Nikkei 225 ETF premium hit 22.44% at one point, the ChinaAMC 17.34%, falling back 16.61% / 13.97% within two trading days, and chasers lost big. Per Huabao Securities, domestic ETF premiums above 15% lasted on average only 1.7 days. The root cause is QDII FX quota tightness → primary-market subscription restricted → arbitrage mechanism fails (per SAFE, as of end-2024-05 QDII cumulative approvals reached 167.8 billion USD across 189 institutions, still often insufficient). So before buying a cross-border ETF, check the premium/discount rate first; a premium >3%–5% is a warning, prefer at-discount or near-NAV products.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: China Securities Journal Network (2023). Cross-border ETFs keep high premiums, fund companies keep warning (2023-05-19 Nikkei ETF premium 22.44%, fell back 16.61% in two days). <https://www.cnstock.com/commonDetail/19741>; State Administration of Foreign Exchange (SAFE) (2024). Coordinating high-quality capital-account opening with high-level security (as of end-2024-05 QDII cumulative approvals 167.8 billion USD, 189 institutions). <https://www.safe.gov.cn/safe/2024/0627/24669.html>; China Securities Journal (2023). High premiums don't last, Nikkei ETF on a roller coaster. <https://www.cs.com.cn/hw2020/202305/t20230524_6346145.html>
- Note: Premium ≠ return; it's an "emotion tax" that converges fast once quotas loosen or hype fades. Cross-border ETFs also carry exchange-rate risk (see Entry 3). Compliant channels also include Stock Connect, QDII funds, and the Cross-boundary Wealth Management Connect.

### 10. One or two broad-based funds are enough for global allocation; don't overdo it
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Resist the urge to "buy one for every market"; cover the globe with one or two broad-based funds.
- In plain terms: For global allocation, one MSCI global / ACWI-type ETF already covers most listed companies. Adding ten is worse than keeping one—more funds only add cost, not return.
- Payoff: One global / all-market ETF already holds thousands of companies across dozens of countries. MSCI ACWI covers 23 developed + 24 emerging markets (about 47 countries); State Street data shows its U.S. weight is about 63%, enough to express "global" exposure; Morningstar counts the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO) expense ratio at only 0.03%/year, while the older SPY is 0.0945%—over 10 years, every 100,000 yuan in fees differs by about 550 yuan, and the long-term compounding gap is significant. On this basis, ordinary investors can finish allocation with "1 domestic broad-based (e.g., CSI 300 ETF) + 1 global broad-based (e.g., MSCI ACWI / ACWI ex-China ETF)," with no need to pile up sector / country ETFs. Note that non-U.S. residents buying U.S.-listed ETFs face a 30% dividend withholding tax (Ireland-domiciled UCITS can cut it to 15%); choose products domiciled for tax efficiency.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: MSCI (2020). Can diversification help weather the coronavirus storm? (MSCI ACWI covers the globe, no home bias). <https://www.msci.com/www/blog-posts/can-diversification-help/01778632038>; State Street Global Advisors (2026). Global diversification is regaining value (ACWI about 47 countries, U.S. weight 63%). <https://www.ssga.com/us/en/individual/insights/mind-on-the-market-07-september-2026>; Vanguard. Understanding the total cost of ETF ownership (VOO expense 0.03% vs SPY 0.0945%, over 10 years every 100,000 saves about 550 yuan). <https://www.vanguardmexico.com/content/dam/intl/americas/documents/latam/en/2025/06/understanding-the-total-cost-of-etf-ownership.pdf>
- Note: Broad-based ≠ risk-free; ACWI's U.S. share is already high, so if you want to cut U.S. exposure pick ACWI ex-USA. ETF expense ratio, tracking error, premium/discount, and domicile tax should all enter the choice.
