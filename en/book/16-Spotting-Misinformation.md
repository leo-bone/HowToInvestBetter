# XVI. Spotting Misinformation

The number-one enemy of investing is often not the market itself, but noise and scams. The cheaper and louder the information, the sooner you should suspect its motive. What this chapter gives you isn't a checklist of "seeing through every specific scam" (that's inexhaustible), but a low-cost, reusable "anti-scam operating system": first ask "who is talking, who pays them," then check the evidence and look for counterexamples, and finally replace reliance on others with your own hard skills.

This system's necessity isn't fear-mongering. Per the Ministry of Public Security's 2024 briefing, in 2023 nationwide public-security organs cracked 437,000 telecom and online fraud cases, and the National Anti-Fraud Center urgently intercepted 328.8 billion yuan of involved funds; just for "pig-butchering" scams alone, the U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) recorded related losses of 1.14 billion USD in 2023. The scale of scams shows: guarding against scams isn't paranoia, it's a required course for modern investors. The 12 entries below each correspond to a trap you meet every day.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses): I. Conflict-of-interest filter—first ask "who pays him" (Entries 1, 2, 12); II. Puncture the "authority / track record" halo (Entries 3, 4, 6); III. Media, noise, and rumors (Entries 5, 9, 10); IV. Cultivate your own judgment (Entries 7, 8, 11).

### 1. Free advice is worth exactly what you paid
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Wariness of "free stock picks / free trade signals / free courses."
- In plain terms: The sky won't drop free and reliable get-rich info. The other side doesn't charge you because you're their product—either you're funneled to be the bag-holder, or sold courses, or signed up for rebate commissions. Ask who pays them before deciding whether to listen.
- Payoff: "Free" is the most common bait in scams and illegal stock-tipping. The Xiamen Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC disclosed that since 2023, Jin Moubin and others, without securities-business qualifications, used selling ginseng wine and ginseng powder as a cover to provide illegal securities investment consulting, with involved transaction amounts over 30 million yuan, and 7 suspects have been prosecuted; the Jiangsu CSRC disclosed a Meng Mou gang that registered a "cultural media company" and impersonated securities-company employees to pull investors into scam groups under the pretext of recommending stocks, causing multiple investors to be defrauded of over 650,000 yuan under the name of investment. Ministry of Public Security data further shows that in 2023, nationwide 437,000 telecom-fraud cases were cracked and 328.8 billion yuan of funds intercepted. "Free trade signals" are essentially a traffic business: you think you're getting a deal, but you're the item being sold. Treat it as advertising, not goodwill, and you filter out most noise.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Xiamen Regulatory Bureau of the China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) (2024). Risk alert on preventing illegal securities/futures/fund activities (case: Jin Moubin et al. provided illegal securities investment consulting via livestream without approval, over 30 million yuan involved). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/xiamen/c105637/c7550231/content.shtml>; Jiangsu Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). Interpretation of typical cases of illegal securities/futures business crimes (IV) (Meng Mou gang impersonated securities-company employees to funnel investors, causing over 650,000 yuan defrauded). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/jiangsu/c105410/c7582277/content.shtml>; Ministry of Public Security of the PRC (2024). Briefing on 2023 public-security work and team-building results (nationwide cracked 437,000 telecom-fraud cases). <https://www.mps.gov.cn/n2254536/n2254544/n2254552/n9372690/n9372708/c9373270/content.html>
- Note: This entry is about the positioning principle of "motive identification"; specific convictions follow judicial determination; illegal stock-tipping often hides behind "knowledge payment" or "communities," and needs to be judged against qualification checks (CSRC, Securities Association websites).

### 2. Who gives you the advice, and who pays them
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The instinct to trust the authority halo.
- In plain terms: Don't ask "is he right," ask first "who does he earn from by saying this." Worth listening to only if interests align; auto-discount those with opposing interests.
- Payoff: Rewrite every piece of advice as "who benefits from this advice," and you'll find that behind most "for your good" pitches there's consideration. Sell-side researchers live on commission splits, finance big-Vs on traffic and product promotion, bank wealth managers on product rebates, and advisors on service fees—their interests aren't naturally aligned with yours. The CSRC's "Interim Provisions on Securities Investment Advisory Business" (CSRC Announcement [2020] No. 66) Article 19 explicitly prohibits advisors from "promising or guaranteeing investment returns in any way," and Article 24 prohibits "false, untrue, or misleading promotion" of service capability and past performance—the very reason regulators must explicitly prohibit shows "misaligned interests + exaggerated promotion" is the industry norm. First locate the other side's fee model (commission, rebate, course sales, traffic, bag-holding), then decide how much trust this info deserves.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) (2020). Interim Provisions on Securities Investment Advisory Business (CSRC Announcement [2020] No. 66), Articles 19 and 24. <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/hunan/c105691/c1294474/content.shtml>; Shanxi Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). 3·15 Investor Protection thematic education activity, Case 1: securities investment advisory institutions' exaggerated promotion (Interim Measures for the Administration of Securities and Futures Investment Consulting, Article 24). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/shanxi/c1055543/c1609430/content.shtml>
- Note: Identifying interests is the first step of "correlation ≠ causation"; the other side having qualifications doesn't mean the conclusion favors you, and independent judgment is still required. Cross-reference: Entry 12.

### 3. A fund manager's past performance doesn't predict the future
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The impulse to chase "last year's champion fund."
- In plain terms: The fund that rose most last year usually underperforms this year. The performance ranking is a "replay," not a "forecast." If you must look, look at the long run, the style, the fee—not the ranking.
- Payoff: S&P's SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) semi-annual scorecard is the hardest evidence for this conclusion: taking the U.S. market, passive indices long dominate active management, and the longer the period, the higher the underperformance ratio—per SPIVA data, the share of large-cap active funds underperforming the benchmark over 1, 3, 5, and 15 years is about 61%, 80%, 87%, and 92% respectively (S&P Dow Jones Indices, 2023). Carhart (1997), using the "momentum three-factor" model, showed fund-performance persistence is extremely weak—the previous year's king most likely reverts to mediocrity or worse the next year. Chasing star funds is essentially buying the past and betting the future, often catching the top and paying higher fees. Looking at long-run geometric averages, style drift, and fees is far more useful than annual rankings.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices (2023). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard (Mid-Year 2023) (active funds long underperform the benchmark). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research-insights/spiva/>; Carhart, M. (1997). On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance, Journal of Finance 52(1). <https://doi.org/10.2307/2329556>
- Note: SPIVA targets U.S. mutual funds; A-share active management still has structural differences versus the index, but the conclusion "performance can't be extrapolated, beware survivor bias" is universal. Cross-reference: Entry 4.

### 4. The survivor bias of finance big-Vs
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The superstition that "he's always right."
- In plain terms: The "god-predictor" blogger you follow is just the lucky one who didn't drown. The silent majority deleted their wrong posts—you can't see them.
- Payoff: Algorithms only push "looks-right" content, so you systematically overestimate others' predictive ability—that's survivor bias. At the scam level it's more lethal: in the Supreme People's Court's February 7, 2024 typical cross-border telecom-fraud cases, Zeng Mou and 66 others disguised themselves as "tall-rich-handsome" and used social software to befriend victims and lure them to fake platforms, defrauding 196 people of over 27.96 million yuan; the Gao Mou case used "pig-butchering" to defraud over 170,000 USD, and when the victim tried to leave, was restrained and beaten, and had to pay "ransom" to return home. The U.S. FTC recorded 64,000 "romance/investment" scams that year, with 1.14 billion USD in losses and a median individual loss of 2,000 USD—of which about 18% (2022) followed the script "a friend got rich in investing, let me bring you in." The big-V "track record" you see and these packaged "success cases" are the same filtering mechanism: the wrong disappear, the right get amplified.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Supreme People's Court (2024). Typical cases of cross-border telecom-network fraud and associated crimes (defendant Zeng Mou, Zhong Mouhua, Wang Mou et al., 67-person fraud case: disguised as "tall-rich-handsome" on social software to befriend and lure to fake platforms, defrauding 196 people of over 27.96 million yuan; defendant Gao Mou fraud case: "pig-butchering" defrauding over 170,000 USD). <https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/425102.html>; U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) (2024). "Love Stinks" – when a scammer is involved (2023 romance scams 64,000 cases, 1.14 billion USD losses, median individual 2,000 USD). <https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2024/02/love-stinks-when-scammer-involved>
- Note: Survivor bias doesn't deny all big-Vs, but reminds you that "what he shows ≠ his real, long-run, replicable record." Cross-reference: Entry 2, Entry 12.

### 5. Media headlines manufacture anxiety, but not alpha
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The hand that trades on news.
- In plain terms: The scarier the headline, the more you want to act—but the market already knows the scary news. Trading on headlines loses steadily long-run.
- Payoff: News is naturally biased toward negative and extreme (attention economy), while the market has mostly already priced in known information. The Ministry of Public Security's 2024 briefing notes that in 2023, nationwide 437,000 telecom-fraud cases were cracked, and since August 2023 the case count has fallen consecutively; the National Anti-Fraud Center issued 9.4 million fund-warning directives, 13.89 million in-person dissuasions, intercepted 2.75 billion scam calls and 2.28 billion scam texts, handled 8.364 million fraud-related domains/URLs, and urgently intercepted 328.8 billion yuan of involved funds—this string of numbers itself shows that "fraud / crash" headlines are never a reason for you to buy or sell immediately. Buying and selling on headline sentiment equals buying the oldest news at the highest emotion; what really moves prices is the expectation gap, not the headline. Treat news as "background material," not "trading instructions," and you significantly cut the frequency and cost of chasing rallies and panic-selling.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Ministry of Public Security of the PRC (2024). Briefing on 2023 public-security work and team-building results (nationwide cracked 437,000 telecom-fraud cases, urgently intercepted 328.8 billion yuan of involved funds). <https://www.mps.gov.cn/n2254536/n2254544/n2254552/n9372690/n9372708/c9373270/content.html>; Supreme People's Court (2024). Typical cases of cross-border telecom-network fraud and associated crimes (fake online investment-and-wealth-management scams account for about one-third of the losses by amount). <https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/425102.html>
- Note: Evidence grade B: Ministry of Public Security data are hard facts, but the "news → emotion → loss" mechanism is a behavioral-finance inference needing validation against your own backtest. Cross-reference: Entry 9, Entry 10.

### 6. Read the research report's conclusion, but also whose seat it sits on
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Treating the sell-side "Buy" rating as gospel.
- In plain terms: 90% of the market is "Buy" rated—the rating itself carries little information. Use research reports as a data library; draw your own conclusions.
- Payoff: Sell-side research is tightly bound to underwriting and commission splits; the share of "Buy" ratings is chronically abnormally high, with few Neutral/Sell—the rating distribution itself tells the story. The CSRC's "Interim Provisions on Securities Investment Advisory Business" (CSRC Announcement [2020] No. 66) Article 19 prohibits "promising or guaranteeing investment returns," and the "Interim Measures for the Administration of Securities and Futures Investment Consulting" Article 24 prohibits "promising securities/futures investment returns to investors" and false or untrue promotion—regulators repeatedly issue documents precisely because "bullish bias + exaggerated past performance" is an industry chronic disease. The correct use: treat research reports as a library of "data, frameworks, industry-chain information," learn their analytical path, but make the conclusions and buy/sell decisions yourself. Especially beware of "investment-advisory services" with "no risk warning, flaunting profit records, promising returns"—that already crosses the violation red line.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) (2020). Interim Provisions on Securities Investment Advisory Business (CSRC Announcement [2020] No. 66), Articles 19 and 24. <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/hunan/c105691/c1294474/content.shtml>; Shanxi Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). 3·15 Investor Protection thematic education activity, Case 1: securities investment advisory institutions' exaggerated promotion (Interim Measures for the Administration of Securities and Futures Investment Consulting, Article 24). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/shanxi/c1055543/c1609430/content.shtml>
- Note: Rating bias exists in both A-shares and U.S. stocks; the value of independent buy-side research lies precisely in its interest isolation from the sell side. Cross-reference: Entry 2, Entry 11.

### 7. Backtests that look beautiful often blow up live
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Belief in the "30% annualized, 5% drawdown" strategy brochure.
- In plain terms: Any "holy-grail strategy" with a particularly pretty historical curve mostly just tuned the past data to fit perfectly. Real gold is the out-of-sample live test.
- Payoff: Overfitting makes a strategy perfect in history and useless in the future. Bailey & López de Prado (2014) proposed the "Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)," proving that among a vast combination of parameters, the optimal backtest's high Sharpe is largely selection bias from "trying too many times"; they further used "combinatorial symmetric cross-validation (CSCV)" to estimate the "probability of backtest overfitting (PBO)," pointing out that most "holy grails" found by optimizers blow up out of sample. In practice, tuning parameters until the curve looks good is cheating: a strategy with Sharpe 1.5, if it's the best picked from 100 candidates, may have a nearly negligible true edge. Identification points: out-of-sample/rolling validation, parameter sensitivity, economic logic, and disclosure of the number of trials. A dazzling backtest ≠ replicable returns.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bailey, D. H., & López de Prado, M. (2014). The Deflated Sharpe Ratio: Correcting for Selection Bias, Backtest Overfitting and Non-Normality, Journal of Portfolio Management 40(5), 94–107. <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551>; Bailey, D. H., Borwein, J., López de Prado, M., & Zhu, Q. J. (2015). The Probability of Backtest Overfitting, Journal of Computational Finance. <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253>
- Note: Evidence grade A: the methodology has strict mathematical proof; but for "a specific strategy" live testing is still required. Individual developers especially must beware of "fooling themselves."

### 8. Replace "Am I right" with "Do I have contrary evidence"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The inertia of finding corroboration for your existing view.
- In plain terms: Don't keep proving you're right—that's too easy. Force yourself to find "what if I'm wrong" evidence; only say "I'm probably right" if you can't find it—that's what's worth money.
- Payoff: Confirmation bias is humanity's most common and most expensive cognitive trap: we naturally search only for information supporting our view and ignore counterexamples, thus going deeper in the wrong direction. Nickerson (1998)'s review notes confirmation bias is "ubiquitous in many guises," systematically distorting judgment from scientific experiments to daily decisions. In investing it shows as: on a rise find positives, on a fall find negatives, forever self-confirming. The highest cost-performance cognitive upgrade is to force yourself to ask "if I'm wrong, where would the evidence be and what would it look like," and actively search for research, data, and cases opposite your position. In a case published by the Shanxi CSRC, an investor was anchored by the promotion "steady no-loss, average return 35%," never seeking the contrary "what if I lose" evidence, and after paying over 10,000 yuan in service fees suffered a large loss. Disprove first, then believe.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Nickerson, R. S. (1998). Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises, Review of General Psychology 2(2), 175–220. <https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175>; Shanxi Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). 3·15 Investor Protection thematic education activity, Case 1 (investor, trusting "steady no-loss" promotion, paid service fees then suffered a large loss). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/shanxi/c1055543/c1609430/content.shtml>
- Note: Evidence grade B: confirmation bias itself has strong empirical grounding (psychology A-grade), but "using contrary evidence to lower the pitfall rate" is an operable methodology whose effect varies by person and needs records and review.

### 9. By the time a rumor reaches your ear, it's already priced in
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The fantasy of betting on "inside information."
- In plain terms: The "sure-win" tip in the group has passed through who-knows-how-many hands by the time it reaches you. If there were real meat, it wouldn't reach you. Trading on tips long-run just feeds the early movers.
- Payoff: Those with real information advantage quietly earn and exit; by the time the tip reaches groups, dinners, and short-video comments, the market has long digested that expectation, and you're often catching the last baton. The Ministry of Public Security's 2024 briefing notes that in 2023, 2.75 billion scam calls and 2.28 billion scam texts were intercepted and 8.364 million fraud-related domains/URLs handled—a significant share of which are funneling pitches like "inside stock," "teacher trade signals," "private placement channel." The Jin Moubin case disclosed by the Xiamen CSRC used a livestream to sell goods as a cover for disguised stock-tipping, over 30 million yuan involved; the "code" investors heard was essentially a funnel tool used to jack up the price for others. Under semi-strong efficient markets, publicly available information is already reflected in prices; your "inside info" is most likely bait someone carefully fed you.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Xiamen Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). Risk alert on preventing illegal securities/futures/fund activities (case: Jin Moubin livestream disguised stock-tipping, over 30 million yuan involved; investors hearing "inside info" are often the last baton). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/xiamen/c105637/c7550231/content.shtml>; Ministry of Public Security of the PRC (2024). Briefing on 2023 public-security work and team-building results (intercepted 2.75 billion scam calls, 2.28 billion scam texts, handled 8.364 million fraud-related domains/URLs). <https://www.mps.gov.cn/n2254536/n2254544/n2254552/n9372690/n9372708/c9373270/content.html>
- Note: Evidence grade B: the efficient-market hypothesis is contested, but "tips decay layer by layer, last baton holds the bag" is a repeatedly verified fact in scams and pump-and-dump. Cross-reference: Entry 1, Entry 12.

### 10. Subscribe less, not more; information overload is a hidden cost
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The restraint to cut information sources and turn off push notifications.
- In plain terms: A lot of your losses come from "watching too much." Turn off push, leave the groups, and you actually earn more. More information isn't better.
- Payoff: More information means stronger trading impulses and overconfidence, and decision quality falls—a shared conclusion of attention economics and behavioral finance. Scams exploit exactly this: illegal consulting reaches you through WeChat, QQ, livestreams, short videos, and tip groups at high frequency across multiple channels; the Ministry of Public Security intercepted 2.28 billion scam texts and handled 8.364 million fraud-related domains/URLs in 2023, showing the "information flood" is laced with massive targeted feeding. Fix a few authoritative sources (exchanges, the CSRC, listed-company annual reports, index-company websites), and block the rest by default; turn off market push, leave the call-group, and swap "screen-watching time" for "financial-statement-reading time." Fewer and better information sources protect your principal and your nerves better than all-day scrolling.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Xiamen Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). Risk alert on preventing illegal securities/futures/fund activities (illegal consulting reaches via WeChat, QQ, livestreams, short videos at high frequency; overload amplifies misjudgment). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/xiamen/c105637/c7550231/content.shtml>; Ministry of Public Security of the PRC (2024). Briefing on 2023 public-security work and team-building results (handled 8.364 million fraud-related domains/URLs, intercepted 2.28 billion scam texts). <https://www.mps.gov.cn/n2254536/n2254544/n2254552/n9372690/n9372708/c9373270/content.html>
- Note: Evidence grade B: information overload harming decisions has experimental support, but the "optimal subscription count" varies by person; the core is active filtering, not passive drowning. Cross-reference: Entry 5, Entry 11.

### 11. Learn to read the three financial statements—more reliable than tips
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The time to learn financial basics.
- In plain terms: When someone gives you a "positive," you can open the financials yourself and see whether it makes money and where the money comes from—then you won't be led by the pitch. This is the hardest self-protection skill.
- Payoff: If you can read the income statement / balance sheet / cash-flow statement yourself, you have a bottom-layer ability to filter noise, no longer dependent on others' "interpretation." In a Shanxi CSRC case, investor Li trusted promotions like "ordinary clients also get *% return" and "one or two months' return *," paid over 10,000 yuan in service fees for stock tips, and ended with a large loss—with basic financial literacy, Li should have spotted the violation signal of "no risk warning, promised returns" (Interim Provisions on Securities Investment Advisory Business Article 24). Financial-statement ability is a one-time investment with long-run compounding moat: it lets you, when others shout "positive," check whether operating cash flow is truly positive, and when others shout "distress reversal," see the debt structure clearly. Upgrade "understanding the pitch" to "reading the statements"—the most real barrier between you and the harvesters.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Shanxi Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). 3·15 Investor Protection thematic education activity, Case 1: securities investment advisory institutions' exaggerated promotion (investment-decision risk borne by the investor; reading fundamentals is the baseline ability). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/shanxi/c1055543/c1609430/content.shtml>; China Securities Regulatory Commission (CSRC) (2020). Interim Provisions on Securities Investment Advisory Business (CSRC Announcement [2020] No. 66), Article 24. <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/hunan/c105691/c1294474/content.shtml>
- Note: Evidence grade B: financial literacy correlating positively with investment-decision quality has research support, but reading statements ≠ beating the market; it guards against "being scammed," not guaranteeing excess returns. Cross-reference: Entry 6, Entry 8.

### 12. Translate every "recommendation" into "what is he earning"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A one-sentence habit switch.
- In plain terms: This is a universal filter. For any "for your good" financial advice, first translate it into "who pays him." After translating, what you should trust needs no persuasion, and what you shouldn't needs no worry.
- Payoff: Tip groups, livestreams, wealth courses, bank pitches, "securities-employee" impersonators funneling—apply the unified model "who does he earn from this advice," and 90% of the sales pitches reveal themselves automatically. The Meng Mou gang disclosed by the Jiangsu CSRC registered a "cultural media company" and impersonated securities-company employees to pull investors into scam QQ groups under the pretext of recommending stocks, causing investors to be defrauded of over 650,000 yuan; in the Supreme People's Court's 2024 typical cases, Zeng Mou and 66 others disguised as "tall-rich-handsome" to befriend and lure to fake investment platforms, defrauding over 27.96 million yuan; the U.S. FTC recorded 2023 "romance + investment" scam losses of 1.14 billion USD. Translate "teacher trade signals" into "he earns my service fee / bag-holding / head-count fee," translate "inside info" into "I'm the last-baton bag-holder," and your judgment upgrades instantly. This entry is the master switch of the chapter.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Jiangsu Regulatory Bureau of the CSRC (2024). Typical cases of cross-border telecom-network fraud and associated crimes (defendant Zeng Mou, Zhong Mouhua, Wang Mou et al., 67-person fraud case: disguised as "tall-rich-handsome" to befriend and lure investment fraud, over 27.96 million yuan). <https://www.court.gov.cn/zixun/xiangqing/425102.html>; U.S. Federal Trade Commission (FTC) (2024).
