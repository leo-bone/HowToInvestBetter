# XX. Extreme Scenarios

Most of the time investing is calm. The real gap is usually opened up in a handful of extreme days: one crash, one week of circuit breakers, one job loss, one unexpected medical bill—any of these is enough to turn a "paper loss" into "permanent principal loss," and turn "long-term compounding" into "starting over." This chapter won't teach you to predict black swans—no one can reliably predict 2008, 2015, 2020—it teaches you to build your "survival" ability in advance: keep enough cash cushion, don't touch leverage, keep your hands still in a crash, and write the worst case into your plan. Extreme markets test not your IQ, but your preparation and discipline beforehand.

History has repeatedly proven that a crisis's damage doesn't lie in the drawdown itself, but in it happening exactly when you are "out of cash, in debt, and need money." In 2008 the S&P 500 drew down about 57% from its high and didn't bottom until March 2009; in the 2020 pandemic crash it fell 33.9% in just 23 trading days and triggered circuit breakers four times; in 2015, A-shares saw 16 "thousand-stock limit-down" days within 58 trading days, and liquidity at one point fell below one-tenth of its pre-crash level. Those who don't blow up at such moments rely not on perfect bottom-picking, but on cash set aside before the crisis, no leverage on their back, and a written contingency plan.

Below, 12 entries are organized along "foundation—discipline—income shock—legal—leverage," each giving actionable cost, payoff, and evidence. Remember one master rule: in extreme scenarios the first goal is not to make money, but to "not be permanently knocked out." As long as you're still at the table, time will repair most of your temporary losses for you.

Entries in this section are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses): cash and emergency cushion (Entries 1, 2, 8, 11); behavioral discipline in a crash (Entries 3, 9, 10, 12); income interruption and unemployment shock (Entries 4, 5); legal and estate arrangements (Entry 6); leverage wipeout risk (Entry 7).

### 1. An emergency fund is a prerequisite for investing, not optional
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Set aside cash or a money market fund equal to 3–6 months of essential living expenses (calculate as monthly essential spending ×3 or ×6; exclude non-essential items like travel and entertainment).
- In plain terms: Without an emergency fund, one job loss, one illness, one accident forces you to cut your positions at the asset bottom or borrow high-interest consumer loans, turning a paper loss into a real loss. Lay the foundation first—keep 3–6 months of living expenses—before talking about investing. This is the foundation of all investing.
- Payoff: Without an emergency fund, any job loss, illness, or accident will force you to sell assets at the market bottom or borrow high-interest debt to cover expenses, turning "temporary paper losses" into "permanent principal losses." It is the safety cushion of all investing: the U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) lists the emergency fund as "the first step in saving," emphasizing that even a small amount can avoid using credit cards or loans to cover sudden expenses and prevent debt snowballing; a 2020 Federal Reserve study further points out that households unable to work remotely held a median of only about 3 weeks of income in accessible savings, and were extremely prone to "not making it" when the pandemic shut down work. With a cushion first, you dare to hold for the long term and won't be forced to sell in a crash.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2024). An essential guide to building an emergency fund. <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>; Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2020). Financial Profiles of Workers Most Vulnerable to Coronavirus-Related Earnings Loss in the Spring of 2020 (FEDS 2020-093). <https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020093pap.pdf>
- Note: The amount adjusts with income stability—3 months for a stable dual-income household, 6 months or more for freelancers / single income sources; keep it in an FDIC-insured money market fund or high-yield savings account, strictly separate from your investment account, and don't use it for daily spending.

### 2. In a crash, cash is the only option you've got
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Keep a portion of cash you can access at any time and don't use for daily spending (share depends on risk appetite, generally 5%–15% of total assets).
- In plain terms: Normally you feel cash wastes interest; only at the crash do you learn it's king. When no one else has money, you with cash can pick up bargains and also survive.
- Payoff: Cash looks like a "waste of returns" in calm times, but is priceless in a crisis. In 2008 the S&P 500 fell from its October 2007 high of 1565 to a March 2009 low of 676, a drawdown of about 57% (Argus Research); in the 2020 pandemic crash the S&P 500 fell from 3386 to 2237 in just 23 trading days, a drop of 33.9%, and triggered market-wide circuit breakers four times (Market Histories). At such moments, whoever holds cash can both add to positions at a bargain and weather an income interruption without having to sell assets cheap—cash turns from "low yield" into "high power," the only option you can always cash out in a crisis.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Argus Research (2015). Analyst Quick Notes (2007–2009 segment: S&P 500 from 1565 to 676, down 57%). <https://www.argusresearch.com/dropbox/doc/20150825_2_QuickNotes.pdf>; Market Histories (2020). The COVID-19 Market Crash: The Fastest Bear Market in History. <https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-covid-19-market-crash-the-fastest-bear-market-in-history-2020>
- Note: Cash is not "zero yield" but an insurance-style call option; too low a share leaves no bullets in a crisis, too high a share lags inflation and equities over the long run. It differs in function from the emergency fund—the emergency fund protects living, crisis cash protects opportunity and buffer.

### 3. In a crash, don't sell and don't go all-in to catch the bottom
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Emotional composure in a crash (not acting is also an action).
- In plain terms: The two most feared things in a crash: panic-selling (turning a loss real) and going all-in to catch the bottom (catching a falling knife). Adding in batches on your original plan and holding beats betting on direction.
- Payoff: Panic-selling turns "temporary loss" into "permanent loss"; a one-shot all-in bottom-fishing means catching the knife at full position if you're wrong. DALBAR's long-term tracking shows retail equity fund investors chronically underperform the index: in 2024 their return was 16.54% while the S&P 500 returned 25.05%, a gap of 848 basis points; J.P. Morgan's aggregated data also shows that holding throughout 2004–2023 returned 9.8% annualized, but missing the 10 best days dropped it to 5.6%—and the best days tend to sit right next to the worst days. The steadiest path is to add in batches on plan, not panic-sell, and let time repair, rather than using emotion to guess the turning point.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: DALBAR (2024). Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (30th Annual) (average equity investor 16.54% vs S&P 500 25.05%). <https://www.dalbar.com/?p=17875/>; Walnut Invest (2026). Investor Behavior Gap Statistics (missing the best 10 days: annualized 9.8%→5.6%). <https://walnutinvest.com/stats/investor-behavior-gap>
- Note: This entry opposes both extremes of "panic liquidation" and "full-position bottom-fishing"; it advocates disciplined batching (e.g., by preset rebalancing thresholds), not predicting the bottom—historical bottoms are only known in hindsight.

### 4. First cut spending to the bone when unemployed, then talk about investing
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Extreme thrift during the no-income period (suspend all non-essential consumption and new "timing-based add-ons").
- In plain terms: When unemployed, first cut spending and protect your principal; don't think about recovering losses through investing. Principal is your capital to turn things around; mess with it and it's really gone.
- Payoff: When income is interrupted, the top priority is to push spending down to the survival line and protect the emergency fund and investment principal from being raided. In the 2008 Great Recession U.S. unemployment rose from 5% in December 2007 to 10% in October 2009 (Argus Research); in April 2020 unemployment spiked to 14.7%, with 20.5 million jobs lost in a single month (BLS). Trying to "recover losses" through investing under such a shock almost certainly fails, because you need money exactly when assets are at a low. Cut spending first, bridge with the emergency fund; investment growth is something you do only "when you have surplus," and the order must never be reversed.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (2020). Commissioner's Statement on the Employment Situation (unemployment 14.7%, nonfarm payrolls down 20.5 million). <https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jec_05082020.htm>; Argus Research (2015). Analyst Quick Notes (2007–2009 unemployment 5%→10%). <https://www.argusresearch.com/dropbox/doc/20150825_2_QuickNotes.pdf>
- Note: During unemployment, suspend "timing-based add-ons" other than regular contributions, prioritize restoring cash flow (re-employment / side business), and avoid using leverage or early withdrawals from pensions to add to positions.

### 5. Diversify your income sources to reduce single-employer risk
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Energy and time to open a second income channel (side business, monetizing skills, rent, etc.).
- In plain terms: The whole family's money relying on one salary is betting your life on one boss. Even a very weak second source can catch you at the critical moment.
- Payoff: Relying on only one salary means betting all your human capital on one employer; when laid off or the industry shrinks there's no buffer. In the 2020 pandemic shock, U.S. total unemployment hit 14.7% in April (BLS), and Pew research showed 44.3% of non-traditional / gig workers experienced reduced hours or job loss; MIT Sloan research found that gig work like ride-hailing served as a "temporary safety net" after unemployment, lowering the probability of filing for unemployment benefits by about 4.8% and reducing debt by about 544 yuan relative. Even a very weak second source can catch you in extremes and shorten the "no-income period."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: The Pew Charitable Trusts (2020). More Than 40% of Nontraditional Workers Had Hours Cut or Lost Jobs Because of COVID-19 (44.3% affected). <https://pew.org/3n9JqOg>; MIT Sloan School of Management (2020). Gig economy serves as a safety net for unemployed (UI filing probability −4.8%, debt −$544). <http://live-mit-sloan.pantheonsite.io/press/mit-sloan-study-finds-gig-economy-serves-a-safety-net-unemployed>
- Note: The second income need not be high; the key is "low correlation with the main job, startable at any time"; avoid using leverage or high-volatility assets to chase a second income, which doubles the risk.

### 6. Set beneficiaries, will, and power of attorney in advance
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Half an hour to write down your posthumous and incapacity arrangements (fill out forms + update regularly).
- In plain terms: Who the beneficiary is, how the will divides, who decides for you if you're in a coma—spend half an hour setting these now, and your family will avoid countless pitfalls if something happens. Don't wait for the accident to think.
- Payoff: The beneficiary designation of retirement accounts (401(k), IRA) takes precedence over the will: if undesignated, the account enters probate, a lengthy process that may incur extra costs (Fidelity). The IRS is explicit that the name on the beneficiary form takes precedence over the will—"as per my will" is not accepted as a valid designation by most custodians; the spouse also has statutory priority rights. Medical power of attorney (POA) and will not set in advance mean the family faces legal friction and even inheritance disputes amid chaos. One clarification saves descendants countless troubles and emotional costs.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Internal Revenue Service (2024). Retirement Topics – Beneficiary (beneficiary form takes precedence over will). <https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-beneficiary>; Fidelity (2024). What happens to your 401(k) when you die (undesignated beneficiary enters probate). <https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/smart-money/what-happens-to-401k-when-you-die>
- Note: Be sure to update after major life events (marriage / divorce / birth / beneficiary's death); for minor beneficiaries, set up a trust or use per stirpes (by generation) clauses to avoid assets being frozen or misallocated.

### 7. Leverage wipes you out in extreme markets
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Vigilance against "leveraging for returns" (don't use margin trading and securities lending, don't borrow to trade stocks).
- In plain terms: Normally leverage makes you money fast; in extremes one reverse swing forces liquidation, principal goes to zero and you owe more. Ordinary households shouldn't touch leverage—that's a high-voltage line for professionals.
- Payoff: Leverage turns "bearable drawdown" into "zero." In 1929 retail widely used 10% margin (about 10x leverage) to buy stocks; after the crash the Dow fell from 381 to 41, a drop of 89%, and countless people were force-liquidated for failing to top up margin, with property and deposits seized (Pomegra); in 2008 Lehman's balance-sheet leverage once exceeded 30x, and a 20%+ home-price drop made it insolvent, leading to bankruptcy (KeepRule). For retail, under 10x leverage a 10% reverse swing zeroes the principal, and forced liquidation throws you out at the bottom and leaves you owing the broker—ordinary people stay away from leverage.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Pomegra (2024). Leverage: The Great Amplifier of Market Crashes (1929 Dow 381→41, −89%; banks 25–35x). <https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-f-lifecycle/market-history/chapter-01-why-history-matters/leverage-the-great-amplifier>; KeepRule (2024). Never borrow to invest (Lehman leverage over 30x, 2008 bankruptcy). <https://keeprule.com/zh/principles/never-use-leverage>
- Note: A-share margin trading, Hong Kong / U.S. financing, futures and options all count as leverage; "using a little is also safe" doesn't hold—under extreme drops of 50%+, any leverage can go to zero, and you may end up owing the institution.

### 8. In a liquidity crisis, whoever holds cash sets the price
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand cash's say in a crisis (and actually set aside cash).
- In plain terms: In a crisis everyone is short of cash and dumping; whoever holds cash calls the shots and can pick up the cheapest. Those without cash can only sell cheap. That's cash's real value in a crisis.
- Payoff: In a crisis assets are dumped and prices fail; only those holding cash can calmly take delivery or roll over; forced sellers are forced to accept the worst prices. After Lehman's 2008 bankruptcy global credit froze and money market funds "broke the buck" (the Reserve Primary Fund fell below $1), and even "safe assets" were dumped; during the 2015 A-share crash market liquidity fell below one-tenth of its pre-crash level and 16 "thousand-stock limit-down" trading days appeared, with the Shanghai Composite falling 28% over 15 trading days from June 15 to July 3 (Baidu Baike). Cash in a crisis turns from "low yield" into "high power"—when others are forced to sell cheap, you need cash to set the price.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Baidu Baike (2015). 中国式股灾 (liquidity below one-tenth of pre-crash level, 16 thousand-stock limit-down trading days). <https://baike.baidu.com/item/中国式股灾/4324647>; Argus Research (2015). Analyst Quick Notes (2008 credit market freeze, Lehman bankruptcy). <https://www.argusresearch.com/dropbox/doc/20150825_2_QuickNotes.pdf>
- Note: Liquidity drying up tends to happen exactly when you need money most; keeping cash / money market funds normally is your ticket to "not be force-liquidated and still pick up bargains" in a crisis.

### 9. Stay away from the screen in a panic; return to the plan
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Composure to check quotes less during a crash (turn off price alerts, set a fixed viewing frequency).
- In plain terms: The more it falls the more you want to look, the more you look the more you want to act, and once you act you lose. Turn off the app, return to the plan you set long ago—it doesn't expire just because it fell today.
- Payoff: Frequent quote-checking amplifies emotion and induces misoperation in a panic. The classic Barber and Odean study "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth" found that overtrading significantly drags returns through costs and timing errors; a CMC white paper citing DALBAR data points out that the average equity investor underperforms the S&P 500 by about 1–8 percentage points per year, mainly due to emotional timing. Your long-term plan doesn't change because of one day's rise or fall—turn off the quote app, return to your preset allocation and rebalancing discipline; that protects returns better than real-time watching.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: CMC Markets (2025). Inside the Mind of the Trader (citing DALBAR: investors underperform 1–8 percentage points per year). <https://cdn.cmcmarkets.com/docs/whitepaper/en-nz/CMC_Inside_the_Mind_of_the_Trader.pdf>; DALBAR (2024). Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior. <https://www.dalbar.com/?p=17875/>
- Note: Set a rule of "check once a month / quarter"; on crash days force yourself not to look at the tape, handing buy/sell decisions to the preset rebalancing thresholds rather than the day's emotions.

### 10. Write "how I survive the worst case" into your plan
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Do a worst-case scenario rehearsal in advance (write a one-page contingency plan).
- In plain terms: Write clearly in advance: six months unemployed, principal down 30%, serious illness—how to tough each out. Think through the "survival" path, and when something happens you won't panic or operate blindly.
- Payoff: The plan should state: if income stops for 6 months, if principal draws down 30%, if serious illness—how to respond respectively (which expense to cut, which reserve to tap, whether to suspend contributions). A 2020 Federal Reserve study showed that households unable to work remotely held a median of only about 3 weeks of income in accessible savings—such households "can't make it" once work stops, precisely for lack of a plan. Thinking through the "survival" path means you don't have to cut positions on impulse in a panic; the plan fixes the priority order of "survival" and "investing" in advance, so in a crisis you only execute.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2020). Financial Profiles of Workers Most Vulnerable to Coronavirus-Related Earnings Loss (FEDS 2020-093) (median only 3 weeks of income in savings). <https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020093pap.pdf>; Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (2024). An essential guide to building an emergency fund. <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>
- Note: The plan must be specific to numbers and actions, not "I'll stay calm"; redo it every year or after each major life change, and align with spouse / family.

### 11. Post-disaster recovery relies on cash + no debt, not bottom-fishing
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Calm toward the "crisis is opportunity" narrative (don't borrow to bottom-fish).
- In plain terms: Who can stand up after a crisis relies on having cash and no debt, not on catching the bottom. First protect the balance sheet from blowing up—more real than betting on a rebound.
- Payoff: For ordinary people, post-crisis resilience comes from "having cash, no leverage, no broken income," not precise bottom-fishing. In 2008 the S&P 500 didn't bottom until March 2009, down about 57%, and took years to recover; after the 2020 crash the Fed and the $2.2 trillion CARES Act backstopped it, and the S&P 500 reclaimed new highs by August (Convextrade). The households that stood up were mostly those without high-interest debt and with cash set aside before the crisis—protecting a debt-free balance sheet is more reliable than betting on a rebound, and less dependent on "perfect bottom-picking."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Convextrade (2020). Monthly Recap – March 2020 (CARES Act $2.2 trillion, S&P reclaimed new highs by August). <https://convextrade.com/recap/monthly/2020-03>; Argus Research (2015). Analyst Quick Notes (2008 S&P −57%, bottomed 2009-03). <https://www.argusresearch.com/dropbox/doc/20150825_2_QuickNotes.pdf>
- Note: Resilience = balance-sheet health, not bottom-fishing skill; in a crisis prioritize deleveraging and replenishing cash, not chasing rallies or adding bets.

### 12. On major bad news, distinguish "temporary" from "permanent"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The homework of judging the nature of bad news (first ask: is this a market scare-drop, or is the fundamentals broken).
- In plain terms: When it falls, first judge: is it a market scare-drop (will come back) or the company is really broken (won't come back). The scared-drop should be held, the truly broken should be cut. Mixing the two up is the most costly.
- Payoff: Price drops come in two kinds: "temporary" volatility from market sentiment (will return), and "permanent" loss from permanently deteriorated fundamentals (won't return). Buffett repeatedly stresses in Berkshire's annual letters the need to "distinguish price fluctuations from value destruction"—in the short run the market is a voting machine, in the long run a weighing machine, and permanent loss comes from deterioration of a business's economic character, not from quote moves. The 2020 pandemic shock was a "temporary" external shock, and the S&P 500 hit a new high within five months; the 2008 Lehman-style fundamental collapse took years to repair. Confusing the two is most fatal: cutting at the scare-drop, holding dead through the truly broken.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Berkshire Hathaway (various years). Chairman's Letter to Shareholders (market is a voting machine in the short run, a weighing machine in the long run; distinguish price from value). <https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/>; Market Histories (2020). The COVID-19 Market Crash (external shock, recovered within 5 months). <https://www.markethistories.com/en/the-covid-19-market-crash-the-fastest-bear-market-in-history-2020>
- Note: The judging basis is "is the money this company / index earns still there, still growing," not "how many more points did it fall today"; the permanent-loss risk of a single holding is far higher than a broad-market index, and diversified holding greatly reduces the probability of "permanent loss."
