# X. The Don't-Do List

The original book called this "subtract first." The moves below have negative cost-performance—cross each one off as it appears. They aren't advice; they're prohibitions. Each corresponds to a specific argument in an earlier chapter; the entry numbers in parentheses let you trace back why.

- Concentrated holdings in one or two stocks / one industry — a single-point black swan can zero out your net worth; diversify across 20–30 stocks in different industries to eliminate about 90% of single-stock risk (see Entry 2, Chapter 1, Entry 2).
- Any form of high leverage (including off-exchange margin financing) — without leverage a 50% drop still leaves you at the table; with leverage you may already be force-liquidated out, selling at the very bottom (see Chapter 1, Entry 3, Entry 6, Chapter 24).
- Trading frequently every month, treating funds like stocks — SSE data on 53.4 million accounts shows frequent traders have the highest loss probability; annual turnover above 300% can eat 15%–18% of principal in costs (see Chapter 3, Chapter 12).
- Paying to join stock-tip groups / "teacher-led" trades — unlicensed stock tipping is illegal; charging to lead trades = taking your money first, then using you as liquidity (see Chapter 1, Entry 13, Chapter 6, Entry 2).
- Chasing hot themes, hyping concepts, trading on rumors — the rumor you hear is often already the moment someone else is looking for the next bag-holder (see Chapter 1, Entry 10, Chapter 16).
- Buying "guaranteed return / principal-protected high-yield" products — the new asset-management regulations have banned principal protection and guaranteed returns; yields above 10% mean preparing to lose all principal (see Chapter 6, Entries 1, 4).
- Investing money you'll need soon (mortgage, tuition, living costs) — the market won't cooperate with your cash timeline; forced selling at the bear-market bottom is the number-one reason retail underperforms the index (see Chapter 1, Entry 4, Chapter 13).
- Acting on "insider tips" or "pre-IPO shares" — insider trading is a criminal red line, and unlisted-equity scams are rife (see Chapter 1, Entry 11, Chapter 6, Entry 7).
- Trying to time the market, guessing tops and catching bottoms — macro forecasting is near random long term, and missing the best trading days hurts returns far more than picking the wrong instrument (see Chapter 1, Entry 7, Chapter 17, Chapter 4).
- Making crypto speculation your main position, betting big on direction — domestic virtual-currency trading and hype are explicitly unprotected by law; it easily goes to zero and platforms easily run off (see Chapter 6, Entry 5).
- Holding losers forever without review, selling winners in a second — this is the disposition effect: retail systematically cuts its best positions and clings to its worst (see Chapter 3, Entry 1).
- No diversification, no allocation, no fee-watching — fees are among the few predictable return variables; a 1.5% vs 0.15% fee over 20 years can differ by about 22% (see Chapter 2, Chapter 4, Chapter 21).
- Watching the tape daily and trading on emotion — the more you watch, the more you buy high and sell low; DALBAR shows investors underperform the index for years running (see Chapter 3, Chapter 4, Entry 3).
- Borrowing / cash-advancing credit cards / consumer loans to invest — credit-card installments run about 13.8% annualized, and consumer-loan costs far exceed equities' long-term return; interest eats you first (see Chapter 2, Chapter 12).
- Treating structured deposits as high-yield deposits — for complex structured products, if you can't read the 7th layer of nesting, default to not buying (see Chapter 1, Entry 8).
- Buying annuity insurance as high-yield wealth management (without computing the IRR) — the predetermined rate is only 2.5%–3.0%; use the IRR to compute real return, not the illustrated rate (see Chapter 14).
- Treating "recruit-and-rebate" schemes as investment — pyramid-style downline recruitment is essentially a Ponzi; after collapse, you bear the principal loss yourself (see Chapter 6, Entries 3, 11).
- Topping up "investments" in apps of unknown origin — fake platforms / simulated-trading buckets bet against you; once the money's in, it doesn't come out (see Chapter 6, Entries 9, 13, 15).
- Handing your account to an unqualified individual for "guaranteed-principal managed trading" — the principal-guarantee promise itself is illegal, and runaway managers leave you unprotected (see Chapter 1, Entry 13, Chapter 6).
- Buying things you can't pass the "explainability test" — if you can't write "whose pocket the money comes from, what I know more than the market," it's a gamble (see Chapter 1, Entries 1, 9).
