# XXI. Fund Selection in Practice

The essence of fund selection is not picking "which one rose most in the past," but picking a "low-cost, style-stable, won't suddenly vanish" container. The previous chapter covered asset allocation (how much stock, how much bonds); this chapter lands on "which specific one to buy"—breaking the selection criteria into actionable, verifiable hard metrics. The vast majority lose at fund selection by looking only at historical returns and star halo, while ignoring "higher-certainty" variables like fees, size, and premium/discount.

Why these variables? Because academic and regulatory research conclusions are highly consistent: cost is the single most reliable predictor of a fund's future performance. When Morningstar testified to the U.S. Congress in 2026 it explicitly called "fees the most reliable variable in predicting a fund's future performance," and its data showed that the share of actively managed funds in the lowest-fee quintile beating their passive peer benchmark over 10 years (31%) was far higher than the highest-fee quintile (17%) (Morningstar, 2026); and SPIVA's long-run tracking shows that the share of U.S. large-cap actively managed funds underperforming the S&P 500 reached 85.59% over 10 years and 92.89% over 20 years (S&P Dow Jones Indices, 2026). This means "pay less in fees, avoid the systematic disadvantage of active management" is the easiest win-rate source for ordinary investors to grasp.

Entries in this section are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses): I. Cost and fees—the first gate of fund selection: Entry 1 (index funds: three checks—fees, size, tracking error), Entry 3 (Class A vs Class C: holding period decides which is cheaper), Entry 4 (buying an ETF at a premium = losing a layer upfront), Entry 5 (QDII premium can reach 5%–20% when quotas are tight), Entry 9 (FOF: double-layer fees, choose with caution); II. Size and survival risk: Entry 2 (avoid both extremes of fund size), Entry 11 (the "actual holdings" disclosed in periodic reports beat the marketing); III. Hidden risks of actively managed funds: Entry 6 (manager change is a hidden risk), Entry 7 (style drift: look at holdings, not the name), Entry 8 (IPO-subscription / arbitrage fund returns are unsustainable); IV. Product identification and bond funds: Entry 10 (bond funds also come in tiers).

### 1. Picking an index fund: three checks—fees, size, tracking error
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Compare peer index funds on three items—fees, size, tracking error—and pick "three lows and one big" (low fee, low error, low premium, large size).
- In plain terms: Index funds track the same index; they are essentially homogeneous "containers," and long-run return differences come almost entirely from cost. Pick ones with large size (good liquidity, low risk of liquidation), low fees (lower combined management + custody fee is better), and small tracking error (smaller annualized deviation from the index is better); all other marketing talk is noise.
- Payoff: In 2026 Morningstar testified to the U.S. Congress stating explicitly that "fees are the most reliable variable in predicting a fund's future performance," and its conclusion holds across all asset classes and time periods (Morningstar, 2026). Empirically, over the 10 years through end-2025, 31% of actively managed funds in the lowest-fee quintile beat their passive peer benchmark, versus only 17% in the highest-fee quintile. Take China's broad-market ETFs as an example: management fees have broadly fallen from 0.5% in the industry's early days to 0.15% (custody fee 0.05%), making "0.15%+0.05%" the mainstream; Wind data shows that by end-2025, 384 ETFs had cut management fees to 0.15% (China Economic Net, 2026). Assuming 7% annualized return, cutting fees from 0.5% to 0.2% leaves about 18% more per 1,000,000 yuan after 30 years (compounding difference)—that's why fee ranks first among the "three checks."
- Evidence grade: A

Morningstar to Congress: Investor Success Hinges on Lower Costs, Greater Transparency (testimony before the U.S. House Financial Services Committee Subcommittee on Capital Markets, "From Wall Street to Main Street: The Future of How America Invests," 2026-06-25). <https://www.morningstar.com/funds/morningstar-congress-investor-success-hinges-lower-costs-greater-transparency>; China Economic Net (2026). 1500 多只 ETF 同台竞技 谁更胜一筹. <https://www.ce.cn/macro/more/202606/t20260603_3007591.shtml>; S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026). SPIVA Institutional Scorecard Year-End 2025. <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-institutional-scorecard-year-end-2025.pdf>
- Note: Tracking error refers to the annualized volatility of a fund's return relative to its benchmark index; it is the core metric for judging an index fund's "replication precision"; in Morningstar's 2026 updated Medalist rating, the "Process" pillar weighs highest for passive funds, focusing on the volatility of excess return versus the benchmark, i.e., tracking error (Morningstar, 2026 Medalist methodology). Fees and tracking error must be viewed together; a "cheap" fund with low fee but large error may not be a bargain.

### 2. Avoid both extremes of fund size
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Avoid mini-funds (liquidation risk) and oversized actively managed funds (hard to beat index); for index funds watch the lower-size bound, for actively managed funds the upper bound.
- In plain terms: Too small a size hits the liquidation red line; too large (especially active) is a ship too big to turn, hard to keep beating the index. Index funds can be more lenient; don't chase an oversized actively managed fund either.
- Payoff: Article 41 of the Measures for the Administration of the Operation of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds stipulates that if an open-end fund for 60 consecutive working days has "fewer than 200 fund unit holders" or "net asset value below 50 million yuan," the fund manager must report to the CSRC and propose solutions such as converting the operation mode, merging, or terminating the contract (CSRC, 2014/2026 current). In practice "mini-funds" are the main force for liquidation, and some fund contracts set 200 million yuan as an automatic liquidation line (e.g., CICC MSCI China A-share Dividend A terminates if net value is below 200 million after 3 years of effect), while sponsored funds trigger termination if net value is below 200 million three years later (China Economic Net, 2022). At the other end, an overly large actively managed size often drags excess returns—SPIVA data shows that by end-2025 the share of U.S. large-cap actively managed funds underperforming the S&P 500 reached 85.59% over 10 years and 92.89% over 20 years (S&P, 2026), and super-large size makes alpha even harder. Practical advice: index fund size is best above 2 billion (ample liquidity, low liquidation probability), and be wary of "giants" at the tens-of-billions level for actively managed funds.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2014/2026). - Note: Liquidation itself doesn't equal principal loss (liquidation returns remaining assets pro rata), but it interrupts the investment plan and incurs tax and reallocation costs; the size-performance negative correlation doesn't hold for index funds (the larger the index fund, the more stable), and the "size curse" only needs warning for actively managed funds. [Grade rationale: the liquidation rule is A-level hard regulation, but size-performance negative correlation is a statistical regularity, hence combined grade B.]

### 3. Class A vs Class C: holding period decides which is cheaper
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Do the math by your expected holding period before deciding A or C; use "subscription fee ÷ annualized sales-service fee rate" to find the break-even point.
- In plain terms: Don't listen to "Class A is always better." Class A charges a front-end subscription fee and no sales-service fee; Class C charges no subscription fee but a sales-service fee. For holding 3+ years pick A, for short trades pick C—first compute the break-even.
- Payoff: The 2025 year-end CSRC-revised Measures for the Administration of Sales Fees of Publicly Offered Securities Investment Funds flattened and lowered the caps on subscription/subscription fees and sales-service fees: actively managed equity-oriented subscription fee no higher than 0.8%, other mixed no higher than 0.5%, index and bond no higher than 0.3% (CSRC, [2025] No. 22, Article 8); share classes that don't charge subscription fees may charge a sales-service fee, with equity / mixed cap 0.4%/year, index / bond cap 0.2%/year, and non-money funds held over 1 year must not keep charging the sales-service fee (Article 11). Under this framework, the A/C break-even ≈ subscription fee ÷ annualized sales-service fee rate. For example, if an index fund's Class A subscription fee is 0.3% (often discounted to 0.15% or 0), and Class C sales-service fee is 0.2%/year, the pure holding-cost break-even is about 1.5 years; if Class A subscription fee is discounted to 0.03% and Class C is 0.2%/year, the break-even is only about 0.15 years, and almost any holding should pick A. Conclusion: long-term holding (>2 years) Class A almost always wins, short-term (<1 year) Class C is cheaper; be sure to compute using the fund's official fee schedule.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2025). ### 4. Buying an ETF at a premium = losing a layer upfront
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Check the premium/discount at the quote before trading (compare to the IOPV net value), and only place orders when the premium rate is near 0.
- In plain terms: An ETF has two prices: the net value (IOPV, estimated every 15 seconds from constituent stock prices) and the market price. If the market price is higher than the net value, that's a premium; buying then is like giving money away first; buy at a discount to pick up a bargain.
- Payoff: An ETF's "net value" (IOPV) and "market price" (secondary-market trading price) theoretically converge via the arbitrage mechanism, but short-term supply-demand imbalance creates premiums/discounts. Mainstream broad-market ETFs (e.g., CSI 300 ETF, CSI A500 ETF) have premiums/discounts mostly within ±0.1%–0.5% thanks to large size and ample market-making (Huatai-PineBridge and others have management+custody fees as low as 0.15%+0.05%, see China Economic Net, 2026), negligible for long-term holding. But chasing in at a high premium equals "losing a layer upfront": if you buy 2% above net value, you need the premium to converge or net value to rise 2% just to break even. The monitoring method is simple—the quote software directly shows the "premium rate," or compare the bid-1 price with the IOPV. Recommended: regular contributors only place orders within ±0.3% premium; in extreme markets (e.g., 2020 oil QDII, small-cap ETF speculation) premiums can spike to several percentage points or more—avoid them.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: China Economic Net (2026). 1500 多只 ETF 同台竞技 谁更胜一筹. <https://www.ce.cn/macro/more/202606/t20260603_3007591.shtml>; Huatai-PineBridge Fund (2025). CSI 300 ETF (510300) / A500 ETF (563360) product legal documents (management fee 0.15%/year, custody fee 0.05%/year). <https://finance.sina.com.cn/jjxw/2025-11-24/doc-infynrpa6640545.shtml>
- Note: The premium/discount magnitude is closely tied to liquidity, quotas, and constituent trading halts; an ETF's arbitrage mechanism can temporarily fail when constituents are halted or FX quotas are restricted (see Entry 5, QDII), and a high premium may not converge for weeks—don't mechanically bottom-fish.

### 5. QDII premium can reach 5%–20% when quotas are tight
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Avoid QDII funds with suspended quotas and high premiums; before buying check the fund announcement "whether subscription is suspended" and the quote's "premium rate."
- In plain terms: You want to buy an overseas fund but hit "subscription suspended + high premium"—rather wait for quotas to reopen than catch the top of the premium.
- Payoff: QDII funds invest overseas using FX quotas; when quotas are exhausted and subscription is suspended while investors can still trade in the secondary market, supply-demand imbalance pushes the market price away from net value, forming a high premium. The 2020 oil crash triggered a "bottom-fishing" frenzy; after several oil QDII funds suspended subscription in March, premiums skyrocketed: E Fund Crude Oil (161129) closed 104.03% above net value on 2020-04-24, Southern Crude Oil (501018) 109.22% on April 28, and Harvest Crude Oil (160723) 70.69% on April 24 (Sina Finance, 2020); E Fund Crude Oil still carried a 27.50% premium as of 2020-06-18 (China Securities Journal, 2020-06-23). That is, under "subscription suspended + tight quotas," the premium can reach 5%–100% or more, and chasers simply give money away. Even outside extremes, when quotas are marginally tight QDII premiums often reach 2%–5%. Countermeasure: enter after quotas reopen and the premium falls back (toward 0); for long-term overseas allocation, prefer ample-quota, large-size QDII or mutual recognition funds.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Sina Finance (2020-04-29). 最高溢价超 100% 3 只原油 QDII 基金集体停牌. <https://finance.sina.cn/money/lczx/2020-04-29/detail-iircuyvi0561172.d.html?cid=76646&vt=4>; China Securities Journal (2020-06-23). 易方达原油证券投资基金(QDII) 溢价风险提示公告. <https://epaper.cs.com.cn/zgzqb/html/2020-06/23/nw.D110000zgzqb_20200623_4-B060.htm>
- Note: A QDII high premium is essentially an emotional premium of "want to buy but can't"; it converges quickly once quotas reopen or the fund resumes subscription, unrelated to the underlying asset's rise or fall; therefore buying at the premium top is pure frictional loss, not an investment judgment.

### 6. Manager change is a hidden risk
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Watch the manager-change announcements of funds you hold (fund company website / CSRC disclosure platform); before buying check the "current manager's tenure."
- In plain terms: When you buy an actively managed fund, you're buying "a certain person can make money," not "a certain code can make money." Once the manager changes, the reference value of past performance drops sharply.
- Payoff: An actively managed fund's "alpha" is highly tied to the specific manager. SPIVA's Persistence Scorecard has long proven that past excellent performance is extremely hard to sustain: by end-2025, of the U.S. large-cap funds in the top quartile at end-2021, "0.00%" remained in the top quartile at end-2025 (S&P Dow Jones Indices, U.S. Persistence Scorecard Year-End 2025)—so-called "star managers'" consecutive outperformance is nearly random. Coupled with personnel changes, once a core manager leaves, the original strategy, attribution, and risk exposure may all change, and the historical performance basically loses its reference value. Morningstar research shows a fund manager's average tenure is positively correlated with the fund family's rating, and high-rated parent companies' 5-year manager retention often exceeds 90% (Morningstar, 2026 Parent data). Practical advice: prefer products with tenure ≥5 years and a stable team; set manager-change announcements as a watch item, re-evaluate after a change, and never treat "past three-year champion" as a future guarantee.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard / U.S. Persistence Scorecard Year-End 2025. <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/spiva/article/spiva-us>; Morningstar (2026). Parent-Level Medalist Data Points (manager tenure and retention). <https://www.morningstar.com/en-gb/business/insights/blog/parent-pillar-data-points>
- Note: Index funds are unaffected by this risk (driven by rules, not people)—another big advantage of passive over active; for actively managed funds, whether the "investment objective/strategy" in the contract changes with the manager matters more to read than historical net value.

### 7. Style drift: look at holdings, not the name
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Periodically (after quarterly disclosure) check the top-ten holdings and industry distribution against the performance benchmark stated in the contract.
- In plain terms: Don't trust the fund's name. Click open the holdings each quarter; if you find "a blue-chip fund full of new energy" or "a steady fund heavy in small-caps," be alert—it has drifted from your allocation intent.
- Payoff: A fund's name is marketing; the actual holdings are the truth. Style drift means a fund's actual investment style deviates from its claimed / benchmark style, which undermines the investor's carefully built asset allocation: you thought you bought a "large-cap value" ballast, but it quietly went all-in on growth stocks, silently amplifying the portfolio's overall risk exposure. In its 2026 updated Medalist rating, Morningstar included "style drift" alongside volatility in the quantitative scoring of actively managed funds (Morningstar, 2026 Medalist methodology), showing its importance. The identification method is simple: ① read the top-ten heavy stocks and industry weights in the quarterly "portfolio report"; ② compare against the "investment scope / performance benchmark" stated in the fund contract; ③ use Morningstar's Style Box to see whether it's actually classified as large/mid/small-cap, value/growth. If it clearly deviates from the benchmark for two consecutive quarters, treat it as a warning and consider rebalancing or switching to an index fund with harder style constraints.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Morningstar (2026). Morningstar adds fee and "fund manager success" to updated Medalist Rating methodology (including style drift metrics). <https://portfolio-adviser.com/morningstar-adds-fee-and-fund-manager-success-to-updated-medalist-rating-methodology>; Securities Times (2024). 《微观量化百问》第十期——基金产品风险收益特征的常用评价指标. <https://stcn.com/article/detail/1322422.html>
- Note: Style drift is sometimes the result of managers "herding into hot themes"; it's hard to notice in a bull market and amplifies drawdowns in a bear; index funds, constrained by tracking error, drift far less than actively managed funds, with stronger allocation discipline.

### 8. IPO-subscription / arbitrage fund returns are unsustainable
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Don't pay for "last year's IPO-subscription / arbitrage returns"; before buying, break down the return source and strip the IPO enhancement out of the net value to see the underlying's true ability.
- In plain terms: A fund that earned 3% extra from IPO subscription last year may lose it back this year. Treating a one-time dividend as permanent ability is a common misjudgment.
- Payoff: Strategies like offline IPO subscription, index-futures discount arbitrage, and block-trade discount depend heavily on market institutions and funding conditions, with obvious "phasing." During the 2020–2021 STAR Market / ChiNext IPO-subscription boom, some "IPO + fixed income" funds saw annualized enhancement of 3%–8%, but as more institutions joined and the new-stock break-issue rate rose, the enhancement decayed rapidly. After the registration-based IPO system was implemented, break-issues became normal—the 2022 break-issue rate was about 28% and fell to about 3% in 2024 (different statistical caliber)—the key is not the single-year break-issue rate, but that "IPO-subscription return ≠ permanent alpha." Once a strategy gets crowded or rules change (e.g., lock-up, pricing rules), this portion of return goes to zero or even gives back due to underlying volatility. Therefore, when evaluating funds with IPO-subscription / arbitrage components, strip the "IPO enhancement" out and look at the underlying's true ability; if a fund's selling point is "IPO magic," it mostly can't serve as a long-term core holding.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Securities Times (2024). 《微观量化百问》第十期——基金产品风险收益特征的常用评价指标 (risk-return not simply extrapolatable). <https://stcn.com/article/detail/1322422.html>; Xinhua Net (2026-01-04). 销售费用管理新规出台 推动公募基金高质量发展 (fee-reform background). <https://www.news.cn/20260104/32676ea0f91440c498b90be39732c07a/c.html>
- Note: The cited "about 28% in 2022, about 3% in 2024" break-issue rates are illustrative industry statistics; specific figures vary by caliber; the conclusion is qualitative—one-time strategy dividends can't be extrapolated into long-term returns, and core holdings should rest on repeatable underlying ability. [Grade rationale: return decay is a market regularity, but magnitude and timing are hard to extrapolate precisely.]

### 9. FOF: double-layer fees, choose with caution
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: See clearly the "parent fund fee + sub-fund fee" stacking of a FOF; compute the combined holding cost first.
- In plain terms: A FOF picks funds for you but charges twice. If you can buy two or three low-fee index funds yourself, a FOF is mostly a pure markup.
- Payoff: A FOF (fund of funds) adds another layer of parent-fund management and custody fees on top of the underlying fund fees, forming "double charging." Under the 2025 new rules, the caps on sales-service and subscription fees were lowered (index / bond sales-service fee ≤0.2%/year, see CSRC [2025] No. 22), but the parent fund's management fee still typically runs 0.6%–1.0%/year; stacked with the sub-fund's 0.15%–0.5%/year, the combined holding cost often reaches 1%–1.5%/year, far above directly buying two or three 0.2%-fee broad-market ETFs (Caixin Global, 2026 notes China already has 370+ ETFs with comprehensive fees as low as 0.2%/year). Morningstar's testimony also stresses that fees are the most reliable predictor of future performance, and higher cost drags long-run compounding more (Morningstar, 2026). A FOF's value holds only when "it does asset allocation / fund selection for you" and that ability truly beats the double charging—for investors who can allocate low-fee index funds themselves, a FOF is mostly a pure markup. Advice: compute the combined fee first, then ask yourself "is its fund-selection / rebalancing ability worth the extra 0.8–1.3 percentage points per year?"

### 10. Bond funds also come in tiers
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Distinguish pure-bond / first-tier / second-tier / convertible-bond funds; look at the stock + convertible-bond share in the prospectus's "investment scope."
- In plain terms: The word "steady" doesn't cover all bond funds. Pure bonds are nearly steady; a convertible-bond fund can swing 20% in a year. Check how much equity it holds before buying.
- Payoff: By "equity share," bond funds differ wildly in risk-return: ① pure-bond funds (bonds only) typically have 1%–3% annualized volatility and max drawdown mostly within 2%, roughly "steady"; ② first-tier bonds (can do IPO subscription / convertible bonds) see slightly higher volatility; ③ second-tier bonds (up to 20% in stocks) have about 3%–6% annualized volatility and max drawdown up to 5%–10%; ④ convertible-bond funds, due to underlying-stock attributes, can have 15%–25% annualized volatility, and a single-year drawdown over 20% is not rare (highly linked to the stock market). The most direct way to judge: read the fund contract's "investment scope / ratio"—the higher the stock + convertible-bond share, the closer the risk is to equities. Regulators also price by asset class differently: index / bond subscription-fee cap 0.3%, sales-service fee cap 0.2%/year (CSRC [2025] No. 22), but that's a "fee" classification, not a "risk" classification, and can't be used to judge volatility. Conclusion: use pure-bond / short-duration bonds for the "ballast"; accept second-tier bonds for enhanced return; treat convertible-bond funds as "stock-like," don't be misled by the word "bond."
- Evidence grade: B

### 11. The "actual holdings" disclosed in periodic reports beat the marketing
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Each year read the quarterly / annual "portfolio report" and "operation analysis" (CSRC disclosure platform eid.csrc.gov.cn).
- In plain terms: Marketing pages say pretty things; quarterly reports only tell the truth. Spending ten minutes on the quarterly report is more useful than reading ten soft articles.
- Payoff: Fund periodic reports (quarterly / semi-annual / annual) are a regulator-mandated, audited source of "truth," including top-ten holdings, full holdings (semi-annual / annual), bond duration, stock industry distribution, share changes, and operation review—enough to spot "say-do mismatch": e.g., claiming "value investing" but heavy in herded growth, claiming "low volatility" but full of convertible bonds. By contrast, marketing materials and sales pages selectively display and have low reference value. Identification path: ① download the latest report from the CSRC Capital Market Electronic Disclosure Platform (eid.csrc.gov.cn) or the fund company website; ② focus on the top-ten heavy holdings in the "portfolio report" and the "industry-classified stock portfolio"; ③ read the "manager's report" operation analysis and market outlook to judge whether the manager practices what he preaches. Spend ten minutes once a year to catch drift early—finding style drift (see Entry 7), size shrinkage (see Entry 2), or manager change (see Entry 6) should all be verified back in the report. This is the lowest-cost, hardest-information link in "ongoing tracking" after fund selection.
- Evidence grade: C
