# IX. Life Stages

Investing isn't a one-size-fits-all recipe; it's a route that shifts with age, responsibilities, and cash flow. The risk a person can bear at 25 and at 55 is worlds apart: when young, your biggest asset is the present value of decades of future wages (human capital), and time is enough to smooth out market swings, so you should tilt toward equities; later, human capital is exhausted and you're near or in the withdrawal phase, so you should gradually trade volatility for certainty. Academia calls this "life-cycle investing"; the most popular simplified version in practice is the "100 minus your age" rule—subtract your age from 100 to get the rough share of equities in your portfolio.

But age is only the first axis. Real family financial resilience also depends on the "responsibility structure": single or dual income, kids or not, self-employed or not, in a remarriage/blended family or not. The heavier the responsibility and the less stable the income, the thicker the emergency fund, the more complete the insurance, and the earlier the education fund should be isolated. At any life stage, one order can never be reversed—secure the base first (emergency fund, protection, risk-free buffer), then attack (equities, long-term compounding). This guide treats "secure the base first, then attack" as the underlying logic of all its entries.

The reality of Chinese households also patches this route. The People's Bank of China's 2023 urban depositor survey shows the top three favored investment methods are still "bank/securities/insurance wealth-management products" (43.8%), "funds and trusts" (20.4%), and "stocks" (15.2%); the Chinese Academy of Social Sciences estimates that in 2023 about 62% of household financial assets were cash and deposits and only about 9.4% were stocks. In other words, most households don't have "too much equity"—they broadly under-allocate to equities and pension savings while overloading real estate and deposits. So while this chapter cites international rules of thumb, it repeatedly reminds: for most Chinese households the real gap to fill is the "long-term equities + private pension" shortfall, not blindly levering up for yield.

The entries below are grouped by theme (entry numbers in parentheses): age-stage line (Entries 1–4: ages 20–35, 35–55, 55–65, post-retirement); family-structure variants (Entries 5, 8–12: single-income, single-no-kids, with kids, dual-income, self-employed, remarriage/blended); cross-stage themes (Entries 6, 7, 13: financial literacy inheritance, emergency fund, longevity risk).

### 1. Young (20–35): high equity share + pour into human capital
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Put your time and energy into your main job's skills and career compounding, not watching the tape and trading short-term; use monthly fixed savings for regular investing, not consumer spending.
- In plain terms: When young you can afford to lose time—don't fear volatility yet. Your biggest asset now is yourself—decades of future earning power, not the few tens of thousands of yuan in your account. Ramp up equity share and harden your skills; that matters ten times more than researching which fund rises fastest.
- Payoff: For the young, "total wealth" = financial assets + human capital, where human capital (present value of future wages) is the largest share and can self-insure via working more and retiring later; so the theoretical optimum is to put a high share (about 70%–90%) of financial wealth into equities, using a 30–50 year horizon to absorb swings and earn the equity risk premium. By the "100 minus your age" rule, 25 gives 75% equities, 35 gives 65%. The compounding gap is enormous: start monthly investing of 2,000 yuan at age 25 at 7% annualized and you accumulate about 3.8 million yuan by 60 (nominal); start the same amount at 35 and you get only about 1.8 million—the missing 2 million comes almost entirely from "a ten-year-later compounding start," not from return differences.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2007). The Theory of Life-Cycle Saving and Investing (working paper, systematically citing the Bodie, Merton & Samuelson 1992 life-cycle model). <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0703.pdf>; Pomegra (no date). The 100-Minus-Age Heuristic (origin and logic of the "100 minus your age" rule). <https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-c-strategies/first-portfolio/chapter-03-asset-allocation-the-most-important-decision/the-100-minus-age-heuristic>
- Note: The model assumes human capital is "bond-like" (low risk). If your income is itself strongly correlated with the stock market (e.g., finance or real-estate workers), your optimal equity share should be below the rule's value—see Entry 11; graded A rests on mature academic consensus, but the proportions are experience ranges, not precise prescriptions.

### 2. Middle age (35–55): allocation + insurance + education fund
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Regular premiums (life/critical-illness/medical/accident insurance), a child's education-fund regular investing, and the time/energy for annual rebalancing and policy review.
- In plain terms: At this stage you're not alone—you're the family's defense line. Drop equity share by the "100 minus your age" rule to about 45%–65% (65% at 35, 45% at 55), buy complete insurance, and isolate the child's education fund separately. Stop going all-in on a single bet; when responsibility is heaviest, what you fear most is one loss wiping out all.
- Payoff: Ages 35–55 are the peak of family responsibility (mortgage, children, elderly support stacked together); replacing the young single high-equity tilt with a steady "equities + bonds + protection" mix sharply lowers the odds that "unemployment + illness + bear market" simultaneously pierce your cash flow. Here insurance is not a return tool but a "stop-loss line": a term-life policy can leverage thousands of yuan in annual premium into a million-level death benefit, sparing the family from being forced to sell assets at the bottom after the breadwinner falls. The education fund, via a separate account's regular investing, spreads a rigid 15–20-year-ahead expense forward across time, avoiding a bear market exactly at the due year.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2007). The Theory of Life-Cycle Saving and Investing. <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0703.pdf>; Survey and Statistics Department, People's Bank of China (2023). 2023 Q2 Urban Depositor Survey Report (residents' top planned increased spending in three months: "education" at 27.1%). <https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2023/0629/c1004-40024314.html>
- Note: The exact equity share should float ±10 percentage points with income stability and risk appetite (stable civil-service pay can be slightly higher, volatile income lower); graded B because the proportions are rules of thumb lacking a strict empirical optimum for Chinese households. Cross-reference: Entries 5, 9, 10.

### 3. Approaching retirement (55–65): lower volatility year by year
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Actively give up the last stretch of upside, and over 5–10 years shift the portfolio gradually from equities to bonds/deposits/annuities.
- In plain terms: Near retirement the worst fear is "getting trapped right at the finish line." Ten years ahead, slowly swap stocks for bonds—trade time for safety; don't gamble the last leg. What you want is a retirement income you can reliably draw, not inflated paper gains in the account.
- Payoff: Use a 5–10 year glide path to smoothly lower equity share from about 45% to 30%–40%, dodging the fatal drawdown of "a bear market the year before retirement." Both academia and product practice support this: the target-date fund (TDF) is the classic standardized product that "auto-lowers volatility with age," and its glide path typically keeps 35%–50% equities at retirement to hedge longevity and inflation. Conversely, if at 60 you're still fully in equities and the next year brings a 2008-style −40% drawdown, retirement principal shrinks 40% at once, with no wages left to fall back on and a very short recovery window.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2007). The Theory of Life-Cycle Saving and Investing (life-cycle risk-reduction path). <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0703.pdf>; Pomegra (no date). The 100-Minus-Age Rule (glide path vs. target-date funds). <https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-c-strategies/passive-investing/chapter-12-asset-allocation-glide-paths/07-100-minus-age-rule>
- Note: If pensions (social security + enterprise/private pension) already cover basic living, you can keep higher equities to fight inflation; graded A rests on life-cycle theory and TDF evidence, but the glide pace needs personal adjustment by pension replacement rate.

### 4. Post-retirement: cash flow first
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Accept some return certainty—replace high-volatility equity positions with bond interest, dividends, annuities, and social security.
- In plain terms: After retirement the account is an ATM, not a casino. Keep 1–2 years of living expenses in a money-market fund/deposit for instant access; cover the rest with cash flow (dividends, bond coupons, annuities, pensions). Never cut positions at a market low to pull cash.
- Payoff: The crux of a retirement portfolio is the "sustainable withdrawal rate." Bengen (1994), backtesting 1926–1992 U.S. stock data, proposed the "4% rule": withdraw 4% of portfolio value in the first retirement year, then raise it with inflation each year; it never failed across 30-year retirements, implying "you need 25 times annual spending at retirement." The Financial Planning Association's 2023 recalculation using bond indices still put SAFEMAX near 4.5%. In other words, with 120,000 yuan annual spending you need about 3 million yuan in investable assets to last 30 years. Keeping a 1–2 year cash buffer avoids being forced to sell stocks cheap in the bear market's first year, triggering a principal spiral.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bengen, W. (1994). Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data, Journal of Financial Planning (the 4% rule original; author's own recap). <https://www.bengenfs.com/the-4-percent-rule/>; Financial Planning Association (2023). Revisiting William Bengen's 'SAFEMAX' Portfolio Withdrawal Rate (SAFEMAX about 4.5%). <https://www.financialplanningassociation.org/learning/publications/journal/NOV23-revisiting-william-bengens-safemax-portfolio-withdrawal-rate-OPEN>
- Note: The 4% rule is based on U.S. history and ignores taxes/fees and medical inflation; China's risk-free rate is lower and out-of-pocket medical share higher, so the real safe withdrawal rate may be below 4%—supplement with social security and commercial pension insurance. See Entry 13.

### 5. Single-income household: secure the breadwinner before returns
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: First equip the earner with complete term life, critical-illness, medical, and accident insurance; premiums take a small slice of the family budget.
- In plain terms: When the whole family's income rides on one person, secure that person first. Don't put the cart before the horse by buying kids a pile of savings-insurance while the breadwinner runs naked—if the breadwinner falls, you may not even afford the kids' savings-insurance premiums.
- Payoff: A single-income household has no "second paycheck" to fall back on; once the breadwinner dies or is disabled, family cash flow drops to zero instantly and the portfolio must be liquidated at the bottom. A term-life policy with thousands of yuan in annual premium can leverage a million-level death benefit, transferring the tail risk of "income interruption" to the insurer. The iron rule of coverage order: the earner before the non-earner, protection-type before savings-type. The PBOC's 2023 survey shows residents still favor wealth-management (43.8%) far above insurance (13%)—precisely the pit single-income households most easily fall into: "heavy on yield, light on protection."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Survey and Statistics Department, People's Bank of China (2023). 2023 Q2 Urban Depositor Survey Report (insurance only 13.0% of planned increased spending). <https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2023/0629/c1004-40024314.html>; U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (no date). An essential guide to building an emergency fund (household financial resilience principles). <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>
- Note: Graded B because insurance priority is general financial-planning common sense rather than strict empirical research; recommended coverage is 5–10 times annual income plus outstanding mortgage. Cross-reference: Entries 7, 11.

### 6. Raising children: financial independence before wealth inheritance
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Invest time and energy in building the child's independent earning ability, money management, and scam-awareness—not just saving money.
- In plain terms: Leaving skills beats leaving money. Teaching a child "where money comes from, how not to be scammed, how compounding rolls" protects them for life more than handing them an apartment. A child who can earn and understands compounding is harder to fleece and more resilient than one who passively inherits.
- Payoff: From a human-capital view, parents' biggest "investment" in a child is education and financial literacy, not inheritance. Surveys like CHFS show Chinese household assets are heavily concentrated in real estate and deposits with uneven financial literacy, and many "rich second-generation" rapidly shrink their wealth after inheriting due to weak independent earning and risk-control ability. By contrast, children with compounding awareness and career competitiveness have a far higher present value of lifetime income than a one-time inheritance. Behavioral-finance research also shows savings and budgeting habits formed early significantly persist into adulthood, lowering the chance of later debt traps.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Survey and Research Center for China Household Finance, Southwestern University of Finance and Economics (2023). China Household Finance Survey Report (bank deposits 57.75%, stocks 15.45% of household financial assets, concentrated allocation). <https://www.sgpjbg.com.cn/searchtag/25973332.html>; Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2007). The Theory of Life-Cycle Saving and Investing (intergenerational human-capital perspective). <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0703.pdf>
- Note: Graded C because "financial-literacy inheritance" lacks quantifiable hard evidence like the 4% rule, being more an inference from behavioral economics and intergenerational-mobility research; it complements Entry 9's education-fund planning—give money and teach ability.

### 7. Emergency fund adjusts dynamically with family responsibility
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: No extra spending—just move the portion of existing savings recalculated by months of family responsibility into a high-liquidity account (money-market fund/deposit).
- In plain terms: Single, 3 months is enough; with elderly and kids above and a mortgage on your back, you need 6–12 months. When responsibility changes, the cushion must thicken with it—don't get forced to sell stocks at the bottom to plug holes during an unemployment + illness + mortgage triple hit.
- Payoff: The emergency fund is the family's financial "shock absorber." The U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) explicitly recommends covering 3–6 months of essential expenses; for unstable-income or single-income/self-employed households it further recommends 6–12 months. Concrete and actionable: stable dual-income no-kids → 3 months; single-income with mortgage and kids → 6 months; self-employed/volatile industry → 6–12 months. At 10,000 yuan monthly essential spending, 6 months = 60,000 yuan in a money-market fund—barely affecting compounding normally, yet avoiding fire-sale of long-term assets or high-interest debt in a crisis.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (no date). An essential guide to building an emergency fund (recommends 3–6 months of essential expenses, higher for unstable income). <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>; Survey and Statistics Department, People's Bank of China (2023). 2023 Q2 Urban Depositor Survey Report (savings inclination 58%, high precautionary saving). <https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2023/0629/c1004-40024314.html>
- Note: The emergency fund doesn't conflict with "investment principal"—it's cash management, not a return tool; graded A on clear regulatory guidance. Cross-reference: Entries 5, 11.

### 8. Single, no kids: high equity share + high savings rate is the golden window
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Put energy into your main job to raise income, push the savings rate as high as possible (target 30%–50%), and cut needless consumption.
- In plain terms: Single, your only "burden" is yourself, so you can max out equity share and savings rate. The principal you pile up in this decade is the floor of all future security—after marriage and kids, this money gets gradually siphoned off by mortgage, education fund, and insurance.
- Payoff: The burden-free period is the decade of highest principal-accumulation efficiency. By the "100 minus your age" rule, ages 25–35 correspond to 65%–75% equities, and risk tolerance is strongest then. More crucial is the savings rate: at 15,000 yuan monthly salary, 40% savings (6,000/month saved), 7% annualized, you can accumulate about 1.04 million yuan in 10 years; start the same rate at 35 after marriage and you get about the same 1.04 million over 10 years but have missed the earlier decade's lower spending and higher time-compounding window. The single-period high-savings + high-equity combo is buying the long-term-compounding ticket at the lowest responsibility cost.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2007). The Theory of Life-Cycle Saving and Investing. <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0703.pdf>; Pomegra (no date). The 100-Minus-Age Heuristic. <https://pomegra.io/learn/library/track-c-strategies/first-portfolio/chapter-03-asset-allocation-the-most-important-decision/the-100-minus-age-heuristic>
- Note: Graded A on life-cycle theory and the dominant role of savings rate in wealth accumulation (Brinson et al. find asset allocation explains ~80%–90% of long-term return differences); high equity share still presupposes tolerance for volatility. See Entry 1.

### 9. Household with kids: isolate the education fund separately
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Open a separate account dedicated to the education fund, invest a fixed amount monthly, and don't mix it with daily-spending or stock-trading accounts.
- In plain terms: Your child's tuition can't mix with your stock-trading account. Separate account, earmarked use; don't let the due year land in a bear market—education spending is a rigid timetable, it can't wait.
- Payoff: A child's education fund is a rigid liability with "a fixed due date and a sizable amount." Isolating it from the main portfolio avoids threefold misuse risk: spent on daily consumption, eroded by investment swings, or pulled for emergencies. In practice use a "target-date" approach—at the child's age 0 equity can be high (70%+), lowering to low volatility as college approaches; if you need 500,000 yuan in 18 years, back-solving at 6% annualized needs only about 1,300 yuan/month—far more relaxed than scrambling near the deadline. The PBOC's 2023 survey also shows "education" ranks first in residents' planned increased spending for three months running (27.1%), confirming its rigid priority.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Survey and Statistics Department, People's Bank of China (2023). 2023 Q2 Urban Depositor Survey Report (education first at 27.1%). <https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2023/0629/c1004-40024314.html>; U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (no date). An essential guide to building an emergency fund (earmarking and cash-flow matching principles). <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>
- Note: Graded B because education-fund planning is goal-oriented financial planning lacking a single hard empirical study; the isolation account is best a low-fee index + bond-fund mix that lowers volatility toward the due date. Cross-reference: Entries 2, 6.

### 10. Dual-income household: more fault tolerance, but don't run naked together
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Both partners get basic protection; the emergency fund can be slightly lower than a single-income household's (about 3–4 months).
- In plain terms: Two paychecks are good, but don't relax protection because of it. If both incomes hinge on the stock market and neither has insurance, a double unemployment (same-industry cycle) is more fragile than single income—at least one person secured, don't skimp on protection.
- Payoff: Dual income lowers single-point unemployment shock; under the CFPB framework a single-income household is advised 6 months of emergency fund, a stable dual-income household can drop to 3–4 months, freeing a sum for long-term investing. But the structural fragility is "correlation": if both are in the same industry or both heavily in equities and uninsured, an industry downturn strips both income and assets at once. So the right posture for a dual-income household is—one leans stable (protection + low-volatility base), both have complete basic protection, and avoid a "double-high-risk fragile structure," turning the fault-tolerance advantage into real long-term compounding rather than lifestyle inflation.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (no date). An essential guide to building an emergency fund (dual-income emergency fund can be lower than single-income). <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>; Survey and Statistics Department, People's Bank of China (2023). 2023 Q2 Urban Depositor Survey Report. <https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2023/0629/c1004-40024314.html>
- Note: Graded B as household financial-resilience common sense rather than strict empirical research; the core lesson is "lower the correlation between income and assets." Cross-reference: Entries 5, 7.

### 11. Self-employed / entrepreneurs need a thicker cushion for cash-flow swings
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Separate business and personal accounts, raise the emergency fund to 6–12 months, and prioritize low-volatility assets.
- In plain terms: Salary workers get paid every month; an entrepreneur may have no income for three months. Your emergency cushion must be twice as thick as an employee's to be safe, or business stress forces you to sell investments at the bottom to cover cash flow.
- Payoff: Self-employed/entrepreneurs have irregular income often positively correlated with the macro cycle (business bad, market bad)—exactly the "human capital and equities falling together" scenario the life-cycle model warns about, where you not only can't hold high equities but need thicker cash. The CFPB explicitly advises 6–12 months of emergency fund for unstable earners, even 12 months near retirement. At 20,000 yuan monthly essential spending over 9 months, you need 180,000 yuan in a current/money-market buffer; it sacrifices some return but buys the option of "not fire-selling long-term assets at a business trough"—equivalent to an invisible insurance policy.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: U.S. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (no date). An essential guide to building an emergency fund (irregular earners advised 6–12 months). <https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/>; Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2007). The Theory of Life-Cycle Saving and Investing (when human capital correlates with equities, equity share should be lower). <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0703.pdf>
- Note: Graded A on the dual support of regulatory guidance and life-cycle theory; separating the business account also lowers tax and legal risk. Cross-reference: Entries 7, 12.

### 12. Remarriage / blended family: front-load asset isolation
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Make a property agreement (in writing) before marriage/remarriage, sort out each party's accounts and existing children's inheritance arrangements, and set up a trust or separate account if needed.
- In plain terms: A blended family's property boundaries need clarifying more than a first marriage. Agree early and leave a trail—it protects both sides and the kids. Don't wait for divorce or a death to discover commingled property and inheritance disputes piercing your investment principal.
- Payoff: The Civil Code's marriage-and-family book gives clear boundaries: Article 1063 states one party's pre-marital property is separate property, not converted to community property by marriage's continuation; Article 1065 allows written agreement that pre-marital/marital property belongs separately, jointly, or partly separately, legally binding on both. On this basis, before remarriage, use a written agreement plus separate accounts to isolate pre-marital assets and gifts/inheritance to children from a prior marriage, avoiding post-commingling divorce division or inheritance disputes that dilute investment principal. For high-asset families, further use insurance trusts or family trusts for directed inheritance, lowering the "remarriage—dispute—liquidation" tail risk.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress / Supreme People's Procuratorate (2020). Civil Code of the People's Republic of China (Book V Marriage and Family: Articles 1063, 1065). <https://www.spp.gov.cn/spp/ssmfdyflvdtpgz/202008/t20200831_478417.shtml>; Department of Justice of Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region (2022). Compilation and interpretation of Civil Code couple-property provisions (separate property and agreed property regimes). <https://sft.xinjiang.gov.cn/xjsft/fzsj/202209/9abcedfd9fab4b1cb36820233a7e6ebe.shtml>
- Note: Graded A on written statute; oral agreements are hard to prove—use written form and keep evidence. See Entry 5 (order of coverage).

### 13. Longevity risk: retirement can last 30 years
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Plan for "25–30 years post-retirement" rather than 10, moderately delay retirement, and raise pension savings.
- In plain terms: Retiring at 60 and living to 90 is common. Don't plan money on "ten years of retirement is enough"—that leaves you penniless at 75 and dumps the pressure on your kids.
- Payoff: Longevity is a gift and a financial bomb. National Bureau of Statistics data shows China's average life expectancy reached 78.6 years in 2023, higher in first-tier cities, and living 25–30 years after a 60-year retirement is normal. Bengen's 4% rule is based on about a 30-year retirement and requires 25 times annual spending; plan for only 10 years and from year 11 you face "money running out before the person." The countermeasures: first, social security + enterprise/private pension as the base (CASS estimates Chinese households' private-pension share is only about 0.3%, far below developed countries' 20%+, the biggest shortfall); second, keep some equities in the retirement portfolio to fight inflation; third, consider delaying retirement a few years—save more and draw less.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: National Bureau of Statistics (2024). Series Report No. 10 on the Achievements of New China's 75 Years of Economic and Social Development (2023 average life expectancy 78.6 years). <https://www.stats.gov.cn/zt_18555/ztfx/xzg75njjshfzcj/202409/t20240913_1956439.html>; Bengen, W. (1994). Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data, Journal of Financial Planning. <https://www.bengenfs.com/the-4-percent-rule/>; National Balance Sheet Research Center, Chinese Academy of Social Sciences (2023). Estimate of household financial-asset composition (private pension share low). <https://m.gelonghui.com/p/2017669>
- Note: Graded A on official statistics and classic retirement research; China's high out-of-pocket medical share means real longevity spending may exceed the 4% rule's assumption—add commercial medical/long-term-care insurance. See Entry 4.
