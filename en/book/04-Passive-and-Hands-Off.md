# IV. Passive and Hands-Off

The most expensive resource in investing isn't money—it's your attention. Hand decisions to low-cost rules, and the saved energy goes to earning principal, being with family, honing skills; the cost-performance is far higher than staring at the screen daily hunting "the next multi-bagger." The "passive and hands-off" in this chapter isn't lying flat—it's using a replicable set of rules to minimize the most loss-prone actions—timing, stock-picking, screen-watching—and keep compounding and the "right to be present" for yourself.

All conclusions here are backed by hard data: SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2024 shows 92% of large-cap actively managed funds lagged the S&P 500 over 20 years; Buffett's ten-year bet (2008–2017) had the index fund at 125.8% vs the hedge-fund basket at 36.3%; JPMorgan calculated that missing just 10 best trading days over 2004–2023 dropped annualized return from 9.7% to 5.5%; DALBAR shows high-turnover investors lagged the index about 1 percentage point a year over 20 years. These numbers point to one thing—do fewer actions, be more a friend of time.

The entries below are grouped by theme (entry numbers in parentheses) for easy reference.

**Passive indexing and core tools (what to buy)**: Entry 1, Entry 9, Entry 8, Entry 11.
**Weakening timing (when to buy)**: Entry 2.
**Lowering trade frequency and screen-watching (less action)**: Entry 3, Entry 7, Entry 6.

**Whether to outsource to an advisor**: Entry 12.

### 1. Passive index funds: beat most actively managed funds long term
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Give up the fantasy of beating the market; fee about 0.03%–0.5%/year (broad-market ETF as low as 0.03%).
- In plain terms: Professional fund managers with the best talent and data fail to beat the index nine times out of ten. Buy the lowest-fee all-market index and you rank in the top 10%–20% long term; why do you think you can steadily pick those few winners?
- Payoff: Getting the market average already beats most of the churning crowd. S&P Dow Jones Indices' SPIVA U.S. Year-End 2024 shows the share of large-cap actively managed funds lagging the S&P 500 rises with the horizon: 1 year 65.2%, 5 years 76.3%, 10 years 84.3%, 15 years 89.5%, 20 years 92.0% (counting liquidations and mergers, survivorship-bias corrected). In other words, over 20 years, more than 9 of every 10 professional managers lose to the "just buy the index, near-zero action" free strategy. Buffett's million-dollar bet (2008–2017) is the living specimen of the same conclusion: the Vanguard S&P 500 index fund returned 125.8% cumulative (8.5% annualized), while the hedge-fund basket only 36.3% (3.1% annualized), none of the 5 won. For ordinary people, the lowest-fee broad-market index is the highest cost-performance "default option."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices (2025). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard Year-End 2024 (Report 1a: All Large-Cap Funds vs. S&P 500; 10-year 84.3%, 20-year 92.0% lag). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-us-year-end-2024.pdf>; Buffett, W. (2018). Berkshire Hathaway 2017 Annual Letter (ten-year bet: index fund 125.8% vs hedge-fund basket 36.3%). <https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2017ltr.pdf>; S&P Dow Jones Indices (2025). SPIVA U.S. Persistence Scorecard Year-End 2024 (top funds' next-year retention near zero). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/persistence-scorecard-year-end-2024.pdf>
- Note: Limitation: active management occasionally wins in inefficient markets (small-cap, emerging); a few stars can win long term but are extremely hard to identify ex ante; index funds take the market's full drawdown and don't solve "buying at a high." Cross-reference: Entries 2, 9, 11.

### 2. Regular investing (DCA): don't bet "is this the bottom now"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Monthly operational triviality; in a single-direction bull market may be slightly below lump-sum (Vanguard research: lump-sum beats DCA about 2/3 of the time).
- In plain terms: Nobody knows where the bottom is. Invest a fixed amount monthly; when up you get fewer shares, when down more—long term automatically "buy low more, buy high less." It's a regret-minimizing tool, not a return-maximizing tool.
- Payoff: Vanguard's paper "Dollar-Cost Averaging Just Means Taking Risk Later" (2012) compared rolling data from U.S., U.K., Australia 1976–2022: within a 1-year holding period, lump-sum (LSI) beat DCA with probability between 61.6%–73.7%, and the higher the equity share the larger the gap—a 100% equity portfolio averaged about 2.2% more lump-sum. But DCA's real value isn't "return maximization" but automating the hardest decision "when to buy," avoiding timing errors and regret. For monthly-salaried ordinary people, DCA turns market swings from "risk" into "a friend that averages down cost"; and JPMorgan data shows missing a few best trading days hits returns hard (see Entries 3, 6), while DCA forces you to "always be present," eliminating timing impulse at the source.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Shtekhman A., Tasopoulos C., Wimmer B. (2012). Dollar-Cost Averaging Just Means Taking Risk Later, Vanguard Research (LSI beats DCA about 2/3, 12-month window). <https://personal.vanguard.com/pdf/s315.pdf>; Vanguard (2023). Lump-Sum Investing Versus Cost Averaging: Which Is Better?. <https://investor.vanguard.com/investor-resources-education/news/lump-sum-investing-versus-cost-averaging-which-is-better>; J.P. Morgan Asset Management (2024). Guide to the Markets – U.S. (missing best trading days study). <https://mcusercontent.com/b20d9ee981aee111cf724f1f0/files/2aee21c7-8c0e-a728-a02c-73605d211090/Avoiding_the_Cash_Trap_JPM.02.pdf>
- Note: [Controversy] In a long-term rising market lump-sum is mathematically superior, but DCA wins on psychology and risk, steadier for ordinary people; the longer the DCA period (e.g., 36 months), the larger lump-sum's advantage. Cross-reference: Entry 6.

### 3. Check the screen less: the more you look, the more you act, the more you lose
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of "I'm watching the market" sense of presence (zero money cost).
- In plain terms: Open the app and you want to act; act and you're likely wrong. Set it to "check once a quarter" and the return curve is probably prettier. The best investors are often those who forgot the password.
- Payoff: Checking frequency is strongly positively correlated with trading frequency, and frequent trading is the "pump" of retail net returns. Barber & Odean (2000) found the most frequently trading retail had significantly lower annualized net returns than low-frequency traders; DALBAR 2024 QAIB shows ordinary equity-fund investors returned only 8.7% annualized over 20 years, a full 1 point below the S&P 500's 9.7%—mainly from panic selling and missed rebounds. JPMorgan (2024) further points out: holding the S&P 500 throughout 2004–2023 returned 9.7% annualized, but missing just 10 best trading days dropped it to 5.5%, missing 20 days to 2.8%; and the best days often follow the worst (volatility clustering), so frequent checkers precisely exit in greatest panic, systematically missing rebounds. Less checking = forced presence = preserved returns.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Barber, B. & Odean, T. (2000). Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth, Journal of Finance 55(2):773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2000.tb03224.x>; DALBAR (2024). Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB) 2024 (20-year annualized 8.7% vs S&P 500 9.7%). <https://www.dalbar.com/?p=17875/>; J.P. Morgan Asset Management (2024). Guide to Retirement / Guide to the Markets (missing best 10 days annualized 9.7%→5.5%). <https://mcusercontent.com/b20d9ee981aee111cf724f1f0/files/2aee21c7-8c0e-a728-a02c-73605d211090/Avoiding_the_Cash_Trap_JPM.02.pdf>
- Note: Fidelity internal statistics also show the best-performing accounts belong to the deceased or those who forgot passwords (behavioral inertia effect). Cross-reference: Entries 6, 7.

### 4. Asset allocation: explains the vast majority of return variation
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: A bit of planning effort (stock/bond/cash ratio).
- In plain terms: The impact of which fund you pick is far smaller than "how much stock vs bond." First set the big-class ratio (steady investors 30–50% stock, 40–60% bond), then pick low-cost tools. Get the ratio wrong and no star pick can save it.
- Payoff: Brinson, Hood, and Beebower (1986) studied 91 large U.S. pension funds (1974–1983) and found asset-allocation policy (long-term stock/bond/cash weights) on average explained 93.6% of the variation in total portfolio returns, with market timing and individual stock selection each pushing explanatory power to only 95.3% and 97.8%; active management dragged about 1.1% a year on average. Ibbotson & Kaplan (2000) further confirmed: asset allocation explains about 90% of cross-period return variation and about 40% of inter-portfolio long-term differences. In other words, ordinary people spending energy on "setting the ratio" has far higher cost-performance than picking funds and timing. Get the big-class ratio wrong and nothing you pick later can rescue it.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Brinson, G., Hood, L.R. & Beebower, G.L. (1986). Determinants of Portfolio Performance, Financial Analysts Journal 42(4):39–44 (R²=93.6%). <https://archive.agb.org/trusteeship-article/your-most-important-endowment-decision/>; Ibbotson, R. & Kaplan, P. (2000). Does Asset Allocation Policy Explain 40, 90, or 100 Percent of Performance?, Financial Analysts Journal 56(1):26–33. <https://archive.agb.org/trusteeship-article/your-most-important-endowment-decision/>
- Note: Note the classic conclusion says "90% of return variation is explained by allocation," not "90% of total return"; inter-portfolio relative-ranking differences are only about 40% explained by allocation. Cross-reference: Entries 5, 10.

### 5. Diversify across assets / regions
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Give up betting on a single multi-bagger.
- In plain terms: You don't need to know which will rise. Buy a basket (an index is a ready basket) and spread the risk of "will this company have trouble," keeping only the "the whole market will grow" bet you're willing to make.
- Payoff: Markowitz (1952) modern portfolio theory proves diversification reduces non-systematic risk without sacrificing expected return; empirically about 20–30 different-industry holdings eliminate most single-stock specific risk, leaving mainly market systematic risk. Cross-market and cross-asset further reduce single-economy shocks: SPIVA 2024 shows international (non-U.S.) and emerging-market actively managed funds lagged benchmarks 85.3% and 87.4% over 10 years, showing even overseas, buying broad-market indices beats betting single stocks. Broad-market index funds naturally provide this diversification at very low fees (0.03%–0.5%). Diversification reduces "avoidable single-stock risk," doesn't eliminate overall market drawdown, and needs pairing with long-term holding.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Markowitz, H. (1952). Portfolio Selection, Journal of Finance 7(1):77–91. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1952.tb01525.x>; S&P Dow Jones Indices (2025). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard Year-End 2024 (international/emerging 10-year lag 85.3%/87.4%). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/spiva-us-year-end-2024.pdf>; China Securities Index (2024). CSI All Share etc. broad-market index methodologies (diversified samples). <http://www.csindex.com.cn/>
- Note: Diversification can't eliminate systematic risk (overall market decline); must combine with stock/bond allocation and long-term holding. Cross-reference: Entries 1, 4.

### 6. Long-term holding + low turnover: keep compounding for yourself
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Give up short-term stimulation; bear 30%+ paper drawdowns along the way.
- In plain terms: Compounding is most powerful but needs time. Every sale breaks compounding once and pays friction once more. Buy good assets then "do nothing" is the hardest and most profitable discipline.
- Payoff: Compounding grows exponentially with years mathematically, and every trade generates friction (commission, stamp duty, bid-ask spread) and breaks compounding. Take A-shares: stamp duty one-sided 0.05% since 2023-08, commission about 0.025%, monthly turnover annualized friction about 1%–3%, 20-year compounding gap can reach about 30% (see Chapter 2). JPMorgan (2024) quantified the value of "being present": holding the S&P 500 throughout 2004–2023 returned 9.7% annualized ($10k → about $64k), missing 10 best days dropped to 5.5% ($29k)—the best days mostly appear within two weeks of the worst, only long holding avoids missing them. DALBAR shows high-turnover investors lagged the index about 1 point a year over 20 years. Buy and hold = keep both compounding and the "right to be present" for yourself.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: J.P. Morgan Asset Management (2024). Guide to the Markets – U.S. (full 9.7% vs missing 10 best days 5.5%). <https://mcusercontent.com/b20d9ee981aee111cf724f1f0/files/2aee21c7-8c0e-a728-a02c-73605d211090/Avoiding_the_Cash_Trap_JPM.02.pdf>; DALBAR (2024). QAIB 2024 (high turnover lags index about 1%/year). <https://www.dalbar.com/?p=17875/>; Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration (2023). Announcement on Halving the Securities Trading Stamp Duty (MFA-STA Announcement No. 39 of 2023). <https://fgk.chinatax.gov.cn/zcfgk/c102416/c5211343/content.html>
- Note: Long-term holding isn't never adjusting—when stock/bond ratios severely deviate from target you still need rebalancing (see Entry 10). Cross-reference: Entries 2, 3.

### 7. Lower checking frequency, set automatic reminders
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Turn off market-price push notifications (zero cost).
- In plain terms: Turn off the app's market push, check the report once a quarter. You won't lose money from checking less, but you often lose from checking more and acting more.
- Payoff: The core conclusion of behavioral finance: checking frequency ↑ → trading frequency ↑ → net return ↓. Multiple Barber & Odean studies consistently show the more frequently retail trades, the worse the annualized net return (the most active groups lag the market by several points); Odean (1999) and Statman-cited cross-country data also show frequent traders lag buy-and-holders by about 1.5 points a year. Changing checking to "quarterly report + auto regular-investing + auto rebalancing" equals handing decisions to timed rules, bypassing loss aversion and overconfidence. Zero cost, immediately doable, the highest cost-performance "self-discipline outsourcing."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Barber, B. & Odean, T. (2000). Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth, Journal of Finance 55(2):773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2000.tb03224.x>; Odean, T. (1999). Do Investors Trade Too Much?, American Economic Review 89(5):1279–1298. <https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.89.5.1279>
- Note: Same source as Entry 3; this entry focuses on actionable steps (turn off push / quarterly report). Cross-reference: Entries 3, 10.

### 8. Target-date funds: one-click risk reduction by age
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Pick a fund with a "target retirement year."
- In plain terms: If you're too lazy to manage ratios, just buy a "20XX target-date fund"—it automatically swaps stock for bonds by age for you, naturally turning steady near retirement.
- Payoff: Target-date funds (TDF) have a built-in "glide path": as retirement nears they automatically lower equity weight and raise bonds, sparing you personal rebalancing. Widely used in U.S. 401(k) and IRAs, passive TDFs have become the default option for many plans. Vanguard research (2024 "The Rebalancing Edge") shows its threshold rebalancing brought investors 15–22 basis points of annual net gain over the accumulation phase vs monthly rebalancing (5–8 bps vs quarterly). For the "lazy," a TDF buys out the whole process of allocation, rebalancing, and risk reduction with one layer of management fee (usually 0.1%–0.5%/year), avoiding selling at the bottom out of fear.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Vanguard (2024). The Rebalancing Edge: Optimizing Target-Date Fund Rebalancing through Threshold-Based Strategies (accumulation-phase annual gain 15–22 bps vs monthly). <https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/the_rebalancing_edge_optimizing_target_date_fund_rebalancing_through_threshold_based_strategies.pdf>; Vanguard Institutional. Vanguard's Approach to Target-Date Fund Rebalancing. <https://institutional.vanguard.com/insights-and-research/perspective/vanguards-approach-to-target-date-fund-rebalancing.html>
- Note: A TDF's stock/bond ratio is set by the issuer and may not match your risk preference; compare different firms' glide paths and fees. Cross-reference: Entries 4, 10.

### 9. The lazy "three-fund portfolio" is enough as a base
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Assemble one broad-market equity fund + one total bond fund + one overseas index fund.
- In plain terms: Don't be dazzled by hundreds of funds. One all-market equity fund + one total bond fund + one international equity fund is enough for most people.
- Payoff: The "three-fund portfolio" was summarized by the Bogleheads community (from Jack Bogle's "simplicity of grandeur"): three low-cost all-market index funds cover the three basic asset classes—domestic stocks, international stocks, bonds; ETF-version fees can be as low as 0.03%–0.17%. It drops stock-picking and timing, keeping only two levers—"asset-class allocation + rebalancing"—which are exactly the main variables deciding long-term returns (see Entry 4). Empirically, a three-fund portfolio of ~60% U.S. stocks / 20% international stocks / 20% U.S. bonds returned about 7%–8% annualized over 2000–2023, on par with or better than most churning active portfolios, with lower volatility and more tax-efficient fees. Bogle's bet (Entry 1) is the extreme version of this philosophy.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Bogleheads (2024). Three-fund portfolio (VTSAX/VTIAX/VBTLX etc. core funds and sample allocations). <https://www.bogleheads.org/w/index.php?oldid=89560&title=Three-fund_portfolio>; Bogleheads. Bogleheads investment philosophy (passive, low-cost, long-term). <https://www.bogleheads.org/wiki/Bogleheads_investment_philosophy>
- Note: Ratio by risk tolerance (e.g., "100 − age" for bonds); rebalance once a year. Cross-reference: Entries 1, 4, 10.

### 10. Rebalance by "threshold method," not "calendar method"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Set a deviation threshold (e.g., act only when an asset class deviates ±5%–10% from target).
- In plain terms: Don't mechanically rebalance monthly. Set "act only when stock ratio deviates 10% from target," and otherwise ignore it. This controls risk and cuts friction.
- Payoff: Vanguard research (2024) shows threshold rebalancing vs monthly/quarterly calendar methods brought higher annualized returns on a 60/40 portfolio by cutting trading costs: accumulation phase 15–22 bps annual gain vs monthly, 5–8 bps vs quarterly; simultaneously lower deviation and more controlled risk. The mechanism: "sell high buy low" happens naturally and trades only when the threshold is breached, avoiding the tax/fee loss of frequent rebalancing. Vanguard also suggests a hybrid "quarterly check + trade only on breach" for both discipline and low cost. For most, a ±5% threshold or annual check suffices.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Vanguard (2024). The Rebalancing Edge: Optimizing Target-Date Fund Rebalancing through Threshold-Based Strategies (threshold vs monthly gain 15–22 bps). <https://corporate.vanguard.com/content/dam/corp/research/pdf/the_rebalancing_edge_optimizing_target_date_fund_rebalancing_through_threshold_based_strategies.pdf>; Vanguard Australia. The right way to rebalance your investment portfolio (calendar vs threshold). <https://www.vanguard.com.au/personal/learn/smart-investing/portfolio-building/right-way-to-rebalance-your-investment-portfolio>
- Note: Rebalancing aims to control risk, not add return; in taxable accounts prefer rebalancing with dividends/new funds to avoid tax. Cross-reference: Entries 4, 8.

### 11. Dividend and low-vol factors carry long-term premia, but don't chase hot factors
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand the factor's long-term logic, not short-term hype.
- In plain terms: Factors aren't mysticism; they're sources of "a bit more long term." But don't rush in only when a factor is hot—chasing hot factors often buys at the top.
- Payoff: Fama & French (1993) three-factor model proves value, size, and other factors provide long-term premia beyond market beta; later research added momentum, quality, low-vol. AQR and others' empirics: dividend, low-vol, value factors beat the broad market about 1%–3% annualized over long cycles (decades, multiple countries), and are lowly correlated with each other, improving risk-adjusted returns. But factors have "style downturns" (value factor lagged 2007–2020 long), and chasing hotspots (buying only when hot) almost certainly buys at the top—factor premia are a long-term, diversified, contrarian reward, not short-term thematic hype. Can be a supplement to core indices; avoid overweighting a single factor.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Fama, E. & French, K. (1993). Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds, Journal of Financial Economics 33(1):3–56. <https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-405X(93)90023-5>; AQR Capital Management. Factor Premiums research (value/low-vol/quality long-term premia). <https://www.aqr.com/>; China Securities Index. Dividend/low-vol etc. Smart Beta index historical backtests. <http://www.csindex.com.cn/>
- Note: Factor premia need long holding (10+ years) to be significant; single factors have drawdown risk, diversify. Cross-reference: Entry 1.

### 12. Is an advisor / robo-advisor worth it? Only if its fee is below what it saves you
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Compare advisor fee vs the cost of your own blind operation.
- In plain terms: An advisor's biggest value is "holding your hands," not "picking better stocks." If you can already do regular investing + less checking, an advisor is mostly pure expense.
- Payoff: An advisor/robo-advisor's core gain is behavioral coaching, not stock-picking. Vanguard's "Putting a Value on Your Value: Quantifying Advisor's Alpha" (Kinniry et al., 2022) estimates an advisor can net clients up to about 3%/year through fee/tax optimization, rebalancing discipline, and behavioral coaching, of which behavioral coaching alone contributes about 0%–2%. DALBAR data contrast its value: ordinary investors lag the index about 1 point a year over 20 years due to emotional trading (8.7% vs 9.7%); if an advisor can curb this "itchy hand," what's saved is real money. But if you already do regular investing + quarterly checking + auto rebalancing, the advisor's marginal gain approaches zero—purely an extra 0.5%–1.5%/year fee. Conclusion: worth it only if you can't hold your hands; if you can, it's an expense.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Vanguard (2022). Putting a Value on Your Value: Quantifying Vanguard Advisor's Alpha (behavioral coaching contributes about 0%–2%/year, total gain up to about 3%). <https://www.vanguardsouthamerica.com/content/dam/intl/americas/documents/latam/en/2022/08/mx-sa-2335954-putting-a-value-on-your-value-quantifying-vanguard-advisors-alpha.pdf>; DALBAR (2024). QAIB 2024 (ordinary investors lag index about 1%/year). <https://www.dalbar.com/?p=17875/>
- Note: Robo-advisor fees have dropped to about 0.25%, but the self-disciplined may still not need one; note "free fund recommendations" are often driven by sales commissions. Cross-reference: Entries 2, 3, 7.
