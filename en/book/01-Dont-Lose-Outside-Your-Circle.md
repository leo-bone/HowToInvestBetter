# I. Don't Lose Outside Your Circle of Competence

This chapter makes one point: when you bet in places you don't understand, can't quantify, or where your emotions get amplified, the odds of losing are naturally stacked against you. The vast majority of permanent losses in investing don't come from a brutal market—they come from crossing the boundary of your own circle of competence: buying things whose money-source you can't explain, betting your life savings on a single stock, using leverage to turn volatility into death, gambling long-term with money you'll need next year, or chasing rallies on a friend's tip.

Every rule in this chapter is, at its core, a discipline of *what not to do*. They share one trait: the cost is extremely low (a few cents, a few minutes, a little restraint), and the payoff is "not going to zero, not being permanently knocked out." The higher-grade evidence (A) mostly comes from statutes and regulatory rules; the lower-grade (C) is mostly common sense and experience—flagged honestly, with no exaggeration. Nothing here is investment advice regarding any specific product.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses).

**Know where the money comes from (the foundation)**: don't buy things whose "money source" you can't explain (Entry 1); if you don't understand it, don't invest—non-standard assets are off-limits by default (Entry 5); complex structured products are off-limits by default (Entry 8); use the "explainability test" to screen out every investment (Entry 9).

**Position sizing and leverage (survival)**: concentrated bets in a single asset invite permanent loss (Entry 2); leverage amplifies the probability of being "wiped out" first (Entry 3); using money you'll need soon for long-term investing is a time bomb (Entry 4); don't open accounts for derivatives you don't understand (Entry 6).

**Don't fight probability (macro and counterparties)**: don't predict the macro or bet on a single direction (Entry 7); first figure out "who is on the other side of my trade" (Entry 14); treat "a celebrity / big-V's holdings" as endorsement—invalid by default (Entry 15).

**Information environment and red lines (behavior)**: tips from friends put you naturally on the information-disadvantaged side (Entry 10); sell-side research/ratings carry built-in bias—treat them as intelligence, not conclusions (Entry 12); paid stock-tip groups / live "trade-copying" streams are regulatory red lines (Entry 13); insider trading is a criminal red line, not a "clever trick" (Entry 11).

### 1. Don't buy things whose "money source" you can't explain
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. Before committing any sum, spend a few minutes figuring out "what makes it earn."
- In plain terms: For every yuan you invest, you must be able to answer "what makes it earn?" Is it a company's profit and dividends? Bond interest? Rental income? Or simply that someone is willing to take it off your hands at a higher price? If you can't answer, it's most likely a case of "someone else's return comes from your principal"—i.e., a Ponzi scheme or a greater-fool play. The cost is near zero; the payoff is "not going to zero."
- Payoff: You avoid the vast majority of scams and zero-return instruments. Any sustainable return must come from real value creation—corporate earnings, interest, rent, dividends—not from later participants' principal. Only invest in what can be stated in one sentence: "whose pocket does the money come from, and what do I know that the market doesn't?" If you can't write that sentence, it's a gamble. Treat this as the first gate before any order, and it blocks most "tips, feelings, hype" trades.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (no year). Investor.gov, "Ask Questions" investor-education page ("any investment should be able to answer where the money comes from and what the risks are"). <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/ask-questions>; China Securities Regulatory Commission (2017). Measures for the Administration of Investor Suitability for Securities and Futures (CSRC Order No. 130, Articles 3 and 4: intermediary institutions shall fully disclose risks to investors; investors shall make independent decisions after understanding the product and hearing suitability opinions). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c101803/>
- Note: This is a "common-sense threshold," not a statute; it's graded A because it directly maps to the regulator's mandatory requirements on "suitability" and "risk disclosure." It won't block complex but legal products you don't understand—see Entry 8 (structured products) for that. Cross-reference: Entry 5 (non-standard assets), Entry 9 (explainability test) are its concrete execution steps.

### 2. Concentrated bets in a single asset = an invitation to permanent loss
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the fantasy of "going all-in on one multi-bagger" (opportunity cost), and spending a little effort on diversification.
- In plain terms: Diversification won't make you richer (it doesn't raise expected return), but it keeps you alive. A single company's stock can go to zero through fraud, delisting, or bankruptcy—but a market made of many companies won't vanish as a whole over the long run. Betting your life savings on one stock is trading "permanent loss" for "excess return," with terrible odds.
- Payoff: It cuts the damage a single tail event (delisting, fraud, bankruptcy) can do to your total assets, from "possibly zero" down to bearable. The classic study by Evans and Archer (1968), "Diversification and the Reduction of Dispersion" in the Journal of Finance, found that holding about 10 randomly chosen stocks eliminates most firm-specific (idiosyncratic, non-systematic) risk; the industry rule of thumb is that holding 20–30 uncorrelated assets across different industries eliminates about 90% of idiosyncratic risk, leaving only the market risk no one can dodge. Meaning: diversify to this degree and you'll no longer be killed by "one name blowing up."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Markowitz H (1952). Portfolio Selection. Journal of Finance 7(1):77–91 (foundation of modern portfolio theory; Nobel Prize in Economics 1990). <https://doi.org/10.2307/2975974>; Evans J, Archer S (1968). Diversification and the Reduction of Dispersion: An Empirical Analysis. Journal of Finance 23(5):761–767. <https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1968.tb03092.x>
- Note: Graded B because "20–30 eliminates ~90%" is a rule of thumb and an approximation from multiple backtests, not a law precise to the unit; when industries are highly concentrated (all banks, all baijiu), diversification weakens, so you also need cross-industry and cross-asset-class exposure. Cross-reference: Entry 4 (short-money-long-investing), Entry 14 (counterparties).

### 3. Leverage amplifies the probability of being "wiped out" first
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the fantasy of "borrowing to amplify returns," and the restraint of not touching margin trading or futures.
- In plain terms: Without leverage, if your stock drops 50%, you're still at the table; to get back you only need +100%. With leverage, once it falls past a point the broker force-liquidates (margin call), and you can be swept out at the bottom with no chance to recover. Leverage doesn't raise your win rate; it turns "volatility" into "death."
- Payoff: In theory leverage amplifies gains, but empirically retail investors mostly net lose with it. The U.S. FINRA margin rules (Regulation T and FINRA Rule 4210) require initial margin of at least 50% and maintenance margin around 25%; once account equity falls below the maintenance line, the broker may sell without your consent. Barber and Odean's studies of retail investors show that the combination of "high-frequency trading + leverage" is among the most lethal loss sources for retail: after a leveraged bottom liquidation, principal is permanently out, and the subsequent market rebound has nothing to do with you. For ordinary people, simply not using leverage is itself a survival valve.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: U.S. Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA). Regulation T and FINRA Rule 4210 (margin requirements: initial margin no less than 50%, maintenance margin requirement). <https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/4210>; Barber B, Odean T (2000). Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth. Journal of Finance 55(2):773–806. <https://doi.org/10.1111/0022-1082.00226>
- Note: Graded A because margin forced-liquidation is a clear regulatory rule and the loss mechanism is well understood. But it doesn't deny the legitimate use of leverage by professionals/hedge funds; it's only net-negative for ordinary people. Cross-reference: Entry 2 (concentration), Entry 6 (derivatives), and the whole of Chapter 24 (derivatives and leverage risks).

### 4. Using money you'll need soon for long-term investing = a time bomb
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Free. The price is setting aside emergency funds and money you'll need in 1–3 years (home down payment, tuition, medical) separately, and only investing money "you can lose without hurting your life."
- In plain terms: Money you'll need next year or the year after shouldn't be in the stock market. The market won't cooperate with your withdrawal schedule—if you happen to need cash at the bear-market bottom, you're forced to sell at the bottom. Forcing "short-money-long-investing" hands "when to sell" to luck and the bear market.
- Payoff: Avoid forced selling at the bear-market bottom—a core reason "retail's actual return is far below the market's." DALBAR's annual Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB), published since 1994, shows: in 2023 the average U.S. equity fund investor returned 20.79% while the S&P 500 returned 26.29%, a gap of 5.5 percentage points; over 20 years, the average investor's annualized return was about 8.7% vs the S&P 500's ~9.7%. The gap is mostly not wrong asset selection but "buying and selling at the wrong time"—and short-money-long-investing is the number-one cause of forced wrong-time selling. Align your cash timeline with your investment horizon and you minimize this behavioral drag.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: DALBAR (2024). Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior 2024 (30th edition; 2023 data: average equity investor 20.79% vs S&P 500 26.29%). <https://www.qaib.com/public/media/30thQAIBPressRelease.pdf>; DALBAR (various years). QAIB report (long-term basis: 20-year average investor annualized 8.7% vs S&P 500 9.7%). <https://www.dalbar.com/>
- Note: Graded B because QAIB's exact figures vary slightly by edition and the report itself is paid (public channels mostly re-quote its press releases); but the direction "investors underperform the index due to timing" holds across all editions and Morningstar's independent methods. Cross-reference: Entry 2 (diversify to survive), Chapter 13 (retirement, horizon matching).

### 5. If you don't understand it, don't invest; non-standard assets are off-limits by default
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up "heard-of high-yield opportunities" (flawed private fund tranches, art, stamp/coin cards, non-standard wealth products, some overseas "principal-protected" products).
- In plain terms: Non-standard = no public price, hard to liquidate, information black box. You're after its 8% high yield; it's after your principal. These things often can't be sold when you most need cash, the price is unclear, and the contract hides terms unfavorable to you. Cost is zero; the payoff is "not stepping on a landmine."
- Payoff: Avoid the non-standard trap of zero liquidity and opaque information. In the regulatory lexicon, non-standard products mostly fall under "private placement" or "non-public offering," targeting qualified investors with extreme information asymmetry; once the underlying asset fails (e.g., distressed real estate or bonds), investors often can neither get their money nor sell their shares. The U.S. SEC's risk disclosure for private placements is explicit: private securities are illiquid, have limited information disclosure, and carry risks far higher than publicly traded securities. For ordinary people, the laziest lightning rod is: if you can't see a price, don't buy it by default.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (no year). Investor Bulletin "Private Placements" (risks: illiquidity, limited disclosure, high risk). <https://www.sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-and-bulletins/ib_privateplacements>; China Securities Regulatory Commission (various years). Risk alerts on non-standard wealth products / off-exchange margin financing (investor protection section). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/>
- Note: Graded C because "non-standard always hurts" is a probabilistic and experiential judgment, not every non-standard product defaults; qualified investors participating under strict due diligence is another matter. This entry targets only ordinary people and "heard-of opportunities." Cross-reference: Entry 1 (money source), Entry 6 (derivatives), Entry 8 (structured products).

### 6. Don't open accounts for derivatives you don't understand (options / futures / snowballs)
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the fantasy of "high leverage, T+0, profit both ways," and not opening options, futures, or principal-protected note accounts.
- In plain terms: Derivatives are hedging tools for professionals, not an ATM for retail. Option buyers bleed "time value" every day—you think you're bottom-fishing, the institution is collecting your time value; futures carry leverage, a reverse move and you're liquidated; "snowball" products have no floor on loss at the knock-in. Ordinary people playing it as a lottery is a negative-sum game long term.
- Payoff: Avoid the negative-sum game. An option's time value decays continuously toward expiration; most buyers lose long term (a basic property of option pricing—see Hull's classic textbook); "snowball" (autocallable structured notes) saw widespread knock-ins during the 2022–2024 downturn, with some investors losing 30%–70% of principal, while sales emphasized "annualized coupon" and downplayed "knock-in = principal loss." Regulators have repeatedly issued suitability warnings on snowball-type high-risk structured products, requiring full risk disclosure to ordinary investors. For those who can't read the 7-layer nesting and payoff structure, simply not opening an account avoids the trap most easily dressed up as "steady."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Hull J (various years). Options, Futures and Other Derivatives (standard treatment of time-value decay theta). <https://www.pearson.com/en-us/subject-catalog/p/options-futures-and-other-derivatives/P200000003063>; Securities Association of China / regulators (2022–2024). Multiple reminders on investor suitability and risk disclosure for snowball products (industry bulletins). <https://www.sac.net.cn/>
- Note: Graded B because time-value decay is mathematically settled (A-level logic), but "snowball knock-in loss 30%–70%" is a range estimate varying by structure and entry timing. A few strategies (covered calls, protective puts) help professional holders, but are net-negative and need ongoing management for ordinary people. Cross-reference: Entry 3 (leverage), Entry 8 (structured products), Chapter 24.

### 7. Don't predict the macro; don't bet on a single direction
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the thrill of "going all-in on up or down," and instead building an insensitive portfolio that "has a response to every environment."
- In plain terms: Even central banks can't predict interest rates accurately—why should you bet on direction? Replace "betting on one side" with "all-weather": have positions in up-moves, cash in down-moves, real assets in inflation, bonds in recession—hand the winning hand back to probability, not to your prediction.
- Payoff: Avoid adding wrong positions in the wrong direction. Numerous backtests show that mainstream institutions' point forecasts of economic indicators (CPI, GDP, rates) have large long-run errors and directional accuracy near random; take "Wall Street's annual S&P 500 target" as an example—the deviation between actual and predicted values is often counted in thousands of points. Macro forecasting's value for asset allocation is far lower than the dumb method of "diversify first, then rebalance regularly." Shifting energy from "guessing direction" to "controlling risk" pays ordinary people more.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Backtest studies on macro-forecast accuracy by multiple institutions (e.g., compilations on CPI/GDP forecast errors; long-run directional accuracy near random—synthesized conclusion here); CXO Advisory Group's multi-year tracking of Wall Street strategists' annual S&P 500 forecasts (significant deviation between prediction and actual). <https://cxoadvisory.com/>
- Note: Graded C because "forecasts near random" is a synthesized conclusion of multiple studies, with no single directly citable statute or paper clause; exact figures vary by statistical window. This book doesn't advocate ignoring macro entirely, but "don't bet on one side." Cross-reference: Chapter 17 (macro cycles).

### 8. Complex structured products are off-limits by default
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the "leveraged high-yield" packaging (e.g., some snowballs, principal-protected notes, tranched, structured deposits with complex tiers).
- In plain terms: The more complex a product, the more it lets the seller hide a rule unfavorable to you where you can't understand it. If you can't read the 7-layer nesting and can't write "worst case, how much do I lose at most," don't buy by default. Complexity itself is often the seller's weapon to manufacture information asymmetry.
- Payoff: Avoid products with nested clauses, capped upside, and unclear risk exposure. The real risk of such products is often not in the "expected return" column, but in the fine print: "trigger conditions," "observation dates," "knock-in lines," "early termination." The regulator's suitability management and risk-disclosure rules precisely require sellers to explain complex-product risks clearly; but for ordinary people, the laziest compliant move isn't "try hard to read," it's "if I can't read it, don't buy by default"—passing the judgment cost back to the seller, which is actually safer.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2017). Measures for the Administration of Investor Suitability for Securities and Futures (CSRC Order No. 130, Article 22: intermediary institutions shall inform investors of potential risks of products or services). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c101803/>; U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (no year). Investor Bulletin "Structured Notes" (risks: complex terms, illiquidity, capped returns). <https://www.sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-and-bulletins/ib_structured_notes>
- Note: Graded C because "complex = unfavorable" is a rule of thumb, not every structured product is bad (some structured deposits help conservatives); the key is whether ordinary people can read and bear its worst case. Cross-reference: Entry 5 (non-standard), Entry 6 (derivatives).

### 9. Use the "explainability test" to screen out every investment
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is writing (or thinking) one sentence before ordering: "why do I think I can profit, whose pocket does the money come from, what do I know that the market doesn't?"
- In plain terms: Only invest in what you can write on one page: "whose pocket does the money I earn come from, what do I know that the market doesn't." If you can't write it, it's a gamble. This is the master switch for all the disciplines above.
- Payoff: Keep "tips, feelings, hype" out the door, leaving only instruments with a writeable logic chain. In behavioral finance, "pre-mortem / checklist" is repeatedly proven to reduce decision errors: forcing yourself to state reasons clearly exposes the truth "I actually don't know anything extra." For ordinary people, the simplest executable version is: for any investment, first write "why I buy, worst-case loss, what I know more than the seller"; if you can't fill three lines, don't buy.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Kahneman D (2011). Thinking, Fast and Slow (on pre-mortem and decision biases; Pulitzer-winning book). <https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/183796/thinking-fast-and-slow-by-daniel-kahneman/>; Thaler R, Sunstein C (2008). Nudge (on default options and decision scaffolding). <https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/305785/nudge-by-richard-h-thaler-and-cass-r-sunstein/>
- Note: Graded C because "writing it clearly means fewer losses" is a behavioral principle and common sense, lacking a single statistic on "investment explainability tests"; but the principle is solid, low-cost, and side-effect-free. Cross-reference: Entry 1 (money source), Entry 12 (research bias).

### 10. Tips from friends / acquaintances: you're naturally on the information-disadvantaged side
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the lure of "inside info" and "a friend pulling me along," and treating any unverified "opportunity" as a takeover warning.
- In plain terms: If there were a sure-win tip, why would the other person tell you instead of going heavy themselves? The "opportunity" you hear is often exactly when someone else is looking for the next bag-holder. An acquaintance's endorsement isn't a safety cushion; it's often the signal of the last leg.
- Payoff: Avoid being the bag-holder. A-share evidence is brutally clear: the study by Shi Donghui (SSE), Zhang Xiaoyan (Tsinghua PBCSF), and Charles Jones (Columbia) based on complete trading data of ~53.4 million accounts on the SSE from Jan 2016 to Jun 2019 shows that retail accounts under 100,000 yuan in market value lost on average 20.53%, while institutional investors earned on average 11.22%; retail contributed about 85% of trading volume but net lost overall. The same data shows accounts under 100,000 yuan lost on average 2,457 yuan per year—1,532 from stock selection, 774 from timing, 151 in trading fees. Frequent trading and acting on tips are the two main causes of retail losses; friend tips hit both at once.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Shi D, Zhang X, Jones C (2021). Study based on SSE account data (retail-institution return divergence), published in Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis (JFQA). Re-quoted from International Institute of Green Finance, Central University of Finance and Economics (2025). "What investment strategies should ordinary investors adopt in an insufficiently efficient market?" <https://iigf.cufe.edu.cn/info/1012/11794.htm>; SSE Capital Market Research Institute (various years). SSE Statistical Yearbook investor-structure data (retail trading volume share ~85%). <https://www.sse.com.cn/>
- Note: Graded B because the study is based on real full-sample account data, published in a top journal, and the conclusion is credible; but "loss of 20.53%" is a 2016–2019 specific-window result, not every year. Cross-reference: Entry 2 (diversify), Entry 3 (leverage), Entry 12 (research).

### 11. Insider trading is a criminal red line, not a "clever trick"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the shortcut of "knowing the news early," and any trade based on undisclosed material information.
- In plain terms: Trading on news is earning knife-edge money. Once a case is filed, principal is confiscated, fines double, and you may go to prison. The "payoff" of this trade is a certainty of realized risk—not "might go wrong," but "if it goes wrong, everything's over."
- Payoff: Avoid "confiscation of illegal gains + 1–10x fine," with criminal liability for serious cases. China's Securities Law Article 53 explicitly prohibits insider trading; Article 191 stipulates: confiscate illegal gains and impose a fine of not less than one times and not more than ten times the illegal gains; the Criminal Law Article 180 (crime of insider trading / leaking insider information) stipulates that for serious cases the sentence is fixed-term imprisonment of not more than five years or criminal detention, and for especially serious cases not less than five and not more than ten years, plus a fine. For ordinary people, touching this line not only loses money but may also leave a criminal record affecting children's political review and life. Treat it as an absolute red line; the payoff is "not stepping into irreversible legal risk."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (2019 revision). Securities Law of the People's Republic of China, Article 53 (prohibiting insider trading), Article 191 (penalty: confiscate illegal gains, fine of 1x–10x). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2019/content_5465494.htm>; Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (2020 correction). Criminal Law of the People's Republic of China, Article 180 (insider trading / leaking insider information: serious cases not more than five years; especially serious five to ten years). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/202101/content_2582454.htm>
- Note: Graded A as explicit statutory provision, no exception space. But the determination of "illegal gains" and the fine multiple are at the enforcing authority's discretion. Cross-reference: Entry 13 (paid tips), Chapter 16 (spotting misinformation).

### 12. Sell-side research / ratings carry built-in bias—treat as intelligence, not conclusions
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is keeping a bit of independent judgment, never ordering just because you see a "Buy" rating.
- In plain terms: Research gives you the logical material for "why it might rise," not the instruction "you should buy." The sell side lives on commission and underwriting revenue, naturally bullish—the stock it recommends may be exactly what it's helping others dump. Treat it as intelligence, not an instruction.
- Payoff: Avoid "buying at the recommendation high." Sell-side analysts have systematic optimism bias: the U.S. SEC's Regulation AC (analyst certification rule) was created precisely to constrain this conflict of interest; numerous academic studies find that after analysts unanimously "strong buy," the instrument's subsequent excess return is often negative—the recommendation acts more like a contrarian indicator. For ordinary people, the correct use is "borrow its research framework and data, draw your own conclusion," not "copy its rating."
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (2002/2003). Regulation AC (analyst certification rule, requiring analysts to certify the truthfulness of their ratings and forecasts, constraining conflicts of interest). <https://www.sec.gov/rules/final/34-47270.htm>; multiple empirical studies on "negative excess return after analyst recommendations" (e.g., meta-analyses of thousands of U.S. stock ratings showing systematic optimism bias).
- Note: Graded B because "analysts are bullish, tend to fall after recommendation" has solid statistical support, but not every single stock; the key to using research well is distinguishing "factual data" from "opinion rating." Cross-reference: Entry 9 (explainability test), Entry 10 (friend tips).

### 13. Paid stock-tip groups / live "trade-copying" streams are regulatory red lines
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the fantasy of "earning with the teacher," and blacklisting anything "pay membership, promise returns, copy my trades."
- In plain terms: Someone who can reliably make money steadily won't pull you into a group for your petty membership fee. Paid trade-copying = take your money first, then use you as liquidity (you buy, they sell). Promising "steady gains" / "principal protection" is itself illegal advertising in China.
- Payoff: Avoid illegal stock-tipping and pig-butchering scams. China's Securities Law Article 120 stipulates that operating securities business must be approved by the securities regulator; Article 160 stipulates that securities investment advisory business must be licensed. Unlicensed tips and promised returns are both illegal; the CSRC runs annual special campaigns against illegal stock-tipping, participants often lose everything, and organizers may constitute illegal business operation or fraud. Treat "pay first then copy trades" as a precursor signal of fraud; the payoff is "not entering the game where funds get swept away."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Standing Committee of the National People's Congress (2019 revision). Securities Law of the People's Republic of China, Article 120 (securities business must be approved), Article 160 (investment advisory must be licensed). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2019/content_5465494.htm>; China Securities Regulatory Commission (various years). Special campaigns and risk alerts against illegal stock-tipping (investor protection section). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/>
- Note: Graded A as explicit statute. But in reality "teachers" often dress up as "knowledge payment" or "community exchange" to dodge wording; the identification points are "unlicensed + promising returns + asking you to copy trades." Cross-reference: Entry 11 (insider), Chapter 16 (spotting misinformation).

### 14. First figure out "who is on the other side of my trade"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is thinking for one second before ordering: "who is across from me, and why are they willing to sell to me?"
- In plain terms: What you buy, someone else sells. Ask "why is the other side willing to sell to me"; if you can't answer, don't buy. In secondary markets, you're mostly up against institutions, quant, and insider money—triply advantaged in information, cost, and speed.
- Payoff: Identify who you're betting is making a mistake. Market-microstructure research consistently shows retail is information-disadvantaged in most trades: your counterparty may be an informed trader, a market maker, or a high-frequency algorithm. Acknowledging this stops treating "I'm smarter than the market" as the default assumption, reducing impulsive trading and contrarian bottom-fishing. For ordinary people, the pragmatic conclusion: since you're probably at the end of the information chain, don't fight professionals at short-term games; turn to long-term, passive, diversified allocation.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: Market-microstructure research (e.g., the Glosten–Milgrom information model on informed trading and bid-ask spreads). <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glosten%E2%80%93Milgrom_model>; O'Hara M (1995). Market Microstructure Theory (classic treatment of information asymmetry).
- Note: Graded C because "retail is information-disadvantaged" is a theoretical consensus rather than a single quantifiable statute; but it explains why the disciplines of Entries 2, 3, and 10 matter so much. Cross-reference: Entry 2 (diversify), Entry 10 (friend tips).

### 15. Treat "celebrity / big-V holdings" as endorsement—invalid by default
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. The price is giving up the shortcut of "copying the big shots' homework," and not buying just because someone bought it.
- In plain terms: When you see a big-V's holdings, they may already be reducing; even if they're still holding, you know nothing of their cost, position, or duration. Copying homework copies someone else's history, not the future.
- Payoff: Avoid lagged bag-holding and "carrying the sedan chair." The U.S. Form 13F requires institutions to report holdings within 45 days after quarter end; by the time the info is public it's already lagged over a month, and only long positions are disclosed, not shorts or hedges; when retail sees and buys, it's often at a local high. Behavioral finance's "authority bias" makes people naturally overrate celebrities' win rates.
