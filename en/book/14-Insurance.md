# XIV. Insurance

Insurance is the "foundation" of a family's finances, not the "finishing on the upper floors." Its essence is one sentence: use a sum of certain, affordable small money to transfer the tail risks your family can't bear—serious illness, accidents, early death, longevity—to the insurer. But this is exactly the area most easily done backwards: sales pitches package insurance as "both protection and earnings," so many people buy participating, dividend, or annuity products first, while their real coverage is as thin as paper—when something actually happens, no payout, and the "savings" part can't even beat inflation.

This chapter sticks to the regulator's "insurance is for protection" orientation (Guo Fa [2014] No. 29, and repeated risk warnings from the National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA)), first explaining the order and methodology of allocation, then breaking down specific products and pitfalls entry by entry. Remember three iron rules: ① protect the person first, then protect the money; ② keep protection and investment separate, account for them separately; ③ the contract terms matter more than the brochure—the unglamorous bits like health disclosure, exclusions, the cooling-off period, and beneficiaries often decide whether you get paid.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses).
- Overall methodology: protect first then invest, priority ranking of protection (Entries 1–2)
- How to buy specific products: term life, the division of labor between critical-illness and medical insurance, the truth about participating/annuity products, the pitfalls of Hong Kong policies (Entries 3–6)
- Allocation discipline and pitfalls: separate protection and investment, honest health disclosure, premium waivers, insure the family breadwinner first (Entries 7–10)
- Contract and rights details: surrender and cooling-off period, beware the "guaranteed return" pitch (Entries 11–12)

### 1. Insurance is for protection: protect the person first, then the money
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Free. It's just a mindset shift; no money spent.
- In plain terms: Insurance is a shield, not a spear. First ask "what risk can't I bear," and transfer only what you can't bear (serious illness, accident, early death) to insurance; don't put the cart before the horse and buy it as a savings product.
- Payoff: The core function of insurance is "transferring the tail risks you can't bear," not investment appreciation. Since the 2014 "New State Ten Articles," regulators have repeatedly stressed "insurance is for protection"—insurance is explicitly positioned as an important industry in the modern economy and a basic means of risk management (Guo Fa [2014] No. 29); among its three major functions—economic compensation, financing, and social management—the one an ordinary family should use most is the first, "economic compensation." The correct order: fully fund protection-type budgets like critical illness, accident, and term life first; only then put the leftover money into truly income-generating assets like index funds and deposits. If you reverse it and let a participating policy crowd out your protection budget, you've paid a high premium for a "low-protection + low-return" combo that pleases neither side. A healthy 30-year-old male can lever a million-yuan term-life payout with an annual premium in the low thousands of yuan; with the same budget buying a participating product, the coverage might shrink by more than half. Protect the person first, then the money—get the order wrong and your protection has a gap.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: State Council (2014). Opinions on Accelerating the Development of the Modern Insurance Services Industry (Guo Fa [2014] No. 29) ("insurance is an important industry in the modern economy and a basic means of risk management"). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/2014-08/13/content_8977.htm>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (Article 8: floating returns must not be misrepresented as guaranteed returns). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>
- Note: This entry is the master outline; graded A because it rests on the State Council's top-level design and regulatory-file orientation. Cross-reference: Entry 3, Entry 5.

### 2. Protection priority: medical > accident > critical illness > term life > annuity
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: No extra cost—just sequence your budget in this order.
- In plain terms: A million-yuan medical plan costs a few hundred yuan a year and covers millions; far more practical than buying an annuity first. Block the risk that "banks you into poverty overnight" before thinking about growth.
- Payoff: Family protection should be ranked by "frequency × financial destructiveness." The first layer is million-yuan medical insurance + accident insurance: a million-yuan medical plan's annual premium is usually only a few hundred yuan (around 300–600 yuan/year at about age 30) for millions in inpatient reimbursement; accident insurance costs a hundred yuan or so a year for a million-yuan payout—extremely high leverage, affordable for almost everyone—so prioritize covering the "high-frequency small + low-frequency large" medical and accident/disability exposure. The second layer is critical-illness insurance: a lump-sum payment on diagnosis of a covered disease, to replace lost income and rehab costs; a 30-year-old female, 500,000-yuan coverage, consumer type, pays a few thousand yuan a year. The third layer is term life (see Entry 3), covering the breadwinner's early-death risk. Only last comes annuity/savings insurance—it doesn't prevent risk, only plans cash flow, and its benchmark interest rate has already been cut to 2.5% for ordinary type and 2.0% for dividend type (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18), so returns are limited. Reverse the order and your money is spent off the blade's edge, while a real illness or accident leaves a huge protection gap.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (Articles 1 and 2: benchmark interest-rate caps for ordinary/dividend types). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>; General Office of the China Banking and Insurance Regulatory Commission (CBIRC) (2020). Notice on Issues Concerning the Use of the Disease Definitions for Critical Illness Insurance (Yin Bao Jian Ban Fa [2020] No. 90) (framework of covered diseases). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/cn/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=940130>
- Note: Singles or those without debt can fine-tune the priority (e.g., low term-life need); this ranking targets the typical family with a mortgage and dependents. Cross-reference: Entry 3, Entry 4.

### 3. Term life is the "love insurance" with the highest leverage
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Low annual premium (healthy 30-year-old male, one-million-yuan coverage often just over a thousand yuan a year).
- In plain terms: Term life is "buying the heaviest responsibility cover with the least money." Young, heavily in debt, family depends on your income—it's the first policy you should buy. Don't buy the pricey, messy participating type.
- Payoff: Term life pays only on "death/total disability within the coverage period"; because it strips out savings and survival-return features, the pure risk premium is very low: take a 30-year-old male, covered to age 60, one million yuan coverage—a consumer term-life annual premium is often just over a thousand yuan, with leverage of several hundred times, the highest-leverage life product. It solves the extreme risk that "during your heaviest-responsibility years (mortgage + child-raising + elderly support), if the breadwinner falls, the family's cash flow breaks": one payout lets you keep fulfilling your support obligations, preventing foreclosure and interrupted children's education. You must name a beneficiary: under Articles 39 and 42 of the Insurance Law, death benefits without a named beneficiary are treated as an estate and distributed under inheritance law, which may trigger inheritance disputes and debt settlement; naming spouse/children as beneficiaries means direct payment and clearer debt isolation. When an employer insures a worker with whom it has a labor relationship, it may not name anyone other than the insured and their close relatives as beneficiary.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2015). Insurance Law of the People's Republic of China (2015 Amendment) (Articles 39, 40, 42: legal consequences of naming or not naming a beneficiary). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/docfile/2025/ca8d8237bdf746e9a3bff26a3bf2af39.pdf>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (benchmark rate affects premiums). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>
- Note: On the trade-off between term life and participating/whole life, see Entry 5, Entry 7; the beneficiary rule also applies to policies with death benefits like critical illness and accident insurance.

### 4. Critical-illness insurance is "income compensation," medical insurance is "reimbursement"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand the division of labor between the two, and allocate them separately.
- In plain terms: Don't think medical insurance alone is enough. A serious illness may keep you from working for a year or two, and the mortgage and kid still need paying—the critical-illness payout is the cash flow that keeps you alive.
- Payoff: The two have completely different functions: medical insurance reimburses treatment costs (inpatient/surgery/drugs) against invoices—"pay what you spent"—and is mostly one-year term, with premiums rising by age and no guaranteed renewal; critical-illness insurance is "a lump-sum payment of the agreed coverage on diagnosis of a covered disease," and you decide how to spend it—to replace lost income, pay rehab costs, support the family; its essence is loss-of-income compensation, not reimbursement. The 2020 critical-illness definition reform (Yin Bao Jian Ban Fa [2020] No. 90, fully effective Feb 1, 2021) expanded core diseases to 28 severe + 3 mild, and capped mild-disease payout ratios at 30% (e.g., thyroid carcinoma is graded by TNM stage, with Stage I treated as mild). This means when buying critical-illness insurance you should watch "whether mild diseases are covered, how much mild pays, whether the disease definition follows the 2020 version." A million-yuan medical plan solves "the money to get treated," critical-illness insurance solves "the money to survive during recovery"—they're complementary, not substitutes; if budget is tight, get medical first, then add critical illness.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: General Office of the China Banking and Insurance Regulatory Commission (CBIRC) (2020). Notice on Issues Concerning the Use of the Disease Definitions for Critical Illness Insurance (Yin Bao Jian Ban Fa [2020] No. 90) (Article 2: old-definition products discontinued from Feb 1, 2021; mild-disease payout cap 30%). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/cn/view/pages/ItemDetail.html?docId=940130>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2015). Insurance Law of the People's Republic of China (2015 Amendment) (relevant health-insurance contract framework). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/docfile/2025/ca8d8237bdf746e9a3bf2af39.pdf>
- Note: The 2020 critical-illness definition reform applies to adult critical-illness products; the 30% mild-payout cap applies only to the three regulator-defined mild diseases, and insurers may set their own limits for other mild diseases. Cross-reference: Entry 2 (priority).

### 5. The real return of participating insurance mostly fails to beat inflation
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Adjust the expectation that "premium = investment."
- In plain terms: The "4% compound" the agent demonstrates is mostly an assumption. Calculated by the contract's cash value, the long-run IRR is often 2–3 points, and liquidity is locked up. For growth, go to index funds; consider this only if you want a certain floor.
- Payoff: The contractual benefits of dividend, universal, and annuity policies split into "guaranteed" and "non-guaranteed" parts. Regulators have repeatedly cut benchmark rates to control the interest-spread loss: a 2023 window guidance cut the cap on ordinary long-term products' benchmark rate from 3.5% to 3.0%, and the 2024 Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) cut it further to 2.5% ordinary, 2.0% dividend, and a 1.5% minimum guarantee for universal (effective Sep 1 and Oct 1, 2024, respectively). This means new products' guaranteed-return ceiling is only 2.5%, and back-solving the IRR from "guaranteed benefits" long-run is mostly in the 2%–3% range; after subtracting recent actual inflation, real purchasing-power growth is limited. And the non-guaranteed dividend/universal demonstrated rates are only assumptions, not actual. More critically, early cash value is extremely low—surrendering in the first few years doesn't even get your principal back, and liquidity is locked long-term. Its correct positioning is a "certain but low-return" allocation base, not a high-return investment; for real growth, index funds' long-run expected return is higher and liquidity better.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (Article 1 ordinary 2.5%, Article 2 dividend 2.0%/universal 1.5%; Article 8: floating returns must not be misrepresented as guaranteed). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>; Xinhua Net (2024). NFRA clarifies: insurance product benchmark rates to be cut from September. <http://www.xinhuanet.com/money/20240820/b053d7c5110b4d44a3d88cd3a3b6c762/c.html>
- Note: IRR varies greatly by product, holding period, and payment method; benchmark rate ≠ the customer's actual return (fees must also be deducted). Graded A on the explicit rate-cap provisions; specific product returns still depend on the contract's cash-value table.

### 6. The triple pitfall of Hong Kong insurance: forex, exchange rate, litigation
<!-- tags: money=little time=some will=much payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The hidden costs of cross-border insurance and the ongoing management effort.
- In plain terms: Hong Kong policies show pretty demonstrated returns, but money doesn't move freely in and out, the exchange rate bites back, and you'd have to sue in Hong Kong. Unless you genuinely need dollar allocation, don't cross borders for that little demonstrated spread.
- Payoff: Hong Kong policies are often denominated in USD/HKD with a slightly higher demonstrated IRR than the mainland, but three hidden risks are underestimated: ① capital crossing borders—renewal premiums and claim proceeds involve foreign exchange, constrained by individual forex quotas and cross-border capital-compliance rules; large claim remittances back home involve cumbersome procedures and possible forex declaration; ② exchange rate—when the RMB appreciates, the USD policy's return converted back to local currency is eaten up; recent RMB volatility has already left many "earning on the demonstration but losing on the exchange"; ③ jurisdiction—the policy is governed by Hong Kong law, and real disputes require litigation or arbitration in Hong Kong, with very high legal fees, travel, and language barriers, and mainland courts' enforcement of Hong Kong policies is uncertain. Also, Hong Kong critical-illness/medical insurance's "utmost good faith" health-disclosure requirement is stricter, and claim standards differ from the mainland. This suits those who already hold real foreign-currency assets and need global allocation; making a special cross-border trip purely for "high demonstrated spread" overrates both cost-performance and safety.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 21 suitability sales; Article 25 must warn of policy-benefit uncertainty). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (Article 8: floating returns must not be misrepresented as guaranteed, applies to dividend/universal demonstrations). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>
- Note: Forex quotas follow the Measures for the Administration of Personal Foreign Exchange (PBOC Order [2006] No. 3) and the latest requirements of mainland forex authorities; exchange-rate and cross-border jurisdiction are practical common sense and are not individually linked to avoid dead URLs.

### 7. Don't treat insurance as investment; keep the accounts separate
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. Just split "protection" from "return" in your thinking.
- In plain terms: A policy that "returns money even if nothing happens" is essentially extra premiums you paid being invested and kicked back to you, with thinner coverage. Buy them separately and both sides are cheaper.
- Payoff: The "return/dividend" of participating, endowment, and dividend policies is essentially the extra premium you paid being invested by the insurer, with part of the future return given back—you pay extra for the "savings" part, so under equal premiums the pure-protection coverage is lower. Regulators have capped benchmark rates for ordinary, dividend, and universal products at 2.5%, 2.0%, and 1.5% respectively (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18), meaning the annualized ceiling on the "returned" part is very low—far below what you could earn by taking the same extra premium to buy index funds or deposits, and you also sacrifice liquidity. The right move: protection is protection (buy cheap consumer critical-illness, medical, term life), investment is investment (index funds, deposits, government bonds), each at its most cost-effective. Comparing one person's "consumer + index fund" combo against a "participating policy," long-run the former has thicker protection, more flexible assets, and higher expected return.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (Articles 1, 2 benchmark-rate caps). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 22: new-type products must warn of policy-benefit uncertainty). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>
- Note: Annuity/increasing-life as a "certain but low-return" succession or retirement base isn't entirely unacceptable; the key is not treating it as high-return investment, and certainly not letting it crowd out the protection budget. Cross-reference: Entry 5, Entry 12.

### 8. Fill in your health disclosure honestly, or you bought it for nothing
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. Spend ten extra minutes checking your medical history at application.
- In plain terms: Concealing your medical history at application saves ten minutes, but loses everything at claim time. Fill what should be filled; don't trust the agent's "no need to tell." Two years later isn't absolutely safe either.
- Payoff: Article 16 of the Insurance Law is the "line of life and death" for claims: when concluding a contract, if the insurer asks about relevant matters, the applicant must disclose truthfully; if intentional or gross-negligence non-disclosure is sufficient to affect underwriting or pricing, the insurer may rescind the contract and bears no liability for insured events before rescission, and for intentional cases doesn't even refund the premium. The so-called "two-year incontestability" (after two years the insurer may not rescind) is not a universal shield—if it's intentional concealment of major medical history and the event relates to the non-disclosed matter, the claim may still be denied; judicial interpretation also makes clear that incontestability doesn't protect fraudulent non-disclosure. Also, exclusion clauses (e.g., pre-existing conditions, events within the waiting period, illegal acts) under Article 17 of the Insurance Law and Article 26 of the Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct require the insurer to give a conspicuous prompt and explicit explanation at application, or the clause doesn't take effect—but this doesn't relieve your truthful-disclosure duty. At application, check your medical records and abnormal checkups item by item; disclose more rather than less.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2015). Insurance Law of the People's Republic of China (2015 Amendment) (Article 16 truthful disclosure and rescission right; Article 17 exclusion-clause prompt and explanation duty). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/docfile/2025/ca8d8237bdf746e9a3bf2af39.pdf>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 26: exclusion clauses must be conspicuously prompted and explicitly explained; Article 27: inquiries must have specific content, no catch-all clauses). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>
- Note: The two-year incontestability clause protects "non-malicious non-disclosure," with exceptions for intentional fraud; the specific adjudication standard follows the Judicial Interpretations (II) and (III) of the Insurance Law and individual cases.

### 9. The waiver clause is cheap "double insurance"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: The small additional premium for the waiver rider.
- In plain terms: Spend a little so that if you fall, your family's policy keeps coverage without further premiums. Tick it when insuring your kid or spouse—high cost-performance.
- Payoff: Premium waiver means that after the policyholder (or insured) suffers a covered event (e.g., critical illness, moderate/mild illness, death, total disability), the insurer waives all subsequent premiums and the contract coverage stays in force. Typical scenario: parents insure a minor child for critical illness/education; if the parent (policyholder) unfortunately gets a critical illness or dies, the child's remaining premiums are all waived and the child's coverage is unaffected—equivalent to using a small rider to buy invisible insurance against "family income interruption." Couple mutual insurance (each being the other's policyholder with a policyholder-waiver rider) achieves "if one falls, both policies' premiums are waived." The cost is usually only a few to a dozen-plus percent of the annual premium—considerable leverage. Note the distinction between "policyholder waiver" and "insured waiver," and confirm the trigger conditions (whether moderate/mild illness is included), waiting period, and health-disclosure requirements; when insuring family, prioritize the policyholder waiver.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2015). Insurance Law of the People's Republic of China (2015 Amendment) (relevant framework on insurance-contract validity and policyholder rescission right). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/docfile/2025/ca8d8237bdf746e9a3bf2af39.pdf>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 14: product descriptions must list coverage scope and exclusion/reduction clauses). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>
- Note: Waiver is an optional rider, not included in every product; trigger conditions and health disclosure depend on the specific clause—it's a product-level agreement, not legally mandatory.

### 10. Insure the family breadwinner first, not the child first
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=family -->
- Cost: Free. Correct the instinct of "love the child, insure the child first."
- In plain terms: Many parents' first policy is for the child—wrong. You are the kid's "money printer"; if you fall, the whole family goes hungry. Insure the one who earns first.
- Payoff: The order of family protection should follow "whose income interruption hits the family finances hardest." A child has no income; losing the child is an emotional loss for the family, not a cash-flow break; but the earning parent (breadwinner), if they die early, get a critical illness, or become disabled, immediately loses the source for the mortgage, child-raising, and elderly support—that's the real financial breaking point. So with a limited budget, first fully cover the income earner with term life + critical illness + medical + accident (see Entries 2–4), then cover the dependents (spouse, children, parents). Insuring children should also prioritize consumer child policies that "cover disease/accident," not put the cart before the horse with education/annuity first. Also, when insuring a spouse or child with a death-benefit product, remember to name a beneficiary (Insurance Law Article 39) to avoid the death benefit falling into estate proceedings. The best way to love your child is to make sure that when you fall, someone picks up the baton.
- Evidence grade: C
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2015). Insurance Law of the People's Republic of China (2015 Amendment) (Article 39: beneficiary designated by the insured or policyholder, policyholder designation requires the insured's consent). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/docfile/2025/ca8d8237bdf746e9a3bf2af39.pdf>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 21: understand the policyholder's insurance needs and premium-paying capacity, sell suitably). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>
- Note: If the family has achieved financial freedom with no debt or dependent pressure, the term-life priority can be lowered; this entry targets the typical "old and young both depend on you" family.

### 11. Don't repeatedly surrender and re-buy; friction eats your principal
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Little—restrain the impulse to "trade old for new."
- In plain terms: Someone tricks you with "the old is worse than the new" and you surrender—the early-years principal is gone, and re-buying costs more. Buy and hold; don't trade insurance as a short-term play. Long-term policies have a cooling-off period; after that, only cash value remains.
- Payoff: Long-term life policies' early cash value is extremely low: a large chunk of the premium first goes to commissions and fees, and early surrender gets back cash value far below premiums paid—possibly losing 30%–40% or more of principal; and as you age, re-insuring the same coverage costs more per year, and worsened health may add loading or denial—the friction cost of switching products is very high. The regulator gives a "right to regret": long-term policies usually have a 10–15 day cooling-off period (some channels/products 15 days), within which surrender returns all premiums (only a ≤10-yuan document fee deducted); but once past the cooling-off period, surrender only returns the cash value. Therefore, unless the original product truly has major defects (e.g., seriously missing coverage, sales misrepresentation you can fight), don't surrender the old and buy new just because of "stop-sale panic" or "upgrade." Use the cooling-off period to calmly check the terms before buying, then hold long-term, treating insurance as protection, not a short-term instrument.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 25: at contract conclusion must prompt the cooling-off period, cash value, and surrender-fee deductions). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2015). Insurance Law of the People's Republic of China (2015 Amendment) (Article 15 policyholder may rescind; Article 37 lapse and cash-value refund). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/docfile/2025/ca8d8237bdf746e9a3bf2af39.pdf>
- Note: The specific cooling-off days follow the product clause and channel rules (generally 10–15 days); the cash-value curve varies greatly by product, and surrender loss follows the contract's cash-value table.

### 12. Beware the "guaranteed return" insurance pitch
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Free. Be wary of high demonstrated returns.
- In plain terms: The agent treats the brochure's high-tier demonstration as a promise—illegal and harmful to you. Flip to the contract and look for the words "guaranteed benefit"; everything else is pie in the sky.
- Payoff: The benefit demonstrations of dividend, universal, and variable-universal products split into "guaranteed" and "non-guaranteed" tiers (universal/dividend also have low/medium/high demonstrations), but only the "guaranteed benefit" or "minimum guaranteed rate" column is truly floored; medium/high demonstrations are only assumption-based investment scenarios, not the future. Regulators explicitly forbid confusing the two: Jin Fa [2024] No. 18 Article 8 requires "floating returns must not be misrepresented as guaranteed returns," and the Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct Article 22 requires that when selling new-type products, the uncertainty of policy benefits must be prompted. Any pitch that presents demonstrated rates as guaranteed rates, calls non-guaranteed dividends certain returns, or uses "compound rolling" to hide early fees and surrender losses is a red-line pitch. Before signing, only trust the contract's "guaranteed benefit / minimum guaranteed rate" column, and require the agent to explain the demonstration assumptions in writing; if you find sales misrepresentation, you can complain to NFRA's consumer-protection channel.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2024). Notice on Improving the Pricing Mechanism for Life Insurance Products (Jin Fa [2024] No. 18) (Article 8: floating returns must not be misrepresented as guaranteed). <https://www.nfra.gov.cn/chinese/OFFICE/PDF/1175200.pdf>; National Financial Regulatory Administration (NFRA) (2023). Measures for the Administration of Insurance Sales Conduct (2023 No. 2) (Article 22: new-type products must prompt policy-benefit uncertainty; Article 17 prohibits false or exaggerated promotion). <https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2023/issue_10826/202311/content_6915815.html>
- Note: Benchmark rate ≠ the customer's actual return, and demonstrated benefit ≠ guaranteed benefit; specific products follow the contract terms and cash-value table.
