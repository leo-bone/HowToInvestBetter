# XXIV. Derivatives and Leverage Risks (The pit ordinary people should most avoid)

This chapter is about tools that "look like they amplify returns": options, margin trading and securities lending, stock-index futures, snowballs, structured products, graded funds, leveraged ETFs, covered/protective options. Conclusion first—for ordinary people, the vast majority of derivatives and leverage are negative-expectancy: they turn "small swings" into "principal to zero," and pile on three layers of risk—time decay, forced liquidation, and clause traps. This chapter breaks down each tool's mechanism and real loss numbers entry by entry; the core principle is one sentence: if you can't understand the leverage structure, can't hold through a margin call, and can't compute the time value, don't touch it.

On evidence: the risks of derivatives and leverage aren't "theoretical"—they are deterministic harms repeatedly verified by regulatory documents, exchange rules, and real blow-up cases. The U.S. SEC investor bulletin explicitly warns that leveraged/inverse ETFs "reset daily, and long-term performance can deviate significantly from the multiple," and that margin accounts "you may lose more than the money you have invested"; China's CSRC Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Derivative Trading bring snowball-type "autocallable knock-in/knock-out options" under regulation; the China Financial Futures Exchange's (CFFEX) Risk Control Management Measures lock stock-index-futures leverage at about 8x via a 12% minimum margin and set a forced-liquidation system; the China Futures Association's Guidance on Futures Brokerage Contracts states plainly that "total losses may exceed all initial and additional margin." Every figure in this chapter is tagged with year, institution, and original source for easy verification.

### 1. Option buyers: time value leaks away every day
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Understand Theta (θ, time decay); know that the premium = intrinsic value + time value, and time value goes to zero at expiration.
- In plain terms: Buying an option is like buying a soon-to-expire ticket—its value drops a bit each day, and faster as expiration nears. You must not only call the direction right but also the magnitude and timing—double the difficulty, and "right direction but still a loss" is the norm.
- Payoff: The premium an option buyer pays = intrinsic value + time value, quantified by Theta in yuan/day, negative for the buyer. CBOE Options Institute material shows an at-the-money option's daily Theta is about -0.10 at 60 days to go, jumping to about -0.55 in the last 7 days, with time value evaporating at several times the speed in the final week; the industry rule of thumb is an option loses about 1/3 of its total time value in the last 30 days and another ~1/3 in the last 14 days. At expiration time value is zero, only intrinsic value remains, so out-of-the-money contracts "bought within 30 days of expiration" mostly go to zero or fall sharply. Black–Scholes (1973) theory also shows at-the-money Theta accelerates roughly by 1/√T. The result: the buyer must call direction, magnitude, and timing all right; long-term buyers are mostly net losers, and exchange/broker disclosure data broadly show most retail option buyers lose.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: CBOE Options Institute (2017). Using the Option Greeks (time value and time decay; at-the-money 40 Call Theta 0.10 at 60 days, 0.55 at 7 days). <https://www.interactivebrokers.com/webinars/2017-WB-2719_CBOE-UsingOptionGreeks.pdf>; Black, F. & Scholes, M. (1973). The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities, Journal of Political Economy 81(3):637–654 (Theta accelerates by 1/√T). <https://doi.org/10.1086/260062>; Koufu / futures manager Jiang (2024). - Note: Limitation: Theta is "average" decay, actually affected by implied volatility (Vega) and Gamma; near expiration Gamma spikes, sharply raising directional risk; the buyer's win rate mostly comes from exchange/broker risk disclosures, not this chapter's calculations. Cross-reference: Entry 7 (a leveraged ETF's volatility decay is another kind of "path/time" loss), Entry 8 (covered call is the side that "earns" theta in reverse).

### 2. Margin trading: interest + forced liquidation turn volatility into death
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Give up the safety cushion of "no leverage"; borrowed funds accrue interest daily (current U.S. broker margin rates about 5%–12%), interest compounded monthly into the debt.
- In plain terms: Margin financing is borrowing money to trade stocks. You earn more when up, die first when down—worse, the broker can sell your stocks to repay the debt before you can react, with no notice and no asking which ones.
- Payoff: Margin trading (margin accounts) amplifies both gains and losses. Fed Regulation T lets brokers lend up to 50% of the purchase price—you put up 1 yuan to buy 2 (about 2x leverage); a 50% rise doubles your principal, a 50% fall zeroes it and you still owe interest. More dangerous is "maintenance margin": FINRA Rule 4210 floor is 25%, brokers internally often set 30%–40%, and can raise it anytime without advance notice. If account equity falls below the maintenance line a margin call triggers; if not met in time, the broker may force-liquidate at its own choice of securities without your consent, often selling at the bottom, and the liquidation itself creates a taxable event. Extreme historical case: LTCM (1998) traded at about 30x leverage, lost 44% in a single August, and by September its net value was only about 600 million USD (over 90% lost); the Fed coordinated 14 institutions to inject 3.6 billion USD to avoid a chain collapse—proving that "model + high leverage" can blow up instantly in a tail event. For ordinary people, one forced liquidation is permanent exit.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: SEC Office of Investor Education and Advocacy (Investor Bulletin). Investor Bulletin: Understanding Margin Accounts ("you can lose more money than you have invested," broker may force-liquidate without notice). <https://www.sec.gov/answers/marsell.htm>; Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (Regulation T, 12 CFR Part 220). Credit by Brokers and Dealers (initial margin 50%). <https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-II/subchapter-A/part-220>; Federal Reserve History (1999). Near Failure of Long-Term Capital Management (LTCM about 30x leverage, September 1998, 14 institutions inject 3.6 billion USD). <https://www.federalreservehistory.org/-/media/Project/FedHistory/FedHistory/Documents/essaysPDFs/Near-Failure-of-Long-Term-Capital-Management-_-Federal-Reserve-History.pdf>
- Note: Limitation: Reg T only binds the "initial" 50%; maintenance margin is set separately by FINRA Rule 4210 (floor 25%) and the broker's "internal rules" (often 30%–40%), which can be raised anytime without advance notice. LTCM is an institutional case, showing even the most refined model can't survive a liquidity crunch and correlation run. Cross-reference: Entry 7 (leveraged ETF similarly amplifies long/short volatility).

### 3. Stock-index futures: the double test of margin and backwardation
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand margin changes and the roll-over cost of "backwardation" (far month below near month); futures margin is raised by the exchange / futures company with market volatility.
- In plain terms: Index futures aren't "cheap stocks." Their backwardation leaks like a steady drip, and margin gets called up with volatility, forcing you to add money at the bottom—testing not just your judgment but your cash flow.
- Payoff: Stock-index futures use margin trading; the CFFEX minimum trading margin is 12% (about 8x leverage), price limits ±10%, and the exchange / futures company can raise the margin ratio with risk. Leverage means a small index move amplifies into a large principal move: at 12% margin, an index move against you of about 12% breaches the margin and may force-liquidate. More complex is "backwardation" (negative basis, far month below near): holding a backwardated contract long requires rolling month by month, each time swapping near for far at "sell low, buy high," and if backwardation persists it forms a steady drag, with long-term return well below the underlying's gain. The CFFEX Risk Control Management Measures set a clear forced-liquidation system—insufficient margin not replenished in the agreed time gets force-liquidated, losses and fees at your own expense; the China Futures Association also warns that "total losses may exceed all initial margin and additional margin deposited with the futures company." So index futures don't fit a "buy and hold long" mindset; they are a risk-management / hedging tool, not "cheap stocks."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China Financial Futures Exchange (2026). CFFEX Trading Rules (Chapter V Risk Control: margin system, position limits, forced-liquidation system; minimum trading margin 12%). <http://www.cffex.com/cn/gzjygz/20260410/43076.html>; China Financial Futures Exchange (Risk Control Management Measures). Stock-index futures contract minimum trading margin 12%, price limit ±10%, forced-liquidation system (Article 46). <http://www.cffex.com.cn/u/cms/www/oldsys/P020120530563270334416.pdf>; China Futures Association (2021). Guidance on Futures Brokerage Contracts (2021 revision; margin trading is leveraged, losses may exceed initial and additional margin). <https://www.cfachina.org/governmentrules/selfdisciplinerules/businessspecificationclass/202101/t20210125_15136.html>
- Note: Limitation: backwardation (basis) isn't an exchange-stated "loss" but set by market supply and demand; holding a backwardated contract long requires monthly rolling, and roll cost varies with the backwardation depth; the margin ratio can be raised by the exchange / futures company with risk. Cross-reference: Entry 1 (time value), Entry 7 (a leveraged ETF's path decay, same logic).

### 4. Snowball: loss has no floor after knock-in, never "principal-protected"
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: See the nature of "non-principal-protected, with knock-in"; confirm you are not a natural person routing around suitability management via a private-fund channel.
- In plain terms: Snowballs advertise "high coupon," but that's your fee for selling a put option. When the market crashes and knocks in, what you catch is a real-money loss—the "steady gain" turns into "principal halved" in extreme markets.
- Payoff: A snowball (autocallable knock-in/knock-out option) is essentially you selling an over-the-counter put with knock-in and knock-out barriers to a broker; the 12%–22% coupon is the payoff for being a "put seller," with capped upside and huge downside exposure. The underlying is mostly the CSI 500 / 1000 indices, with knock-in lines often at 70%–80% of the initial price. In the 2022–2024 deep pullback, the CSI 500 / 1000 triggered knock-ins multiple times; the outstanding scale peaked at about 500 billion yuan (2021), and in early 2024 brokers' knock-in ratio was about 7%; some investors lost 30%–70% of principal. Real case: Fei bought 23 snowballs through a private-fund channel; the 2023–2024 underlying crash triggered knock-in, net loss about 14.34 million yuan; Shanghai Financial Court (2026), case (2026) Hu 74 Min Zhong No. 527, ruled the institution jointly liable for 10.04 million yuan, but the investor still bore part of the loss. Conclusion: a snowball is in no way "principal-protected high-yield"; after knock-in you catch a real-money loss, and natural persons are in fact prohibited from being the direct counterparty.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2023). Notice on Public Comment on the Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Derivative Trading (Draft for Comment) (bringing autocallable knock-in/knock-out options under regulation). <https://www.csrc.gov.cn/csrc/c101981/c7326196/content.shtml>; China Economic Net (2024). Snowball products, big impact on the stock market? (underlying CSI 500/1000 outstanding about 200 billion yuan, early-2024 brokers' knock-in ratio about 7%). <http://m.ce.cn/bwzg/202401/30/t20240130_38886503.shtml>; Baidu Baike (2021). Snowball (essence: selling an exotic put with knock-in/knock-out barriers; August 2021 CSRC risk warning, peak outstanding about 500 billion yuan). <https://baike.baidu.com/item/%E9%9B%AA%E7%90%83/63994128>
- Note: Limitation: snowball principal loss depends on "how far below the initial price at expiry after knock-in," not a fixed amount; the high coupon corresponds to the "put seller's" tail risk. In 2022–2024 the CSI 500/1000 knocked in multiple times, with some principal losses of 30%–70% (HuaTai case: Fei net loss about 14.34 million yuan, Shanghai Financial Court (2026) case (2026) Hu 74 Min Zhong No. 527). Cross-reference: Entry 8 (protective put is the reverse "buy insurance" move).

### 5. Structured products: capped upside, unclear risk exposure
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: Reject products whose nested clauses you can't understand; before buying, ask for and read the "payoff structure" section of the prospectus in full.
- In plain terms: The fancier the product, the more it helps the seller hide the clause unfavorable to you. If you can't read the seventh layer of nesting, don't buy by default—what you save may not be return but principal.
- Payoff: Structured products (e.g., structured notes, snowballs, principal/partial-principal-protected notes) bundle "bond + embedded derivative," with capped upside and multiple layers: participation rate, barriers, knock-in, capped return, clauses ordinary people can't compute real probabilities on. The SEC investor bulletin notes structured notes are complex, often issued above fair value (with sales/hedging costs), with very poor secondary liquidity and possible large discounts on early redemption; so-called "principal protection" depends only on the issuer's credit—after Lehman's collapse its structured-note holders, as unsecured creditors, nearly lost everything. Most structured notes aren't principal-protected, and the upside is capped (e.g., at 50% participation, a 20% index rise gives you only 10%), while the downside may be fully yours. Complex structures help the seller hide unfavorable clauses. The CSRC's Measures for the Supervision and Administration of Derivative Trading (second draft for comment) has explicitly "restricted the development of excessively complex derivative contracts." If you can't read the seventh layer of nesting, don't buy by default.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: SEC Office of Investor Education and Advocacy (Investor Bulletin). Investor Bulletin: Structured Notes (complex structure, issue price above fair value, poor liquidity, issuer credit risk). <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins-76>; SEC Office of Investor Education and Advocacy (Investor Bulletin). Structured Notes with Principal Protection: Note the Terms ("principal protection" depends on issuer credit, Lehman case). <http://www.investor.gov/news-alerts/investor-bulletins/structured-notes-principal-protection-note-terms-your-investment>; People's Daily (2023). Many derivative-trading behaviors to be banned, ensuring "see-through, controllable" (second draft for comment restricts excessively complex derivative contracts). <https://finance.people.com.cn/n1/2023/1120/c1004-40122013.html>
- Note: Limitation: most structured notes aren't principal-protected, with capped upside and clauses like participation rate, barriers, knock-in; issue price often above fair value (with sales/hedging costs). Cross-reference: Entry 4 (a snowball's essence is also a structured / exotic option, a put seller).

### 6. Graded funds have exited the stage, but are a living lesson
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Remember this history; spot new variants of "leverage structures in fund clothing" (high-premium sector LOFs, leveraged ETFs, etc.).
- In plain terms: The once "leverage wonder" graded-B lost retail everything in days in the crash, zeroed by down-conversion. It's been cleared out, but the "leverage + high premium" playbook keeps reappearing in new clothing—be wary when you meet it.
- Payoff: Graded funds (born 2007, peak scale about 500 billion yuan) split the parent fund into A (stable) / B (aggressive); B borrows A's money to form leverage—leverage weakens as net value rises, spikes as it falls, showing "slow up, fast down." In the 2015 crash, graded-B hit limit-down consecutively and couldn't be sold, and multiple down-conversions zeroed the premium instantly; many retail lost everything in days, with discount losses up to 90%; the regulator then required in the 2018 Asset Management Rules (资管新规) that public funds not be graded, with the stock cleared up by end-2020. The CSRC made clear in 2020 that "the standardized rectification of public graded funds will not be extended," and from 2021 graded funds exited the stage, converting to LOFs or liquidating. It is the most vivid "leveraged-retail trap" textbook: high-premium B's bubble bursts on conversion/liquidation, and those above 100% premium (e.g., Biopharma B at 235% premium) could lose big on buying. The lesson—leverage structures in "fund" clothing: ordinary people don't touch.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: China Securities Regulatory Commission (2020). Spokesperson Q&A: standardized rectification of public graded funds will not be extended (completed by end-2020). <https://cs.com.cn/xwzx/hg/202007/t20200731_6081783.html>; China Aviation News (2020). Goodbye, graded funds! (born 2007, peak scale about 500 billion yuan, exited from 2021; extreme-market discount loss up to 90%). <https://ep.cannews.com.cn/index.php?banmianid=2331&c=PhonePaper&id=87682&m=showWen&paperid=7>; 21st Century Business Herald (2020). - Note: Limitation: already cleared, but "leveraged funds / high-premium B" reappear in new forms (e.g., some sector LOFs, leveraged ETFs); the lesson is "don't touch leverage structures you can't understand." Cross-reference: Entry 7 (leveraged ETF is the modern vehicle for "leverage").

### 7. Leveraged ETFs are unfit for long-term holding
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Understand the drag of "daily rebalancing" (volatility decay); give up the illusion that "holding a year = underlying × multiple."
- In plain terms: A 2x leveraged ETF held a year doesn't mean "underlying +10%, you +20%." In a choppy market, volatility decay secretly eats the return—you may see "the index didn't fall, but your money did."
- Payoff: Leveraged / inverse ETFs are "daily reset," targeting the day's multiple (e.g., 2x, 3x), not the holding-period cumulative multiple. Because of daily compounding + rebalancing, long-term performance deviates significantly from "underlying × multiple," with "volatility decay" in choppy markets. SEC example: over 4 months an index rose only +2%, yet the 2x leveraged ETF fell -6%, and a 3x leveraged ETF (different index) fell -53% while the underlying rose +8%; two-day scenario: index -10%/+10% back to 99 (-1%), the 3x leveraged ETF back to 91 (-9%). The principle: the index's single-day move, squared, is amplified, so the loss base on down days is larger than the gain base on up days. In a one-way rise a leveraged ETF can outperform, but the "hold long term" assumption doesn't hold—holding a year ≠ underlying +10%, you +20%. FINRA also warns it's unsuitable for buy-and-hold investors. Conclusion: leveraged ETFs suit only intraday / short-swing trading; held long term, volatility secretly eats the return.
- Evidence grade: A

Leveraged and Inverse ETFs: Specialized Products with Extra Risks for Buy-and-Hold Investors. <https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-alerts/sec>; FINRA. ### 8. Covered / protective strategies are useful only to professional holders
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Admit you aren't that person; if you must try, first have a stock position and can continuously monitor assignment / roll / exercise.
- In plain terms: Sellers love to pitch "covered-call income," but that requires you already hold the stock and manage it daily. Ordinary people want peace of mind—best not to touch; you lack both the underlying and the time, and bear assignment / margin-call execution risk.
- Payoff: Covered call (hold stock and sell a call to collect premium) and protective put (hold stock and buy a put as insurance) are of small use to "professional investors who already hold long term and can continuously monitor": covered adds income in choppy markets, put floors losses in a crash. But this is professional operation of the seller/buyer role, requiring you already have a stock position and to manage exercise / roll / assignment daily. CBOE material shows covered call is a "Level 1" basic strategy, but its upside is capped (stock gets called away, missing the upside), and the put costs premium continuously (Theta works against you). The China Futures Association risk disclosure states plainly that "selling options can cause huge losses, exceeding all margin." For retail who want peace of mind, no position, no time to manage, such strategies are net-negative and time-consuming—you lack both the underlying and the time, and bear assignment / margin-call execution risk. If ordinary people want "peace of mind," they shouldn't use option strategies to replace asset allocation.
- Evidence grade: B
