# XVII. Macro Cycles

Macro cycles are the "weather" of asset pricing: interest rates, inflation, the exchange rate, and economic prosperity determine the direction of the discount rate for almost all assets. Understanding this mechanism helps you read the real signal behind financial news, rather than being led around by narratives like "rate cuts must mean a rally" or "a recession is coming."

But the real discipline this chapter delivers is: understanding the mechanism ≠ predicting the turning point. You can judge the broad direction of interest rates, but exactly which month rates will rise, which recession, which day the bottom forms—even highly paid chief economists and fund managers who claim to time the market systematically fail at it (Entry 4, Entry 10). More painfully, even the famous "Merrill Lynch Investment Clock" type of macro-allocation model frequently fails out of sample.

So treat the macro as a "constraint" and a "risk budget," not a buy/sell instruction: use it to decide your duration, cash ratio, overseas exposure, and hedging—not to bet on direction. Resilient allocation matters far more than being right about the timing once.

This chapter's entries are grouped by theme below (entry numbers in parentheses): interest-rate mechanism and bond prices (Entry 1, Entry 7); yield-curve and economic-cycle signals (Entry 2, Entry 6, Entry 12); inflation, gold, and monetary easing (Entry 3, Entry 8, Entry 11); unreliability of macro forecasting and timing (Entry 4, Entry 10); exchange-rate multiplier effect on overseas returns (Entry 5, Entry 9).

### 1. Interest rates are the gravity of asset prices
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The time to understand one or two pages of the discounting model and the concept of duration (almost zero cost, but most retail investors have never thought about it).
- In plain terms: Interest rates are like gravity for assets. A rate hike = stronger gravity, prices fall; a rate cut = weaker gravity, prices float up. Don't just watch the headlines; watch the direction of rates.
- Payoff: The value of almost all assets comes from discounting future cash flows, and the interest rate sits in the denominator. When the Federal Reserve raised the federal funds target rate from 0–0.25% by a cumulative 425 basis points to 4.25%–4.5% in 2022, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield spiked from about 1.5% to 4.2%, the 2-year from 0.75% to 4.3%, and bond prices crashed accordingly—the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index fell 13.0% for the year, its worst annual return since records began in 1976. Stocks and real estate came under the same pressure, because a higher discount rate lowers the present value of any long-duration asset. This is the underlying logic for understanding "why the market falls when rates rise," and the starting point for judging the duration risk of the assets you hold.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve (2022). Federal funds target rate hikes and Treasury yield moves (10Y 1.5%→4.2%, 2Y 0.75%→4.3%). <https://finance.yahoo.com/news/why-ordinary-investors-got-hit-so-hard-in-2022-morning-brief-110004332.html>; Winthrop Wealth (2022). 2022 Market Review and Outlook (Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index -13.0%). <https://winthropwealth.com/commentary/2022-market-review-and-outlook/>
- Note: Evidence grade A—the inverse relationship between rates and prices is an axiom of asset pricing, and there is a full real-world test from 2022. Graded A because bond price–yield inversion is determined by the duration formula, not an empirical correlation. Limitation: the longer the duration, the more sensitive to rates; short-term bonds are less affected. Cross-reference: Entry 7 (bonds stabilize at the end of the tightening cycle), Entry 3 (inflation and long bonds).

### 2. Yield-curve inversion signals recession, but is not an alarm clock
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The opportunity cost of misreading "invert and sell immediately" and prematurely clearing your positions on that basis.
- In plain terms: Short-term rates higher than long-term (inversion) has historically often been a precursor to recession, but "how long after, and whether it comes at all" nobody knows precisely. Treat it as an alarm, not a trigger.
- Payoff: The New York Fed's Probit model built on the 10-year minus 3-month Treasury spread (10Y-3M) shows that the probability of recession in the next 12 months rises significantly after an inversion; parameters α=-0.5333, β=-0.6330, a 0-basis-point spread corresponds to about a 25% probability, -100bp about 50%, -200bp about 70%. But the model also concedes its precursor record is uneven: before the 1973–75, 2001, and 2020 recessions it never came close to 30%. The 2-year vs 10-year spread inverted before every recession since 1969, but with lead times ranging from 6 to 24 months, and the deep inversion of 2022 had still not delivered an NBER-defined recession as of early 2026. It's a useful early-warning dashboard, not a fixed-time alarm clock.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of New York (updated). The Yield Curve as a Leading Indicator (10Y-3M term spread recession probability model). <https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/capital_markets/ycfaq.html>; Federal Reserve Bank of New York (2026). Probability of U.S. Recession Predicted by Treasury Spread (Prob_Rec.pdf, parameters α=-0.5333, β=-0.6330). <https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/capital_markets/Prob_Rec.pdf>; Estrella & Mishkin (1996). The Yield Curve as a Predictor of U.S. Recessions (Current Issues in Economics and Finance 2(7)). <https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/current_issues/ci2-7.pdf>
- Note: Evidence grade A—based on decades of statistics and the official central-bank model. Graded A because the sample is long and the method is public, but it is a probabilistic warning, not a deterministic signal. Limitation: unstable lead time, false positives (e.g., 2022). Cross-reference: Entry 6 (recession wrong-kills), Entry 12 (expectation gap).

### 3. In high inflation, both cash and long bonds get hurt
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The effort to build the asset-allocation awareness that "inflation erodes purchasing power" (almost zero).
- In plain terms: Money sitting idle gets quietly devalued by inflation; long bonds also fall when rates are hiked to fight inflation. To withstand inflation, you need to hold some assets that move with prices.
- Payoff: High inflation hits both cash purchasing power and long-duration bonds. From 1973 to 1981 the U.S. CPI averaged about 7.4% a year, and the nominal 10-year Treasury's real annual return was negative (e.g., in 1980 the nominal return was only -1.29%, and -13.81% after deducting inflation), while synthetic TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) over the same period recorded double-digit returns of 11%–15%—exactly the problem the U.S. Treasury set out to solve by formally issuing TIPS in 1997: swap "nominal coupon" for "real coupon plus inflation compensation." The World Gold Council estimates gold's annualized return from 1971 to 2023 was about 8%, significantly higher than the CPI's 4% and the 3-month U.S. Treasury's 4.4% over the same period, showing that physical / store-of-value assets can beat inflation over the long run. But note: in the 1970s stocks and bonds sometimes "rose and fell together" (positive correlation), and the traditional 60/40 diversification failed—confirming that when inflation is higher than about 2.5%, the stock-bond correlation turns from negative to positive. No single asset class can perfectly hedge; the key is to diversify and hold real assets.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Federal Reserve Bank of Boston (2009). A TIPS Scorecard: Are TIPS Accomplishing What They Were Supposed to Accomplish? (Working Papers No. 09-8). <https://www.bostonfed.org/-/media/Documents/Workingpapers/PDF/ppdp0908.pdf>; PanAgora (updated). Risk Parity & Inflation (1970–1979 annualized CPI 7.4%, TIPS real return ~3 percentage points above CPI). <https://www.panagora.com/assets/PanAgora-Risk-Parity-and-Inflation.pdf>; World Gold Council (2024). "Building-block" method for estimating gold's expected return (1971–2023 gold annualized 8% vs CPI 4%). <https://china.gold.org/page/18826>
- Note: Evidence grade A—consistent conclusions from multiple institutions' long-term data. Limitation: the 1970s is an extreme sample; TIPS "inflation protection" only locks in if held to maturity, and short-term is still affected by rate volatility (duration). Cross-reference: Entry 1 (rates and long bonds), Entry 8 (gold), Entry 11 (the double-edged sword of easing).

### 4. Don't try to time the macro; even professionals are often wrong
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Trading fees, taxes, and missed-rally losses from repeatedly adjusting positions based on "experts say it's going to crash / rally."
- In plain terms: Even high-paid chief economists can't pin the turning point. You churn in and out on "I feel it's going to drop"—basically working for the broker. Set your allocation and touch it less.
- Payoff: The difficulty of macro and market-direction forecasting has been repeatedly confirmed by many studies. CXO Advisory tracked 6,582 forecasts by 68 "market-timing experts" from 1999 to 2012, of which 42 (61.8%) had accuracy below 50%, and after deducting transaction costs "not a single one" actually made a profit; another study covering 28 timers' over 4,500 forecasts from 2000 to 2012 found only 10 beat the 50% mark, and none beat the broad market. Even the famous "Merrill Lynch Investment Clock" frequently fails out of sample: Huatai Securities' backtest found that after 2011, blindly applying the Merrill Clock to Chinese asset allocation was only 28% accurate, rising to 72% after adding an interest-rate dimension; Sina Finance's statistics show the model was 88% correct in the U.S. from 1970 to 2020 but only 40% in China. Nobel laureate Sharpe calculated that a timer needs to be right about 74% on both the "down" and the "rebound" sides to beat a passive portfolio that is always fully invested. Conclusion: timing works occasionally, but systematically beating the market over the long run is nearly impossible.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard Year-End 2025 (79% of large-cap active equity funds trailed the S&P 500; 94% over 20 years). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research-insights/spiva/>; Advisor Perspectives (2020). Market Timing Is the Enemy of Investment Success (CXO review: 68 timers, none profitable after costs). <https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2020/01/27/market-timing-is-the-enemy-of-investment-success>; Sina Finance (2021). Is the traditional Merrill Lynch Investment Clock dead in China? (1970–2020 Merrill Clock 88% correct in U.S., 40% in China). <https://finance.sina.com.cn/stock/hkstock/hkstocknews/2021-03-04/doc-ikftssaq0788452.shtml>
- Note: Evidence grade A—consistent conclusions across multiple independent studies, a strong consensus in behavioral finance and empirical research. Graded A because it is not a single paper but repeated cross-sample verification. Limitation: a few "superforecasters" can marginally outperform after training, but they are extremely rare. Cross-reference: Entry 10 (doomsday prophecies), Entry 12 (expectation gap).

### 5. The dollar's strength is multiplied directly onto your overseas return
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The attention to calculate one extra step of exchange rate before buying overseas assets.
- In plain terms: You earned 10% on U.S. stocks, the dollar fell 5%, and back in yuan you only made 5%. This exchange-rate multiplication is something many people remember only after buying.
- Payoff: When you invest in U.S. stocks, U.S. Treasuries, or Hong Kong stocks in your home currency (e.g., yuan), the final home-currency return = the asset's local-currency return × the exchange-rate move. Vanguard's research shows that from 2000 to 2010 the dollar's depreciation contributed about +3% annualized to U.S. investors' overseas assets, while from 2010 to 2020 the dollar's appreciation eroded about -1.5% annualized—a ten-year reversal of direction, a gap equivalent to about 4.5 percentage points per year. The same applies to yuan investors: a weaker dollar eats into a slice of foreign-currency gains, a stronger dollar adds to them. Vanguard also points out that currency has no intrinsic long-term return (no coupon, no earnings growth), affecting only volatility; for stocks whether to hedge mainly depends on risk tolerance rather than return, but for bonds, if unhedged, volatility is amplified to near stock levels, weakening their "ballast" role. The exchange rate is an invisible second layer of return or loss.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Vanguard (2023). The U.S. dollar is unlikely to continue defying gravity (dollar overvalued ~12%, ~75% probability of decade decline, unhedged overseas stocks ~+1.1% annualized). <https://advisors.vanguard.com/insights/article/the-us-dollar-is-unlikely-to-continue-defying-gravity>; Vanguard (updated). Global equity investing: The benefits of diversification and sizing your allocation (currency has no intrinsic long-term return, mainly contributes volatility). <https://www.vanguardmexico.com/content/dam/intl/americas/documents/mexico/en/global-equity-investing-diversification-sizing.pdf>
- Note: Evidence grade A—long-term data from a large asset manager. Limitation: the exchange-rate direction itself is not reliably predictable, and Vanguard also emphasizes long-run mean reversion. Hedge bonds, tolerate unhedged stocks for diversification. Cross-reference: Entry 9 (exchange-rate multiplier), Entry 1 (rate spreads drive the exchange rate).

### 6. In a recession, good companies get wrong-killed too—opportunity and trap
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The work and time to distinguish "temporary decline" from "fundamentally broken"; counter-cyclical buying in a falling market requires some capital and mental effort.
- In plain terms: In a crash, good stocks fall too—that's a chance to pick up cheap shares; but don't take "fell a lot" as "cheap"; some are genuinely rotten. Tell temporary from permanent.
- Payoff: In a systematic decline (such as a recession signaled by a yield-curve inversion), quality assets are often sold indiscriminately, offering a buying opportunity; but in the same downturn many companies genuinely have deteriorating fundamentals. The key is whether the decline is market panic or a worse company. Academic research uses "earnings surprise" to measure fundamentals: from Ball & Brown (1968) to Bernard & Thomas (1990) it was repeatedly shown that companies beating earnings estimates still show about 2.9% cumulative abnormal return drift (PEAD) over the 60 trading days after the announcement, showing the market often underreacts to good companies and oversells bad ones. Combined with Entry 2's recession warning, focusing in panic-driven wrong-kills on companies "still beating earnings, with low debt ratios, and an unbroken moat" gives a higher win rate; but be sure to avoid the "value trap"—companies with downgraded earnings and a deteriorating industry structure are not cheap no matter how far they fall.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bernard & Thomas (1990). Evidence That the Size-Adjusted Returns Around Earnings Announcements Are Related to Subsequent Earnings (PEAD drift). <http://en.m.wikipedia.com/wiki/Post-earnings_announcement_drift>; Federal Reserve Bank of New York (updated). The Yield Curve as a Leading Indicator (recession warning). <https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/capital_markets/ycfaq.html>
- Note: Evidence grade A—PEAD is a long-validated anomaly. Limitation: identifying wrong-kills requires fundamental research ability; ordinary investors can use broad-market index dollar-cost-averaging in panic periods as a substitute for "picking single stocks." Cross-reference: Entry 2 (curve inversion), Entry 12 (expectation gap).

### 7. Near the end of a tightening cycle, bonds often stabilize first
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The psychological misjudgment of "afraid to buy bonds once they fall," plus a little effort to study the rate turning point.
- In plain terms: When everyone is scared of rate hikes, bonds may have already bottomed. Once rates peak and fall, bonds stabilize before stocks—don't wait until everyone is optimistic to remember to buy.
- Payoff: Bond prices move inversely to rates, and bonds are extremely sensitive to expectations of "rates peaking." In 2022 the Fed's aggressive hikes made the Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Bond Index plummet 13.0%, but once the market began expecting the hikes to be near an end and yields peaked at end-2022 (the index's yield-to-worst rose to 4.68%, the highest since 2008), the index rebounded +5.53% in 2023—bonds repaired ahead of any reversal in stock-market sentiment. This is exactly the "leading indicator" property of the rate cycle: long-bond prices often bottom months before the policy rate actually turns. In other words, by the time the news is fully optimistic and everyone shouts "you can buy bonds now," yields have already fallen and most of the price gain has likely been realized. For ordinary investors, buying with short duration or a ladder is steadier than trying to nail the bottom precisely.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Diamond Hill (2023). Rising Rates: Lessons from 2022 (Bloomberg US Aggregate 2022 -13.01%, 2023 +5.53%; YTW from 1.75% to 4.68%). <https://www.diamond-hill.com/news/a-942/articles/rising-rates-lessons-from-2022/>; Winthrop Wealth (2022). 2022 Market Review and Outlook (after rates peaked Agg +1.9% in Q4). <https://winthropwealth.com/commentary/2022-market-review-and-outlook/>
- Note: Evidence grade A—price–rate inversion is a pricing axiom with real-world validation. Limitation: if inflation resurges and rates rise again, bonds will fall again; the "end" can only be confirmed in hindsight. Cross-reference: Entry 1 (gravity of rates), Entry 3 (long bonds and inflation).

### 8. Gold is insurance, not a staple
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Managing expectations of gold as a "sure-thing investment"; the opportunity cost of the capital occupied by a 5–10% allocation.
- In plain terms: Gold pays no interest and doesn't rise much long term; its use is "when the world breaks, it holds the line." A small allocation as insurance is enough; don't make it your main asset.
- Payoff: Long term, gold's value lies in tail-risk hedging rather than appreciation. The World Gold Council's "building-block" method estimates gold's annualized nominal return from 1971 to 2023 was about 8%, higher than the CPI's 4% and the 3-month U.S. Treasury's 4.4% over the same period—but this mainly comes from currency devaluation and safe-haven demand after leaving the gold standard, not steady "interest." It generates no cash flow, yet its volatility is not small (it also corrected visibly in 2022 as real rates rose). In stagflation, currency-credit crises, and geopolitical conflict, gold's correlation with stocks and bonds falls, letting it play an insurance role; but treating it as a staple and loading up drags down the portfolio's long-term compounding (stocks' long-run annualized return is far above gold's). Practical advice: allocate 5%–10% as tail-risk insurance, and prefer low-cost physical gold or gold ETFs over high-premium mining stocks.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: World Gold Council (2024). "Building-block" method for estimating gold's expected return (1971–2023 gold annualized 8% vs CPI 4%, 3-month U.S. Treasury 4.4%). <https://china.gold.org/page/18826>; PanAgora (updated). Risk Parity & Inflation (gold's real return in inflation periods above CPI). <https://www.panagora.com/assets/PanAgora-Risk-Parity-and-Inflation.pdf>
- Note: Evidence grade A—WGC long-term data. Limitation: gold's return is highly dependent on the monetary and geopolitical environment, and the post-gold-standard sample is bullishly biased; allocation ratio varies by person. Cross-reference: Entry 3 (inflation protection), Entry 11 (easing dilutes currency value).

### 9. The exchange rate has a multiplier effect on your final return
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: One extra layer of thinking to assess exchange-rate exposure and (optional) hedging cost before cross-border investing.
- In plain terms: The same overseas asset—the exchange rate can make you earn more or earn nothing. Before loading up on a foreign currency, first figure out whether you're bullish on the asset or incidentally betting on the exchange rate.
- Payoff: The home-currency return of an overseas asset gets amplified or shrunk by the exchange-rate move over the holding period—an invisible layer of "multiplication." Vanguard points out that currency has no intrinsic long-term return (no coupon, no earnings growth), affecting only volatility: for international stocks, whether to hedge mainly depends on risk tolerance; but for international bonds, if unhedged, volatility is amplified to near stock levels, seriously weakening their role as the portfolio's "ballast." For yuan investors, in a strong-dollar period (e.g., 2010–2020 dollar appreciation eroded U.S. investors' overseas assets by about -1.5%/year) foreign-currency gains are amplified, and vice versa in a weak period. So loading up on a single foreign currency equals, on top of being bullish on the asset, extra betting on the exchange-rate direction—if you don't want to take that, use a hedged ETF or a home-currency-denominated product to "set this multiplier to 1."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Vanguard (2023). The U.S. dollar is unlikely to continue defying gravity (unhedged overseas stocks' long-term annualized FX contribution ~±1%–3%). <https://advisors.vanguard.com/insights/article/the-us-dollar-is-unlikely-to-continue-defying-gravity>; Vanguard (updated). Global equity investing: The benefits of diversification and sizing your allocation (unhedged bonds' volatility rises to stock level). <https://www.vanguardmexico.com/content/dam/intl/americas/documents/mexico/en/global-equity-investing-diversification-sizing.pdf>
- Note: Evidence grade A—long-term research from a large asset manager. Limitation: the exchange-rate direction cannot be reliably predicted, and hedging has costs; for long-term stock investors, staying unhedged can instead lower correlation with home-country assets and hedge domestic inflation. Cross-reference: Entry 5 (dollar multiplier), Entry 1 (rate spreads drive the exchange rate).

### 10. Discount the "doomsday prophecies"; prophets win by frequency
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The panic triggered by "crash imminent" articles and possible mistaken liquidation.
- In plain terms: Those "crash any day now" prophets, if they shout long enough, will eventually be right once. Don't be scared by one lucky hit; look at how many times they've been wrong over the past decade.
- Payoff: Philip Tetlock, a psychologist at UC Berkeley, spent 20 years tracking 284 "experts" whose job is commenting on political and economic trends, collecting 82,361 predictions; the conclusion is that their overall accuracy is no better than "a dart-throwing chimpanzee," and the more famous the prophet, the less accurate they tend to be (overconfidence). In the tripartite classification, the experts as a whole did even worse than the simple baseline of "each of three outcomes 1/3 probability"; things they claimed 100% certain eventually came true only about 80% of the time, and things they were 80% sure of only about 65%. This explains why "doomsday prophets" shouting crash for ten years eventually hit one right—one hit is enough to deify them, and the dozens of prior misses nobody remembers. Think in base rates: shouting crash for ten years and hitting once doesn't mean they have predictive power. Response: build a written allocation discipline and keep macro noise out of your trading instructions.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: The New Yorker (2005). Everybody's an Expert (on Tetlock's 20-year study: 284 experts, 82,361 predictions, overall no better than random). <https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2005/12/05/everybodys-an-expert>; University of Pennsylvania, Wharton (2007). Review of Expert Political Judgment (Tschoegl & Armstrong: 284 experts, 82,361 predictions, experts no better than informed non-experts). <https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/08/Tetlock-16.pdf>
- Note: Evidence grade A—20-year large-scale study, robust conclusion. Limitation: a few "fox-type" thinkers can marginally outperform after training, and ordinary investors cannot tell them apart. Cross-reference: Entry 4 (unreliable timing), Entry 12 (expectation gap).

### 11. Easing lifts assets but also dilutes currency value—a double-edged sword
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The simplified understanding that "the central bank prints money = stocks must rise," and the drawdown from not reducing positions in time when liquidity recedes.
- In plain terms: The central bank eases, stocks and houses rise, but money also gets cheaper. This is a two-sided bet—your assets rise, and purchasing power quietly shrinks. Don't see only one side.
- Payoff: Quantitative easing (QE) pushes down long-end rates and lifts risky assets through "portfolio rebalancing" and "signaling" channels. The UN's World Economic Situation and Prospects notes that in the decade after the first round of QE the S&P 500 rose over 200%, the Euro Stoxx index nearly doubled, and U.S. and European house prices were also lifted by the extra liquidity; during the Fed's three rounds of LSAP (QE1–3), academic estimates put QE1 alone as lifting the S&P 500 by about 10% cumulatively and lowering the 10-year Treasury yield by 0.5%–1%. But the other side of the coin: broad money / GDP rose from 79.5% in 2007 to 117% in 2021 (U.S.), diluting currency purchasing power; in 2022 the Fed's sharp turn to hikes quickly gave back the above valuation windfall (Aggregate Bond -13%, S&P -19%). Easing is bullish for risky assets short term, and long term takes part of the gains back through inflation. Understand this layer and you won't be unidirectionally held hostage by the "easing narrative."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: United Nations (2023). World Economic Situation and Prospects as of mid-2023 (S&P 500 +200% in decade after QE, broad money / GDP 79.5%→117%). <https://sdgs.un.org/sites/default/files/2023-07/SIDS_WESP%202023_Mid-Year_Final.pdf>; Federal Reserve Bank of New York (2019). Ten Years Later—Did QE Work? (QE1 lifted S&P 500 +10% cumulatively, 10Y yield down 0.5%–1%). <https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2019/05/ten-years-laterdid-qe-work/>
- Note: Evidence grade A—central-bank and UN documents + academic event studies agree. Limitation: QE's effect depends on the economic environment and exit pace; the 2022 tightening reversed part of the effect. Cross-reference: Entry 3 (inflation), Entry 8 (gold insurance), Entry 1 (rates inverse).

### 12. The real signal in economic news is "better/worse than expected"
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=no payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The attention bias of being led by the headline "GDP grew X%" itself.
- In plain terms: News says "the economy is bad," but not as bad as people thought—stocks may rise. The market trades on "beating or missing expectations," not the number itself.
- Payoff: The market reaction looks at the "actual vs expected gap," not the absolute number. A "bad but better than panic expectations" data point makes assets rise instead. This has solid evidence at the micro level: the "post-earnings announcement drift" (PEAD), pioneered by Ball & Brown (1968) and deepened by Bernard & Thomas (1990), shows that stocks beating earnings estimates can show a cumulative abnormal return of about 2.9% over the 60 trading days after the announcement, while those missing estimates go the other way—the market often underreacts to surprises. The same holds macro: the "expectation gap" in PMI, CPI, and nonfarm payrolls often drives asset prices more than the values themselves. For ordinary investors, rather than predicting macro turning points (Entry 4), it's better to watch "whether the market has already priced in pessimism": when consensus expectations are extremely pessimistic yet the data only slightly misses, that is instead an opportunity for risky assets to repair. Watching the "expectation gap" gets you closer to market logic than watching the "number."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Bernard & Thomas (1990). Evidence That the Size-Adjusted Returns Around Earnings Announcements Are Related to Subsequent Earnings (PEAD, 60-day drift ~2.9%). <http://en.m.wikipedia.com/wiki/Post-earnings_announcement_drift>; S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026). SPIVA U.S. Scorecard (active management can't sustainably beat the index, confirming the difficulty of forecasting). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/research-insights/spiva/>
- Note: Evidence grade A—PEAD is a long-standing anomaly, and the macro expectation gap has abundant event studies. Limitation: expectation-gap trading has a high execution threshold for retail; indexation + rebalancing is more practical. Cross-reference: Entry 4 (no timing), Entry 6 (wrong-kills), Entry 2 (curve warning).
