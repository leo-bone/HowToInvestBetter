# XII. The Cold Calculator

This chapter does only arithmetic, no opinions. Every entry below is piled up from verifiable formulas and public historical data, to hand you a "benchmark ruler"—so that when others use stories, sentiment, or candlestick charts to talk you into trading, you can fire back with these cold numbers: "Have you actually run the math?" Every return figure is sourced; where this book itself ran the formula, it's clearly labeled "this book's estimate," never passed off as raw data. This chapter is not investment advice; change the assumptions on fees, inflation, taxes, or sample period by a hair and the conclusion shifts—plug in your own parameters.

One caveat: most of these numbers come from U.S. or China statistics of specific years, and carry sample-period bias, survivorship bias, and cross-country differences. They tell you "the likely case" and "the order of magnitude," not "the certainty." The only moats that truly stand the test of time are two: push costs to the floor, and stretch the holding period to the longest. Everything else is mostly noise.

This section's entries are grouped by theme (entry numbers in parentheses):
- Fee and cost erosion (how management fees and subscription/redemption rhythm eat returns): Entries 1, 7, 14
- The cost of frequent trading and leverage: Entries 2, 13, 15

- Holding period, dollar-cost averaging, and asset allocation: Entries 8, 11
- Asset attributes and institutional-arbitrage dividends: Entries 9, 10, 12

### 1. A 1% annual fee eats about 1/4 of final value over 30 years
<!-- tags: money=0 time=0 will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The "convenience" of picking a 1% fee product over a 0.1% one, and the laziness of not checking the fee table buried in the fund contract.
- In plain terms: You agonize over a 1% gain or loss on a fund, yet ignore the 1% management fee quietly deducted every year. Under compounding, that 1% devours a quarter of your principal's growth—and it's the most certain "negative return" there is.
- Payoff: The compound final-value formula FV = PV(1+r)^n. Suppose principal 100,000 yuan, gross annual return 7%, held 30 years: at 0% fee, FV = 100k × (1.07)^30 ≈ 761,000 yuan; if 1% management fee is charged yearly, the real annual return drops to 6%, FV = 100k × (1.06)^30 ≈ 574,000 yuan, about 24.5% less than the zero-fee case. Even just a 0.9-point difference (1% vs 0.1% fee), the former's final value is only about 77.6% of the latter's, i.e., about 22.4% less. Run another: 1.5% vs 0.15% fee over 20 years: 100k × [(1.07)^20 − (1.055)^20] ≈ 387k − 292k = 95,000 yuan gap (this book's estimate, assuming 7% gross). The SEC's same-basis example: 100,000 USD growing at 4% for 20 years, a 1% annual fee is worth about 179k, a 0.25% annual fee worth about 208k—a gap near 29,000 USD. The Rule of 72 tells us 7% doubles in about 10.3 years, but a 1% fee drags the annual return to 6%, stretching the doubling to about 12 years—a certain loss that never comes back.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: People's Bank of China (2021), PBOC Announcement No. 3 of 2021 (Article 4: loan annualized rate may use the compounding method, i.e., internal rate of return method, establishing that annualization must include all costs). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2021-03/31/content_5597156.htm>; SEC (no year), Investor Bulletin: How Fees and Expenses Affect Your Investment Portfolio (100k USD, 4%, 20 years, 0.25% / 0.50% / 1.00% fee comparison table). <https://www.sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-bulletins/ib_fees_expenses.html>; Investopedia (no year), Compound Interest (FV = PV(1+i)^n and Rule of 72). <https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/compoundinterest.asp>
- Note: The 1.5% vs 0.15% 20-year gap is this book's compound-formula estimate, not raw statistics; a fund's true hidden costs also include trading commission, stamp duty, and bid-ask spread, so the real erosion is often larger than the fee-table number. Grade A: the formula and SEC example are both public and verifiable, but the 7% gross return is an assumption, and the conclusion is sensitive to it.

### 2. Frequent trading: the highest-turnover group underperforms the lowest by about 6.5 percentage points a year
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The thrill of "I'm operating" from frequent buying and selling, and the commission, stamp duty, and slippage paid for it.
- In plain terms: The more you churn, the poorer you get—not a joke. The group that keeps turnover lowest simply earns about 6.5 points/year more long-term than the churning group—and that 6.5 points is almost impossible to reliably make back through stock-picking.
- Payoff: Barber & Odean, using tens of thousands of U.S. brokerage accounts, found that the most actively trading 20% of households underperformed the least actively trading 20% by about 6.5 percentage points a year in net returns. Put 6.5 points into compounding for scale: 100,000 yuan principal at 10% annualized (the 3.5% net group) becomes about 1.99 million yuan in 20 years; if you could reach the 10% net group, about 6.73 million yuan in 20 years—the 6.5-point gap balloons into a final-value difference of over 4.7 million yuan in 20 years (this book estimates via FV = PV(1+r)^n). The SEC also explicitly warns that short-term trading in volatile markets causes "significant and unanticipated losses." The Rule of 72 also shows the scale: if the high-frequency group drags annualized from 10% to 3.5%, the doubling time stretches from 7.2 years to 20.6 years—the same money takes thirteen extra years to double, time that could have earned compounding.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Barber, B. & Odean, T. (2000), Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth: The Common Stock Investment Performance of Individual Investors, Journal of Finance, 55(2), 773–806; SEC (2021), Investor Alert: Thinking About Investing in the Latest Hot Stock? Understand the Risks of Short-Term Trading Based on Social Media. <https://www.sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-and-bulletins/risks-short-term-trading-based-social-media-investor-alert>
- Note: The 6.5 percentage points is a U.S.-sample conclusion; A-shares, with stamp duty and high volatility, may see larger or different actual losses from frequent trading; this book's final-value gap is formula-derived, not the original paper's number. Grade A: conclusion from a peer-reviewed journal and a regulatory warning, method verifiable.

### 3. Smallest retail lose most: accounts under 100,000 yuan average −20.53%
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Admitting the behavioral pattern of "small accounts chasing rallies and selling dips," and dropping the illusion that "little money means you must trade more."
- In plain terms: The less money you have, the more violently you trade, the more you lose. This isn't luck—it's nailed down by account-level data: small accounts doing momentum trading simply lose on average.
- Payoff: The SSE team and Tsinghua PBCSF, based on proprietary data covering over 53 million accounts from January 2016 to June 2019, found: retail accounts with market value under 100,000 yuan averaged an annualized return of −20.53%, the 100k–500k group −9.98%, while institutional investors averaged +11.22% and corporate legal persons +6.68%. Broken down per account: under-100k retail lost 2,457 yuan a year on average, of which stock selection lost 1,532 yuan (62%), timing lost 774 yuan (31%), and trading fees 151 yuan (6%). In other words, the smaller the account, the more principal it feeds to commission and wrong timing. If you convert the average 2,457 yuan annual loss at a 7% opportunity cost over 20 years, it amounts to a large share of the substantial appreciation 100,000 yuan principal could have generated (this book estimates via annuity opportunity cost)—small accounts aren't "losing little," they're continuously feeding principal to friction and wrong timing.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Shi Donghui, Zhang Xiaoyan, et al. (2020), Passive or Active? The Roles of Retail Investors and Mutual Funds in the Chinese Stock Market (based on SSE 2016–2019 account-level data); re-quoted in Changsha Evening News (2020-07-28), "Characteristics of Chinese Retail Trading Behavior: Loves Chasing Rallies and Selling Dips, Gambling Mindset, the Less You Buy the More You Lose." <https://www.icswb.com/h/168/20200728/668963.html>; Securities Association of China investor-education material (with SSE per-group annual profit/loss table). <https://investor.amac.org.cn/community/tzzhdzthd/ylj/202304/P020230410489976164138.pdf>
- Note: −20.53% is the mean over the specific choppy-market window 2016.1–2019.6, not any single year's number; a bull-market window would differ significantly; the account classification and sample period determine the magnitude, not that every small account must lose. Grade A: based on full-market account-level data, not a sampled survey.

### 4. Monthly turnover over 100%: annual loss rate about 91.3%
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The operating habit of "turning over your whole position more than once a month."
- In plain terms: People who turn over their whole holding every month—nine of ten lose. Turnover rate is the most honest "loss-probability meter."
- Payoff: The same SSE account-level data shows that accounts with a monthly turnover over 100% (i.e., buy-and-sell amount in a month exceeding total holdings) had an annual loss rate of about 91.3%; by contrast, the low-frequency group's loss probability is far lower. The Rule of 72 also makes sense in reverse: if such accounts drag annualized net return to about −15% via high turnover, principal halves in about 4.8 years (72÷15≈4.8), and after ten years there's little left—high frequency isn't "making quick money," it's the conveyor belt of "losing quick money." Translating the 91.3% loss probability into money: if such accounts average 100,000 yuan principal and −15% net annualized, only about 44,000 yuan remains after 5 years (100k × (0.85)^5, this book's estimate), while the low-turnover accounts are very likely still at the table; high frequency isn't "poor technique," it's "mathematically certain to lose."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Shi Donghui, Zhang Xiaoyan, et al. (2020), as above (SSE 2016–2019 account-level trading and holding data); Securities Association of China investor-education material (with grouped profit/loss table). <https://investor.amac.org.cn/community/tzzhdzthd/ylj/202304/P020230410489976164138.pdf>
- Note: 91.3% is the high-turnover group's loss probability disclosed in that study, a sample statistic not a regulatory caliber; this book's "halving years" via the Rule of 72 is an estimate demonstration. Grade A: same-source account-level data, but the turnover-grouping caliber follows the original paper.

### 5. Over 20 trades a month on average: loss rate about 84.6%
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The habit of watching the tape daily, moving your fingers, treating trading as a "sense of participation."
- In plain terms: Trade frequency is the leading indicator of loss. Change "watch the tape daily" to "check once a month" and you've outrun 80% of the losing crowd.
- Payoff: SSE account data shows accounts trading over 20 times a month on average had a loss rate of about 84.6%. Every trade carries explicit (commission, stamp duty) and implicit (bid-ask spread, impact cost) drag; at A-share unilateral stamp duty 0.05% and broker commission about 0.025%, 20 round-trips a month generates about 0.15% in pure friction cost, evaporating about 1.8 percentage points a year (this book estimates over 12 months), before counting the directional losses from timing errors. Cutting frequency first pulls yourself back from the "certain-loss friction tax." An 84.6% loss rate means only about 1 of every 6.5 high-frequency accounts profits; if you save that monthly-20-trade friction tax (about 1.8 points a year) and put it into a 7% portfolio, 100,000 yuan yields about 210,000 yuan more in 20 years (this book estimates via compounding difference).
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Shi Donghui, Zhang Xiaoyan, et al. (2020), as above; Securities Association of China investor-education material. <https://investor.amac.org.cn/community/tzzhdzthd/ylj/202304/P020230410489976164138.pdf>
- Note: 84.6% comes from SSE account-level grouped statistics; this book's 1.8-percentage-point friction cost is an estimate from current tax/fee rates, actually varies with broker commission and trade size. Grade A: same data source as Entries 3–4.

### 6. Holding under 1 month: profit probability only about 9.3%
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The expectation of fast in-and-out short-term trading, hoping to "make a bundle in a few days."
- In plain terms: People who want to sell a stock held under a month—only one in ten makes money. Short-term trading is the conveyor belt that hands retail money to the market.
- Payoff: SSE account data shows ultra-short-term accounts holding under 1 month had a profit probability of only about 9.3%, an annualized loss of about 18.7%. Converted via real interest rate: a nominal loss of 18.7%, if that year's inflation is about 2%, real purchasing-power loss is about 20.7% (real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation, this book's plug-in estimate). For ultra-short-term to profit consistently, you'd have to repeatedly bet in a game where almost every single trade is a near-certain loss—negative expected value, the longer you play the more you lose. A 9.3% profit probability is about a 1/11 win rate; if you fully rotate monthly and average −18.7% a year, 100,000 yuan principal is about 41,000 yuan after 5 years (100k × (0.813)^5, this book's estimate). Ultra-short-term isn't "flexible," it's repeatedly betting principal at negative expectation.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Shi Donghui, Zhang Xiaoyan, et al. (2020), as above; Securities Association of China investor-education material. <https://investor.amac.org.cn/community/tzzhdzthd/ylj/202304/P020230410489976164138.pdf>
- Note: The 9.3% profit probability is SSE ultra-short-term grouped statistics; the real-rate conversion example is this book's estimate via "real ≈ nominal − inflation." Grade A: same-source account-level data.

### 7. Fund return gap: equity-fund investors take 2%–3.6% less each year
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The subscribe-high, redeem-low rhythm, and the impulse to "chase hot sectors."
- In plain terms: Same fund, because of your timing and chasing rallies and selling dips, you earn 2–3.6 points less a year than the fund itself. This isn't the market's problem—it's your trading rhythm.
- Payoff: Morningstar (China)'s 2025 "China Public Fund Investor Return Gap Research Report" shows that the five-year annualized return gap through end-2024 was entirely negative: aggressive allocation funds −2.17%, active non-sector equity −2.65%, sector funds −3.59%, and fixed-income also −0.62%. Put −2.65% into compounding: 100,000 yuan at a 10% gross annualized for 20 years should be about 673,000 yuan, but if the return gap eats 2.65 points a year (net 7.35%), final value is about 420,000 yuan, a gap over 250,000 yuan (this book estimates via FV = PV(1+r)^n). The fund makes money; the fund investor doesn't—the gap is in the subscription/redemption behavior. The sector fund's −3.59% gap is especially glaring: invest 100k at 12% gross, the 20-year should be about 965,000 yuan, but after the return gap eats 3.59 points (net 8.41%), final value is about 499,000 yuan, a gap near 470,000 yuan (this book's estimate). This is the typical cost of "bought the right fund but sold at the wrong time."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Morningstar (China) (2025), "China Public Fund Investor Return Gap Research Report" (five-year annualized return gap through 2024-12-31); re-quoted in Sina Finance (2025-05-22), "Morningstar China 'Advises' Fund Investors: Avoid Short-Term Timing and Emotional Decisions." <https://finance.sina.com.cn/wm/2025-05-22/doc-inexmwyy6922130.shtml>; SEC (no year), Investor Bulletin: How Fees and Expenses Affect Your Investment Portfolio (fees and behavior jointly erode final value). <https://www.sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-bulletins/ib_fees_expenses.html>
- Note: −2.17% / −2.65% / −3.59% are Morningstar's disclosed specific values; the 250,000 yuan gap is this book's estimate at a 10% gross-return assumption. Grade A: from Morningstar's publicly released research, transparent method.

### 8. The longer you hold, the less you lose: <6 months >50% loss probability, >3 years <20%
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The patience to hold, and the anxiety of "sell early, rest easy."
- In plain terms: The secret to funds making money is one word: wait. Sadly most people can't hold—most holders hold under a year, deleting the "wait" step themselves.
- Payoff: Institution and platform data broadly show holding a fund under 6 months has a >50% loss probability, dropping to <20% after holding over 3 years; yet about 2/3 of fund holders still hold under 1 year. The DCA final-value formula quantifies the power of "starting early": monthly DCA of 1,000 yuan at 7% annualized for 30 years, final value ≈ 1,000 × [(1.07)^30 − 1] / (0.07/12) ≈ 1.227 million yuan (this book estimates via the annuity final-value formula, including 360k principal and 867k interest). Reverse it: start the same DCA 10 years late and final value is only about 568,000 yuan—the delayed "opportunity cost" is 659,000 yuan. Holding and starting early are two sides of the same coin. One more: a lump-sum 100k at 7% for 30 years is about 761k; if you repeatedly churn in and out because you can't hold, the return gap (Entry 7) will cut that number substantially. Starting early and holding long both save the same kind of "certainty."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Securities Association of China / various platform holder-behavior data; Morningstar (China) (2025), "China Public Fund Investor Return Gap Research Report" (long holding reduces the return gap). <https://finance.sina.com.cn/wm/2025-05-22/doc-inexmwyy6922130.shtml>; Investopedia (no year), Compound Interest (annuity / DCA final-value formula). <https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/compoundinterest.asp>
- Note: The specific thresholds of holding period and loss probability come from industry behavior statistics, and platform calibers differ slightly; the DCA final value and delayed opportunity cost are this book's formula estimates (assuming 7% annualized). Grade A: direction and magnitude agree, thresholds per the original disclosure.

### 9. Gold since 1900: real annualized about 0.7%—it's insurance, not an investment
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The fantasy of gold as an "inflation-beating fortune tool," and the equity returns missed by overweighting gold.
- In plain terms: Over more than a hundred years, gold barely rose after inflation. Its value is "it rises when others panic"—allocate 5%–10% as insurance, but overweighting it as a fortune-maker is a misunderstanding.
- Payoff: Credit Suisse's Global Investment Returns Yearbook (Dimson, Marsh, Staunton), covering 35 markets from 1900–2022, shows gold's real annualized about 0.76%—1 dollar of gold in 1900 had only about 2.5 dollars of real purchasing power by 2022, while U.S. stocks became about 2,024 dollars over the same span. Via the real-rate lens: real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation; when nominal gold annualizes about 3% and long-run inflation about 2.3%, the real is only about 0.7%. The Rule of 72 in reverse: real purchasing power doubles in about 72÷0.7 ≈ 103 years. Gold's role is crisis hedge, not wealth engine. Versus stocks: 1900–2022 global stocks real annualized about 5%, gold only 0.76%—the same 1 dollar becomes 2,024× for stocks, only 2.5× for gold. If you put 10% of assets in gold instead of stocks, the amount underperforming over a century is astonishing (this book estimates by real-annualized difference). Keep the insurance attribute, drop the fortune fantasy.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Dimson, E., Marsh, P. & Staunton, M. (2023), Credit Suisse Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2023 (Chapter 8 Commodities and Inflation: gold real annualized 0.76% since 1900). <https://www.london.edu/news/commodities-offer-hedge-against-inflation-2153>; Credit Suisse Research Institute (2023), Global Investment Returns Yearbook 2023 Summary Edition. <https://www.ryt9.com/en/prg/266031>
- Note: The original "1800" caliber was imprecise; Yearbook data start from 1900, so corrected to "since 1900." 0.76% is the USD real return; other currencies / periods differ slightly. Grade B: authoritative long-run data, but gold is highly volatile short-term and its correlation with the portfolio changes over time—don't extrapolate mechanically.

### 10. Active funds: over ~90% fail to beat the index in 15 years
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The expectation of paying a high-priced (1%+) manager to beat the market.
- In plain terms: You pay a management fee to have someone beat the market, and over fifteen years more than nine in ten didn't. This isn't an exception—it's a statistical law.
- Payoff: S&P SPIVA's latest data (through end-2025): U.S. large-cap active funds trailing the S&P 500—78.8% in 1 year, 89% in 5 years, 85.6% in 10 years, about 90% in 15 years, 92.9% in 20 years; all-market caliber about 90.5% trailing in 15 years. This means holding active funds long-term, you're ten-to-one using 1%+ in fees to buy a "likely-to-trail" outcome. Fee erosion (see Entry 1) is a main cause: active fund annual fees are often 0.6–1.5 points higher than index funds, and Sharpe (1991) already proved "active management overall is only the market average even before fees." Convert the 90% trailing into money: 1 million yuan in active funds for 20 years, if active net-of-fee annualized is 1.5 points below the index (8% vs 9.5%), final value about 4.66 million vs 6.15 million, a 1.49 million gap (this book estimates via compounding). Picking low-cost index is voting with your feet against this probability disadvantage.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices (2026), SPIVA U.S. Scorecard (Year-End 2025, percent trailing benchmark by horizon). <https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/tc/research-insights/spiva/>; Sharpe, W. (1991), The Arithmetic of Active Management, Financial Analysts Journal, 47(1), 7–9
- Note: 90% is the SPIVA 15-year all-market caliber approximation; some fund categories (e.g., large-cap value) trail slightly less. Grade A: SPIVA is the most widely cited active/passive comparison study, and corrects for survivorship bias.

### 11. Asset allocation explains about 90% of return variation
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Spending lots of time picking individual stocks and timing, yet not doing the homework on "how much stocks, how much bonds"—the real big knob.
- In plain terms: Instead of agonizing over which stock to buy, decide "how much stocks, how much bonds" first. What decides whether you sleep at night is the big-class proportion, not those few stocks.
- Payoff: Brinson, Hood & Beebower (1986), studying 91 large U.S. pension funds (1974–1983) quarterly data, found asset allocation (policy weights) on average explained 93.6% of portfolio return variance; the 1991 updated sample (1977–1987) was 91.5%. Often relayed as "asset allocation determines about 90% of return performance." Convert to opportunity cost: spending a few hours a week picking stocks is worse than spending the same energy getting the stock-bond ratio right and then rebalancing with discipline—the former's marginal contribution is limited, the latter directly decides volatility and drawdown. Ibbotson & Kaplan (2000) further noted allocation explained about 40% of cross-fund return-level differences and about 90% of time-series volatility. Feel it in numbers: a 60% stock / 40% bond portfolio's long-run volatility mainly comes from the ratio of these two, not "which bank stock you bought." Shifting the 10 hours a month of staring at individual stocks to rebalancing and position calibration is a no-cost opportunity gain.
- Evidence grade: B
- Sources: Brinson, G., Hood, L. & Beebower, G. (1986), Determinants of Portfolio Performance, Financial Analysts Journal, 42(4), 39–44; re-quoted in China Securities Journal (2016-02), "CICC: The Era of Large-Class Asset Allocation Is Coming" (listing 93.6% / 91.5% values). <https://news.cnstock.com/news,zxk-201602-3716825.htm>; Ibbotson, R. & Kaplan, P. (2000), Does Asset Allocation Policy Explain 40, 90, or 100 Percent of Performance?, Financial Analysts Journal, 56(1)
- Note: The classic conclusion measures "return variance / volatility," not "return level," often misread as "allocation determines 90% of return rate," so be cautious; the sample is U.S. pension funds. Grade B: solid academic basis, but the wording is easily inflated by marketing, caliber noted.

### 12. Break-issue rate peak 28% (2022): the dividend disappears
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Treating "IPO subscription always profits" as permanent common sense, and the opportunity cost of funds tied up long-term just for subscription.
- In plain terms: There's no permanent arbitrage. Before 2021 blind subscription made money; in 2022 you lost over a fifth. Treating a "free lunch" as a permanent benefit will eventually backfire.
- Payoff: After the registration-based inquiry reform, A-share break-issue rates swung violently: in 2022 the new-share break-issue rate exceeded 28% (some months over 80%), 2023 about 16.6%–21.4%, 2024 of about 100 listed only 1 broke (about 1%–2%), and through June 2025 all 45 new shares rose on the first day, break-issue rate 0. In opportunity-cost terms: in 2022 if you blindly subscribed and held to the first day, your account could directly show a paper loss over 20%; while the low break-issue in 2024–2025 is more a result of "issue P/E back to around 22× + tightened supply," not an institutional guarantee. The dividend can reverse any time as supply ramps or pricing rebounds. Run the numbers: in 2022 if you subscribed every new share and held to the first day, assuming negative average winning returns and 28% break-issue, principal drawdown is considerable; while the "mindless profit" of the 2024–2025 low-break period reverses immediately once supply ramps. The essence of arbitrage dividends is a phased pricing dislocation, not a permanent right.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: NetEase (2025-06), "Zero Break-Issues, Back to the Blind-Subscription Era" (2022 break-issue rate >28%, 2024 only 1, 2025 zero break-issue). <https://m.163.com/dy/article/K1KJDE1P0530MI2R.html>; Baidu Baike (no year), Break-issue rate (2010–2025 A-share first-day break-issue rate statistics). <https://baike.baidu.com/item/%E7%A0%B4%E5%8F%91%E7%8E%87/69139871>
- Note: The break-issue-rate caliber is "first-day break"; different statistics (first-day / cumulative) differ; the 2025 zero-break is a phased phenomenon, not a steady state. Grade A: data from public statistics, but describes a market phenomenon, not a regulatory conclusion.

### 13. Leverage: a 50% drop needs +100% to recover; leverage can go to zero
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self+family -->
- Cost: The sense of safety "you can recover after a drop" without leverage, and the interest paid to add leverage.
- In plain terms: Leverage doesn't change your win rate, it just turns "volatility" into "death." It turns a small pullback into a wipeout—the button ordinary investors should permanently blacklist.
- Payoff: The compound formula FV = PV(1+r)^n is just as cold on the loss side: without leverage, a 50% principal drop needs +100% to recover (because 0.5 × 2 = 1); with 2× leverage, a 25% drop in the underlying zeroes your principal (1 − 2 × 25% = 0), with no chance to recover. More hidden is the interest cost: per PBOC Announcement [2021] No. 3, loan annualized rate must be disclosed via the internal rate of return (IRR) method including all costs—margin financing annual rates often run 6%–8%, and by the Rule of 72, interest alone eats about half your principal in roughly 9–12 years (72÷7≈10.3). The SEC warns: a margin account "may lose more than the principal," and the broker may liquidate without notice. Example: at 2× leverage, a 25% underlying drop zeroes principal; at 1.5× leverage a ~33% drop zeroes it. Adding 7% financing interest (IRR annualized, per PBOC announcement caliber), the real annual cost far exceeds 7%—for a family, forced liquidation can upgrade "investment drawdown" to "household balance-sheet breach."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: People's Bank of China (2021), PBOC Announcement No. 3 of 2021 (Article 4: annualized rate may use the compounding i.e. internal rate of return method, including all loan costs). <https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2021-03/31/content_5597156.htm>; SEC (no year), Investor Bulletin: Leveraged Investing Strategies – Know the Risks (margin may lose more than principal, may be force-liquidated). <https://sec.gov/oiea/investor-alerts-bulletins/ib_leveragedinvesting>
- Note: A 50% drop needing +100% to recover is pure math; leverage forced-liquidation and interest are mechanistic risks, and specific rates and maintenance-margin ratios vary by broker. Grade A: mathematical identity + regulatory warning, high certainty.

### 14. Timing drag about 3–4 percentage points a year
<!-- tags: money=little time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: Attempts to sell high and buy low, dodge the crash and catch the bottom, and the trading taxes paid for it.
- In plain terms: You think you're "dodging the crash and catching the bottom," but the data says you thereby earn 3–4 points less a year. The cost of timing is certain and expensive.
- Payoff: DALBAR's Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB) tracks the gap between investors' actual returns and benchmarks: in 2024 the ordinary equity fund investor returned 16.54% while the S&P 500 returned 25.02%, a gap of 8.48 percentage points (second-largest in a decade); since 1985, the average stock investor has trailed the index for 15 consecutive years. Put the 3–4 point annual drag into compounding: 100,000 yuan at 10% for 20 years should be about 673,000 yuan, but if timing drags net to 6.5%, final value is only about 357,000 yuan, a gap over 310,000 yuan (this book estimates via FV = PV(1+r)^n). Timing loses not just on direction but on "missing the best few days"—Wells Fargo calculated that dropping the largest-up days makes long-run returns cliff-dive. At the single-year 8.48-point gap (2024) annualized: ordinary investor 16.54% vs index 25.02%, 848 bp; if you earn 4 points less for 30 years, 1 million becomes 3.24 million instead of 8.67 million (this book estimates via compounding). Timing drag is a triple tax: "invisible, certain, and compounding-amplified."
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: DALBAR (2025), Quantitative Analysis of Investor Behavior (QAIB) 2025 (2024 ordinary equity investor 16.54% vs S&P 500 25.02%, gap 848 bp); press release. <https://www.dalbar.com/press-release/investors-missed-the-best-of-2024s-market-gains-latest-dalbar-investor-behavior-report-finds/>; Wells Fargo Advisors (no year), The perils of trying to time volatile markets (effect of dropping best trading days on long-run returns). <https://www.wellsfargoadvisors.com/research-analysis/reports/policy/volatile-markets.htm>
- Note: 3–4 points is an approximation of QAIB's long-run average magnitude; the 310,000 yuan gap is this book's estimate at 10% vs 6.5%. Grade A: DALBAR has tracked continuously since 1985, method public, but the sample is the U.S. market.

### 15. Retail contributes 80% of trading volume, under 10% of profits
<!-- tags: money=0 time=little will=some payoff=mid scope=money impact=self -->
- Cost: The illusion of treating "frequent operating" as a sense of participation, and the time and energy consumed for it.
- In plain terms: Retail does 80% of the work but earns under 10% of the money. This isn't a joke—it's black and white in the yearbook. The conclusion is clear: do less work, earn more money.
- Payoff: The SSE Statistical Yearbook (2018 Volume) shows that in 2017 individual investors contributed 82.01% of trading volume but only 9.00% of total profits (institutions 32.30%, general legal persons 55.70%); individuals' total profit was 310.8 billion yuan, institutions 1,115.6 billion yuan—institutions earned 3.6× retail. Do the "input-output ratio" clearly: retail use 5.9× the trading volume to get back under 30% of the profit share—very low output per unit of operation. Cutting ineffective trades saves both taxes and fees (see Entry 5) and avoids timing losses (see Entry 14)—the highest cost-performance "move." Output ratio: retail use 82% of volume for 9% of profit, institutions use 14.76% of volume for 32.3% of profit—retail's per-trade output is about 1/20 of institutions' (this book estimates via yearbook ratios). Trading less isn't "lying flat," it's pulling yourself out of a negative-expectation game.
- Evidence grade: A
- Sources: Shanghai Stock Exchange (2018), SSE Statistical Yearbook (2018 Volume) (2017 investor trading volume and profit structure); re-quoted in 21st Century Business Herald (2018-08-30), "SSE Statistical Yearbook: Small Retail Contribute 80% of Volume, but Only 10% of Profit." <https://m.21jingji.com/article/20180830/herald/3a5530ea4e34c0c8201843a2464326bc.html>; Yangcheng Evening News (2021-01-20), "A-Shares Accelerate from Retail Market to Institutional Market" (citing yearbook 82.01% volume). <https://ep.ycwb.com/epaper/ywdf/html/2021-01/20/content_695_352263.htm>
- Note: The 82.01% volume and 9.00% profit are 2017 Shanghai-market data; the Shenzhen market and recent structure have changed (institution share rising); the "retail low output" direction is robust. Grade A: from the exchange yearbook, authoritative caliber.
